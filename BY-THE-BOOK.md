# By the Book — how the questions are written

The guide for writing the **By the Book** division of every maths chapter,
Classes 6 to 10. Read [DESIGN-MATHS.md §6a](DESIGN-MATHS.md) for where the
division sits in a chapter; this file is about what goes in it.

It was written on 25 September 2026 from a reading of what CBSE actually sets
for Class 10 Mathematics:

* 16 sample question papers and their marking schemes, 2020-21 to 2026-27,
  Standard and Basic, including both 2021-22 terms;
* 36 distinct board-examination sets, 2023 to 2026 (Standard series 30/x and
  Basic series 430/x);
* CBSE's Additional Practice Questions, 2021-22 to 2023-24, with their
  marking schemes.

The papers and the notes taken from them are not in the repo. Where this
guide says "the board does X", that is what the papers showed, not a guess.

---

## 1. What By the Book is for

**By the Book prepares a student for the CBSE Class 10 board paper, starting
in Class 6.** Every chapter, from the first chapter of Class 6, ends with
questions in the six forms that paper uses, framed the way the paper frames
them. By the time a student sits the board examination they have answered
roughly two thousand questions in its forms, and none of its forms is new.

Three things follow from that.

1. **It aims at Mathematics Standard (041), not Basic.** A student ready for
   Standard is ready for Basic; the reverse is not true. There is no easier
   tier and no marking of questions as harder.
2. **The mathematics is the chapter's; the form is the board's.** A Class 6
   long answer is Class 6 mathematics, set out as a board long answer. We
   never borrow content from a later class to make a question look like a
   Class 10 one.
3. **Classes 1 to 9 are examined by the school.** Their teachers set their own
   papers, and in practice lift questions from the book in front of them. So
   By the Book is also the school's question bank: every question must be
   usable as it stands in a class test, with its marks printed and its answer
   in the key.

### How it differs from the rest of the chapter

| | NCERT exercises (in the chapter) | By the Book | Beyond the Book |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | practise the idea just taught | answer in the form the school and board examine | stretch toward olympiad and entrance problems |
| Order | follows the teaching | by form, fixed (§2) | by competitive format |
| Tone | exploratory, "try", "can you" | examination: exact, complete, marked | puzzle-like, one clever step |
| Marks | none | printed on every form | none |
| Content | the section's | the whole chapter's, mixed | the chapter's, taken further |

**What it should feel like to the reader:** a well-set school paper. Plain,
exact, calm. Every question says precisely what to find and how much it is
worth. Nothing is chatty, nothing coaches, nothing is decorated. A student
turning to it should know at once that this is what a test looks like.

## 2. The shape of the division

The same in every chapter of every class. Decided with the user on
25 September 2026.

| # | Form | Questions | Marks each | Sub-head |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Very short answer | 10 | 2 | Very short answer |
| 2 | Short answer | 10 | 3 | Short answer |
| 3 | Long answer | 10 | 5 | Long answer |
| 4 | Assertion and reason | 5 | 1 | Assertion and reason |
| 5 | Case-based | 5 | 4 | Case-based questions |
| 6 | Objective (multiple choice) | 10 | 1 | Objective questions |
| | **Total** | **50** | **200** | |

* **The order is the user's, not the board's.** The board paper opens with
  its objective section; By the Book follows the order of the NCERT exercise
  style — written answers first — and ends with the objective questions.
  Do not reorder it to match the paper.
* **One numbered run, 1 to 50**, under the sub-heads above.
* **No note lines under the sub-heads** (user, 25 September 2026). "2 marks each. Show the working …" was set under every form and removed as a tagline. The only line under a sub-head is the assertion-and-reason key, which a student needs to answer. Marks per form are in the table above and in the key.
* **No internal choice.** The board's "OR" exists to give an examination
  candidate a choice; in a practice set both halves would simply be two
  questions. Write two questions instead.
* **No answers under the questions.** The key is in the Answers stage at the
  end of Beyond the Book (§7).

## 3. The six forms

Each form below gives: what the board does, how we write it, the wording to
use, and a Class 6 and a Class 10 example so the scale is visible.

### 3.1 Very short answer — 2 marks

**What the board does.** Five per paper. A short calculation or a short
reasoned statement, answerable in two to four lines. Bare mathematics far
more often than a story — at most one real setting in the five. Two marks
means two steps, marked 1 + 1 or ½ + 1 + ½.

**How we write it.**

* One demand, reached in **two steps**. If it can be answered from memory in
  one word, it is an objective question, not a VSA.
* Open with the command: *Find*, *Evaluate*, *Show that*, *Prove that*,
  *Verify that*, *Write … and give a reason*.
* The yes/no question with a reason is a VSA staple: *Is 100 a triangular
  number? Give a reason.* / *Can 6ⁿ end with the digit 0? Justify your answer.*
* About one in four works backwards: the result is given, a number is
  asked for. *The sum of the first n odd numbers is 169. Find n.*


| Class 6 | Class 10 |
|---|---|
| Find the 12th number of the sequence 4, 7, 10, 13, … Show how you found it. | Find the zeroes of p(x) = x² − 3 and verify the relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients. |
| Is 36 both a square number and a triangular number? Give a reason. | Two numbers are in the ratio 2 : 3 and their LCM is 180. Find their HCF. |

### 3.2 Short answer — 3 marks

**What the board does.** Six per paper. About a third are proofs; the rest
are multi-step applications, often with two things asked in one stem, often
ending in *find the value of k*. Word problems appear here with a named
person. From 2026-27 one short answer may show a student's working and ask
where it goes wrong.

**How we write it.**

* **Three marked steps.** Set up, work, conclude — each worth about a mark.
* **Ask for two things in one stem** where it is natural: *Find … Also
  find …* / *Do these form a sequence with a rule? If so, find the 10th
  number.*
* **One per chapter is a short proof or a justification**: *Show that …*,
  *Explain why … is always …*. In Classes 6 and 7 this is reasoning in
  words and pictures (*Explain, using a picture of dots, why the sum of two
  odd numbers is even*); in 9 and 10 it is a proof.
* **One per chapter is a find-the-mistake question** (§3.7), from Class 7.
* Word problems: one named person, one or two plain sentences, rupees and
  real units, one closing question.


| Class 6 | Class 10 |
|---|---|
| Arjun arranges chairs in rows: 1 in the first row, 3 in the second, 5 in the third, and so on. How many chairs are in the 10th row? How many chairs are there in all in the first 10 rows? | Given that √3 is irrational, prove that 5 + 2√3 is irrational. |
| Explain, using a picture of dots, why adding a triangular number to the next one gives a square number. | Rehana withdraws ₹2,000 in ₹50 and ₹100 notes, 25 notes in all. How many notes of each kind does she get? |

### 3.3 Long answer — 5 marks

**What the board does.** Four per paper, and they come from four fixed
kinds, nearly every year:

1. a **word problem** that becomes an equation (trains, boats, pipes, ages,
   money);
2. a **theorem proved, then used** — *Prove that … Using the above, …*;
3. **combined figures or solids**, usually with a cost;
4. **heights and distances** or **statistics with a missing value**.

A long answer is **one substantial problem**, not a list of small ones. From
2026 the board sometimes splits one into labelled parts (i)–(iv) that build
on each other, and every long answer asks for two outputs — *Find … Also
find …*. The marking scheme gives about one mark per step: set up, form the
equation or draw the figure, solve, reject what is impossible with a reason,
state the answer in the words of the question.

**How we write it.**

* **One problem that takes five marked steps.** If the parts could be
  answered separately, in any order, it is four short answers, not a long
  one. Parts are allowed only when each uses the one before (*(i) … (ii)
  Hence …*).
* **Use the board's four kinds, scaled to the chapter.** A Class 6 chapter
  with no equations still has a word problem that has to be modelled, a
  result that is shown and then used, a figure to work from, and a table to
  read.
* **End with a stated answer** in the words of the question, with units.
* **Draw the figure** when the problem needs one, or ask the student to
  (*Draw a figure to show …*): the board gives a mark for it.

follow it.*

| Class 6 | Class 10 |
|---|---|
| A shopkeeper stacks tins in rows: 1 in the top row, 2 in the next, and so on. She has 60 tins. How many full rows can she make, how many tins are left over, and how many more tins does she need to finish the next row? Show your working. | From the top of a 75 m high lighthouse, the angles of depression of two ships on the same side are 30° and 60°. Find the distance between the ships. (Use √3 = 1.73) |
| Show that the number of lines in a complete graph with 5 points is 10. Use the same reasoning to find the number of lines when there are 12 points. | Prove that if a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle, it divides the other two sides in the same ratio. Using this, prove that … |

**What this replaces.** Class 6 Chapter 1 was first written with long
answers as four-part investigations — *(a) write the first ten … (b) which
are even … (c) … (d) …*. Those are good exercises and wrong long answers:
the parts do not depend on each other and none asks for a modelled,
finished solution. They are to be rewritten to this section.

### 3.4 Assertion and reason — 1 mark

**What the board does.** Two per paper. The Assertion is a **particular
claim with numbers in it**; the Reason is **the general rule** behind it.
The four codes are printed once, in fixed words. About half the pairs have
one false statement, and the false one is built from a common mistake: a
misremembered formula, an over-general rule, outcomes that are not equally
likely.

**How we write it.**

* A = a specific numerical claim. R = a rule, a definition or a general
  fact.
* Spread the answers over all four codes in each chapter. Of five, aim for
  one or two (a), one (b), one (c), one (d).
* Make the false statements tempting: the mistake a student in that class
  actually makes (*every odd number is prime*; *a number with more factors
  is always bigger*; *the product of two irrational numbers is irrational*).
* Code (b) — both true, R does not explain A — is the hardest to write and
  the one most worth practising: R must be true and relevant-sounding.

**The key line under the sub-head (the board's wording, our lower-case letters):** *Choose (a) if A and R are both true and R is the correct explanation of A;
(b) if A and R are both true but R is not the correct explanation of A;
(c) if A is true but R is false; (d) if A is false but R is true.*

| Class 6 | Class 10 |
|---|---|
| A: 36 dots can be arranged as a square. R: 36 is the sum of the first 6 odd numbers. → (a) | A: The number 5ⁿ cannot end with the digit 0. R: A number ending in 0 has both 2 and 5 as prime factors. → (a) |
| A: 15 is a triangular number. R: 15 is an odd number. → (b) | A: When two coins are tossed, P(two heads) = 1/3. R: Equally likely outcomes have equal probability. → (d) |

### 3.5 Case-based — 4 marks

**What the board does.** Three per paper. A passage of 50 to 100 words — a
general opening sentence, then a named person or place and the data, often
with a figure, table or map — then the fixed line *Based on the above
information, answer the following questions.* Three parts:

* **(i) 1 mark** — read something off, or write the model (*Write the first
  four terms*, *Form the equation*);
* **(ii) 1 mark** — one computation;
* **(iii) 2 marks** — a two-step or reverse question, often a *what if*
  (*If she had only 50 tins …*), or *Is it possible …? Justify your answer.*

Each part can be answered without the one before. From 2026 the passages
use Indian settings with a name — Kolam, Gilli Danda, the Chenab bridge,
Jaipur on a grid, AQI readings — and open with *Observe the figure*.

**How we write it.**

* 50–100 words in Classes 9–10; 40–70 in Classes 6–8. Never longer.
* A real Indian setting the student could recognise, with the data given
  in the passage or in a small table or figure.
* Exactly three parts, (i) 1, (ii) 1, (iii) 2, in rising difficulty, set
  with the house `.c-parts` (which counts in roman numerals, as the board
  does).
* No OR in part (iii): write the harder of the two.

marks.*

### 3.6 Objective questions — 1 mark

**What the board does.** Eighteen per paper; four options; one correct.

* Most stems are **one sentence ending where the options begin**: *If …,
  then the value of k is* / *The 20th term of the AP … is*. No question mark
  when the options complete the sentence.
* About 40% are one-step recall or evaluation; about 30% find a parameter
  from a condition; the rest read a figure or graph, test a statement
  (*Which of the following is not true?*), or describe a named student's
  method or claim (*Devansh proved that … Which criterion did he use?*).
* The wrong options are the answers of real mistakes, not random numbers.
* From 2026-27, the multi-statement form: three statements I, II, III, and
  options *I only / II only / I and II / II and III*.

**How we write it.**

* Ten per chapter: about four recall or one-step, three parameter or
  reverse, one figure or table, one statement or multi-statement, one named
  student's method or claim.
* Each wrong option must be the result of a specific mistake you can name.
* Spread the correct letter evenly across (a) to (d) in each chapter.


| Class 6 | Class 10 |
|---|---|
| The 20th odd number is (a) 39 (b) 40 (c) 41 (d) 20 | If one zero of 2x² − 5x + k is 2, then the value of k is (a) −2 (b) 2 (c) 3 (d) −3 |
| Ravi says every square number is also a triangular number, and gives 36 as his reason. Which is true? (a) He is right (b) 36 is not triangular (c) 36 is both, but 4 is not triangular (d) 36 is not square | Which of the following cannot be the probability of an event? (a) 0.7 (b) 2/3 (c) −1.5 (d) 15% |

### 3.7 Find the mistake

Not a form of its own — it lives inside the short answers, one per chapter
from Class 7. The board brought it in in 2026-27: a named student's working
in numbered steps, and the demand *Identify the step in which [name] has
made an error, if any. Correct it and find the right answer.* The marking
gives 1 mark for naming the step and 2 for the corrected working.

* Four or five short numbered steps, set as a `.work` block.
* One real mistake of the kind that class makes: a sign, an order of
  operations, a misapplied rule. Never a typing slip.
* Sometimes there is no mistake — *if any* is in the demand for that
  reason. Use this at most once in three chapters.

## 4. By the Book, class by class

The forms, counts, marks and wording are the same in every class. What
changes is the mathematics inside them and how much a question asks the
student to hold at once.

| | Class 6 | Class 7 | Class 8 | Class 9 | Class 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Numbers** | whole numbers to a few thousand, simple fractions | integers, fractions, decimals | squares, cubes, powers, rational numbers | real numbers, surds | as the board |
| **Steps in a long answer** | 3–4 | 4 | 4–5 | 5 | 5–6 |
| **Letters for unknowns** | only as the chapter introduces them; otherwise "a number" | yes, simple equations | yes | yes | yes |
| **Proof** | *Explain why*, with a picture | *Show that*, in words | short algebraic *Show that* | geometric proofs, *Prove that* | board proofs, theorem + use |
| **Reverse / find the parameter** | 1 in 5 | 1 in 4 | 1 in 4 | 1 in 3 | 1 in 3 |
| **Find the mistake** | — | 1 per chapter | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| **Multi-statement MCQ** | 1 per chapter | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1–2 |
| **Case passage** | 40–60 words | 40–70 | 50–80 | 50–100 | 50–100 |
| **Figures** | drawn, labelled, nothing to infer | drawn | *In the given figure* | *In the given figure*, some inference | as the board |
| **Constants** | — | — | (Take π = 22/7) | (Use √2 = 1.41) | (Use √3 = 1.73) |

### What each class should feel like

**Class 6 — the first test paper.** Short sentences. Numbers a child can
work with on paper. Every figure drawn. The forms are the board's, and
this is the year a student first sees *2 marks each* and learns that it
means two steps. Long answers are the chapter's patterns and shapes turned
into a small problem that has to be set out, not guessed. Proofs are
*explain why*, with a picture.

**Class 7 — writing it down.** The same, with the working expected in full:
the key shows every step. The find-the-mistake question starts here, because
Class 7 is where students begin to check another person's method. Simple
equations appear in word problems.

**Class 8 — the algebra year.** Word problems are modelled with a letter and
solved. *Show that* becomes algebraic. *In the given figure* replaces a
described figure. Case passages carry a small table or diagram.

**Class 9 — the board's rehearsal.** Schools set their Class 9 papers on the
Class 10 pattern, and By the Book should read as a Class 10 paper would for
Class 9 mathematics. Geometric proof arrives in full: given, to prove,
figure, proof, conclusion. Constants are supplied in brackets.

**Class 10 — the board paper.** Indistinguishable in form from the CBSE
Standard paper. Every kind the paper uses appears at least once in the
volume: the boat and train quadratics, the lighthouse, the theorem proved
and used, the median with missing frequencies, the tent with its canvas
cost.

## 5. How a question is worded

These are the board's habits, and they apply in every class.

1. **Command first, target named.** *Find the number of rows.* Not *Can you
   work out how many rows there are?* — By the Book never asks the student a
   question about themselves.
2. **The house verbs:** *Find, Evaluate, Show that, Prove that, Verify that,
   Write, Draw, Explain why, Justify your answer, Give a reason.* Avoid
   *calculate the following*, *work out*, *figure out*, *what do you think*.
3. **Given, then demand.** *If … , then find …*; *Given that …, show that …*.
4. **"In the given figure,"** whenever a figure carries data — and what the
   figure means is also said in words, so that a student who cannot read the
   figure (and the board's visually impaired version) can still answer.
5. **Constants in brackets at the end:** *(Use √3 = 1.73)*, *(Take π =
   22/7)*. Choose numbers that cancel them: radii of 7, 14, 21.
6. **A real setting gets one named person**, an Indian name, rupees and real
   units, and a closing question that is what that person wants to know.
   Vary the names; never the same name twice in a chapter.
7. **Chain with "Hence" and "Also"** where the second demand uses the first.
8. **"Justify your answer"** on any yes/no demand.
9. **Plain English.** The chapter's own words, short sentences, no idiom
   (see the plain-English note in DESIGN-MATHS). An examination question is
   precise, not literary.
10. **No coaching.** No hints, no *Remember that …*, no *Think about …*. The
    marks line is the only guidance.

### Settings the board uses again and again

Use these, in the chapter's mathematics. They are familiar to examiners and
to students, and the board keeps returning to them.

* **Shops and money** — prices, notes, fixed charge plus daily charge,
  savings each month, simple interest, cost of canvas or paint per m².
* **School** — seating, groups and rooms, sports day, a charity run, test
  scores, a school trip.
* **Travel** — trains, a boat in a stream, cars from A and B, aircraft.
* **Heights** — towers, lighthouses and ships, kites and strings, ladders,
  shadows, trees broken in a storm, a drone.
* **Round things** — clock hands, wheels and revolutions, a tethered cow or
  horse, a sprinkler, a pizza.
* **Solids** — tents, tanks, cones of ice cream, capsules, a toy of cone and
  hemisphere, a bird-bath.
* **Chance** — dice, coins, a pack of cards with some removed, numbered
  cards or T-shirts, balls in a bag, a spinner.
* **Maps and grids** — a town on a grid, friends' houses, a garden with
  saplings.
* **India** (the 2026 direction) — kolam, rangoli, Gilli Danda, a railway
  arch, rainfall and AQI data, a monument, a festival.

## 6. Setting it on the page

* Sub-heads, notes and blocks as in §2, one question per `.c-practice`
  block (split-practice does this).
* Options with `.c-parts c-parts--alpha` (`--4` when four short ones fit
  on a line); case-study parts with `.c-parts` (roman, as the board).
* Multi-statement MCQs: the statements as `.c-parts c-parts--1` (roman),
  then the options.
* A case-study passage in `.c-case`, a table inside it if the data needs
  one.
* A find-the-mistake working as a `.work` block with `Step 1` … labels.
* Long answers that need a figure carry it as a `.c-figure` inside the
  block, so the question and its figure never part.

## 7. The answer key

The key is in the Answers stage at the end of Beyond the Book, under the
group *By the Book*. It teaches how to write for marks, so it is written the
way a marking scheme reads.

* **Objective and assertion–reason:** the letters, in a `.c-answers` row.
* **Very short and short answers:** the answer and the one or two lines of
  working that earn the marks.
* **Long answers and case studies:** the steps in order, ending with the
  stated answer in words and units. Reject an impossible value with its
  reason (*the number of rows cannot be negative*).
* **Find the mistake:** name the step, say what is wrong, give the correct
  answer.

## 8. Checklist before a chapter is refit

1. 10 / 10 / 10 / 5 / 5 / 10, in that order, numbered 1–50.
2. No note lines under sub-heads, except the assertion-and-reason key.
3. Every VSA needs two steps; none is one-word recall.
4. Every long answer is one problem with at least four marked steps and a
   stated answer; no independent (a)–(d) investigations.
5. Assertion–reason: answers spread over (a)–(d); at least one false
   statement built from a real mistake.
6. Case studies: 3 parts, 1 + 1 + 2, passage within the word limit for the
   class, a named setting.
7. Objective: correct letters spread; every wrong option a nameable mistake;
   one multi-statement item.
8. From Class 7: one find-the-mistake short answer.
9. One in four or five questions works backwards.
10. No question is copied from the chapter's own exercises.
11. Every answer is in the key.
12. Plain English; no coaching; no lone word on a line (check the proof).
