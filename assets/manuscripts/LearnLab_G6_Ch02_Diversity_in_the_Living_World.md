# 2. Diversity in the Living World

Go outside your classroom. Stand still for two minutes.

The place may look very ordinary. But living things are all around you. Grass is growing through a crack in the path. A crow sits on the wall. Ants move in a line along the edge of a step. One tree has wide leaves. The tree next to it has thin ones.

Nobody planted most of these. Nobody arranged them. And no two of them are exactly the same.

This chapter is about that variety. How much of it is there? How do we make sense of it? And why are the living things in one place so different from the living things in another?

No labels and no children in the scene. The point is that a very ordinary place is full of different living things.

## 2.1 The Variety Around Us

Most of us walk past living things without really seeing them. We see a tree. But we do not see that its leaves are pointed, and the next tree's leaves are round. We hear birds. But we do not hear that three different birds are calling.

Looking carefully is a skill. Like any skill, it gets better with practice.

That is what the first activity is for.

## Living Things Need Each Other

The living things in a place are not just sharing space. They are joined to each other.

A tree gives shade. Small plants that cannot take strong sunlight grow in that shade. Birds build nests in its branches. They eat insects from its bark. A bird eats a fruit, flies away, and drops the seed far from the tree. The seed grows into a new tree there. Old leaves fall and rot into the soil. That soil feeds the next plant.

Now take one part away. The others feel it. If the insects go, the birds have less food. If the tree goes, the shade goes, the nests go, and the falling leaves go too.

## 2.2 Making Sense of the Variety

Your two tables now hold a long, untidy list. Nature really is like that. But it is hard to study anything from an untidy list.

Think of a shop that sells stationery. Suppose everything was thrown into one big heap. Finding one pen would take an hour. Shops do not do this. Pens are kept with pens. Notebooks are kept with notebooks. Geometry boxes are kept together.

Once things are in groups, two things become easy. You can find what you want. And you can see what the shop has.

Scientists do the same with living things. Putting things into groups by the features they share is called **grouping**.

### Activity 2.3 — Sort the Pile

**You will need:** old magazines or newspapers, scissors, glue, small cards.

**What to do:**

2. Work in groups of five or six. Put all your cards together.
3. Sort the pile into groups. You decide what the groups will be.
4. Write down, in one line, the rule you used.
5. Now mix all the cards again. Sort them a second time, using a completely different rule.

**Observe:** Compare both your sortings with another group's.

**Think:** Did any two groups in the class use the same rule?

## There Is No One Correct Sorting

When the class compares answers, something surprising happens. Almost every group sorted the same cards differently. And none of them were wrong.

One group may have put plants on one side and animals on the other. Another may have used size. Another may have used where the living thing lives, or what it eats, or whether it has flowers.

> **Illustration:** Four rounded label boxes in a row, each a different pale colour, containing the text: "Has flowers / no flowers", "Soft stem / hard stem", "What it eats", "Where it lives". Below them, a caption line: "Some ways of grouping."

This is important to understand. It is easy to think that living things come with group names already fixed on them. They do not. We make the groups. And we make them to suit the question we are asking.

A gardener may sort plants by height. That helps a gardening question. A doctor may sort plants by which ones are used as medicine. That helps a different question. Both sortings are useful. Neither one is the "true" grouping of plants.

Scientists look for a grouping that is useful again and again. The best kind is this: when you know that something belongs to a group, you can say several other things about it. You will meet a grouping like that later in this chapter. Watch for it.

**Think:** Your school library keeps books in a certain order. What is easy to find because of that order? What is hard to find?

## Herbs, Shrubs and Trees

Read down your table. Three groups appear on their own.

Some plants are small. They have soft green stems that bend easily. These are called **herbs**. A tomato plant is a herb. So are mint and coriander, and most small plants in an open field.

Some plants are taller. They have several hard brown stems. The branches start close to the ground. These stems are not as thick or as tall as a tree's. Such plants are called **shrubs**. A rose plant is a shrub.

Some plants grow very tall. They have one thick, hard, brown stem. The branches start well above the ground. These are **trees**. A mango is a tree.

Now think about what the stem has to do. A tomato plant holds up a few leaves and some fruit. A mango tree holds up a heavy top of branches, ten metres above the ground, for many years, through wind and rain. A soft green stem could never do that job.

**Can you explain why?** A banana plant is taller than you and its stem is thick. But the stem is soft and green. Which group does it belong to?

## Plants That Cannot Stand Up

Not every plant can hold itself up.

Some plants have stems that are too weak to stand. So they climb up something else — a wall, a fence, a stick, or another plant. These are called **climbers**. A money plant is a climber. So is a grape vine.

Other plants also have weak stems. But instead of climbing, they spread flat along the ground. These are called **creepers**. Pumpkin and watermelon plants are creepers.

Both kinds have the same problem. A weak stem cannot lift leaves up to the sunlight. A climber solves this by using another plant's height. A creeper solves it in a different way. It gives up on height and spreads sideways instead.

> **Illustration:** Two plants shown side by side. Left: a climber with a thin stem winding around a vertical bamboo stick, with small curling tendrils gripping the stick — include a small inset circle enlarging one tendril. Right: a creeper spreading flat across the ground with large leaves and a fruit resting on the soil. Label the two "Climber" and "Creeper".

**Think:** A climber and a creeper both have weak stems. Which one do you think gets more sunlight? Why?

So far you have grouped plants using only the stem. Leaves give us another way to sort the same plants. That way turns out to be more useful.

## Lines on a Leaf

Hold a leaf up against the light. You will see thin lines running through it. These lines are **veins**. The pattern the veins make is called **venation**.

Veins are not just for decoration. They are pipes. They carry water into the leaf, and carry food away from it. They also make the leaf stiff, so it holds its shape instead of hanging loose.

### Activity 2.5 — Take a Leaf Print

**What to do:**

1. Put a leaf on the desk with its back side facing up. The veins stand out more on that side.
2. Place a sheet of thin paper over the leaf.
3. Hold the paper still. Rub the side of the pencil lead flat across it, using light strokes.
4. The pattern of veins will appear on the paper.
5. Do this for every leaf. Write the plant's name next to each print.

**Observe:** Put all your prints side by side.

## Two Patterns

However many leaves you print, the patterns almost always fall into two kinds.

In some leaves, one thick vein runs down the middle. Thinner veins branch off it on both sides. Those branch again, and again, until the whole leaf is covered with a net. This pattern is called **reticulate venation**. You can see it clearly in a hibiscus leaf. Mango, neem and peepal leaves have it too.

In other leaves there is no thick middle vein and no net. Instead, many veins of the same thickness run side by side. They go from the base of the leaf to its tip, staying parallel all the way. This pattern is called **parallel venation**. A banana leaf has it. So does every blade of grass.

> **Illustration:** Three leaves drawn large and flat, with veins clearly picked out in a darker tone: (a) a hibiscus leaf showing one thick middle vein with a branching net of finer veins on both sides, (b) a banana leaf showing many parallel veins running from base to tip, (c) a single blade of grass showing the same parallel pattern at a smaller scale. Label the patterns "Reticulate venation" and "Parallel venation" and caption each leaf with its plant.

Once you know these two names, you can sort almost any leaf you pick up.

**What do you predict?** Maize and wheat both have long thin leaves, like grass. What venation would you expect in them?

## Under the Ground

Roots do two jobs. They hold the plant firmly in place against wind and rain. And they pull water and minerals out of the soil.

Roots are buried, so most people never see them. But roots differ from plant to plant, just as leaves do. And you can watch them grow yourself.

### Activity 2.6 — Grow Your Own Roots

**You will need:** a handful of chana (chickpea) seeds, a handful of wheat or maize seeds, two shallow trays or plates, cotton cloth or cotton wool, water.

**What to do:**

1. Soak both kinds of seed in water overnight.
2. Spread a wet cloth in each tray. Put the chana seeds in one tray. Put the wheat seeds in the other.
3. Keep the cloth wet for the next five or six days. Wet, not flooded. Check it every morning.
4. From day three, take out one seed from each tray every day. Look at what has grown from it.
5. On day six, wash the roots gently. Lay them on paper, side by side.

**Observe:** Draw what grew from each kind of seed. Draw both at the same size.

**Think:** Which of your two root systems would be easier to pull out of soil? What would that mean for the plant?

> **Layout:** Two bordered drawing boxes, each about 55 × 70 mm, labelled "Chana" and "Wheat".

***

**[ PAGE 14 ]**

## Two Kinds of Root

Your two trays will have grown two very different things.

From the chana seed, one thick root will have gone straight down. Much thinner roots will have branched off it. That main root is called a **taproot**. Mustard, hibiscus, mango and radish all have taproots. In fact, a radish *is* a taproot. It is swollen with stored food. That is why we eat it.

From the wheat seed, there will be no single main root. Instead, a bunch of thin roots of about the same thickness will have come out together and spread outward. These are called **fibrous roots**. Grass, maize and lemongrass have them.

> **Illustration:** Two young plants shown side by side, each with its roots fully visible below a cut-away soil line: (a) a chana seedling with one thick central root going straight down and thinner side roots branching from it, (b) a wheat seedling with a spreading bunch of thin roots of similar thickness. Label "Taproot" with an arrow to the thick central root, and "Fibrous roots" with an arrow to the bunch. Draw both at the same scale.

The two shapes do two different jobs.

A taproot goes deep. It reaches water that lies far below the surface. This is why a deep-rooted tree can stay green through a dry month.

Fibrous roots stay near the top and spread wide. They hold a large area of loose soil, and they catch light rain before it sinks away. This is why grass grows so well on a bank of loose earth.

## The Two Go Together

Compare answers across the whole class. The pattern is easy to see.

Plants with reticulate venation almost always have a taproot.
Plants with parallel venation almost always have fibrous roots.

*Sadabahar* has net-like veins and a taproot. Chana has net-like veins and a taproot. Lemongrass has parallel veins and fibrous roots. Wheat has parallel veins and fibrous roots.

Notice the word *almost*. A rule that is true nearly every time is still very useful. But we should say "almost always", not "always". That is more honest.

Now a question should be troubling you. Why should the veins in a leaf have anything to do with the shape of a root? They are at opposite ends of the plant.

The answer is not in the leaf. It is not in the root. It is in the seed.

### Activity 2.8 — Open a Seed

**What to do:**

2. Peel the thin outer coat off a soaked chana. Now try to split the seed with your thumbnail.
3. Try to split a soaked maize seed in the same way.

**Observe:** Which seed comes apart neatly into two halves? Which one does not?

**[ PAGE 17 ]**

## What Is Inside a Seed

A soaked chana splits neatly into two equal halves. Each half is called a **cotyledon**. Look carefully between the two halves. You will see a tiny root and a tiny shoot already formed. That is the start of the new plant, with food packed around it.

A maize seed does not split like that. It has only one thin cotyledon.

This gives us a third way to sort plants. And this one explains the other two.

Plants whose seeds have two cotyledons are called **dicotyledons**. We say **dicots** for short.
Plants whose seeds have one cotyledon are called **monocotyledons**, or **monocots**.

> **Illustration:** Two seeds drawn large and cut open. Left: a soaked chana seed opened into its two halves, hinged apart like a book, with the small root and shoot visible between them. Label "Cotyledons" with arrows pointing to both halves. Right: a maize grain shown in vertical section with its single thin cotyledon picked out in a contrasting tone and labelled. Caption: "Dicot seed (chana)" and "Monocot seed (maize)".

Now put all three findings together.

| | Dicots | Monocots |
|---|---|---|
| Cotyledons in the seed | Two | One |
| Leaf venation | Reticulate | Parallel |
| Roots | Taproot | Fibrous |
| Examples | Chana, mustard, hibiscus, mango | Wheat, maize, grass, banana |

This is the grouping worth having. Split one seed, and you can say two more things about a plant you have never seen before.

That is what makes a grouping really useful. It lets you say something new. It does not just tidy up what you already knew.

## What Movement Tells Us

Animals move in many ways. They walk, run, hop, crawl, fly, swim and glide. Some do more than one, like a pigeon.

Sorting them this way shows us something interesting. Animals that are not alike at all may still move in the same way, using similar body parts. A housefly and a pigeon are very different creatures. But both have wings, and both fly. A fish and a dolphin look alike in water, even though a dolphin is not a fish.

That is a clue about something you will meet soon in this chapter. The way an animal is built has a lot to do with *where* it lives.

Movement is not the only way to group animals. We can also group them by what they eat, by where they live, or by what covers the body — scales, feathers, fur, or nothing at all. As with plants, the best grouping depends on the question we are asking.

## Built for the Place

Look at a plant from a hot desert.

Water is hard to find there. The sun is very strong all day. The nights turn cold. A cactus meets all this with a thick, fleshy stem. The stem stores water through the long dry spells. Its outer surface is tough, so very little water escapes. Its leaves are only spines. A spine loses far less water than a wide flat leaf would.

Now look at a deodar tree, high in the Himalayas.

Here the problem is not heat. It is snow. Snow piling up on branches gets heavy enough to break them. A deodar is shaped like a cone. Its branches slope downward and bend easily. So the snow slides off instead of piling up.

Caption: "Hot desert" and "Cold mountain".

Neither plant chose its shape. But each one has features that suit the place it grows in. Plants without those features do not last long there.

## The Same Animal, Two Deserts

The clearest example of this is an animal you already know well.

Camels live in the hot sandy desert of Rajasthan. Camels also live in the cold, high, rocky desert of Ladakh. Both places are deserts. Both are dry, with little food and little shelter. But they are dry in very different ways. And the two camels are not the same.

The Rajasthan camel is tall, with long legs. Its hooves are broad and flat. They spread its weight, so it does not sink into loose sand. It has one hump. Its coat is thin.

The Ladakh camel is shorter and heavier. Its legs are shorter, which keeps it steady on rock and steep ground. It has two humps. It grows a thick shaggy coat, especially at the head and neck, for winters that fall far below freezing.

> **Illustration:** Two camels drawn side by side at the same scale for direct comparison. Left: a one-humped camel on sand, long-legged, with a short thin coat — draw an arrow to its broad flat hoof and label it. Right: a two-humped camel on snowy rocky ground, shorter and heavier, with a thick shaggy coat and long hair at the neck — draw an arrow to its smaller, harder hoof and label it. Caption: "Hot desert camel" and "Cold desert camel".

You may have heard that a camel stores water in its hump. That is not true. The hump stores fat. The camel lives off that fat when there is no food. By the end of winter, the humps of a Ladakh camel become smaller and lean over to one side.

Camels save water in other ways. They pass very little urine. Their dung is dry enough to burn. They hardly sweat at all. So a camel can go many days without drinking.

## Adaptations

The cactus stem, the deodar's sloping branches, the camel's hooves and hump — all of these are examples of one idea.

Features that make a living thing fit to survive in a particular place are called **adaptations**.

Adaptations are not always about extreme places, and they are not always easy to spot. A fish has a smooth body that slips through water easily, and fins to steer and push with. A duck has skin joining its toes. That turns each foot into a paddle. A pigeon's foot has no such skin, because a pigeon is not a swimmer.

Even the same kind of plant can show different adaptations in different places. Rhododendrons grow high in the Nilgiris, where the wind is strong. There they stay short, with small leaves that the wind cannot tear. The same kind of plant grows in more sheltered mountains in Sikkim. There it becomes a tall tree.

**What do you predict?** One plant grows on a windy seashore. The same kind of plant grows in a sheltered valley. Which one would you expect to be taller?

## Habitat

The place where a living thing lives is called its **habitat**.

A habitat gives a plant or animal what it needs — food, water, air, shelter, and a safe place for its young. The habitat of a camel is the desert. The habitat of a sea turtle is the ocean. The habitat of a rhododendron is the mountains.

Many different living things share one habitat. That is why habitats are where we find biodiversity.

Habitats can be sorted into two broad kinds.

Living things on land live in **terrestrial** habitats. Forests, deserts, grasslands and mountains are terrestrial habitats. So is the ground under your feet.

Living things in water live in **aquatic** habitats. Ponds, lakes, rivers and oceans are aquatic habitats.

Some animals do not fit into either one. A frog spends part of its life in water and part on land. Animals that live in both ways are called **amphibians**.

> **Layout — Know a scientist box:**

**Salim Ali (1896–1987)** spent many years travelling across India, watching birds. He recorded where they lived, when they arrived, and where they went next. Watching carefully like this, year after year, is how scattered sightings turn into real knowledge. His surveys led directly to the protection of Keoladeo in Rajasthan and Ranganathittu in Karnataka. His books also made it possible for ordinary people to name the birds around them. He is remembered as the Birdman of India.

## When a Habitat Is Lost

Ask your parents, your grandparents, or an older neighbour one simple question. Which birds, insects or plants did they see often as children, but rarely see now?

Many of them will be able to name several.

When a habitat is damaged or destroyed, the living things there lose everything at once — their food, their shelter, and the place where they raise their young. They usually cannot move somewhere else, because there is often nowhere else that suits them. They simply become fewer. Then they are gone. This is how biodiversity is lost.

In India, the Bengal tiger, the cheetah and the Great Indian bustard have all become fewer as their habitats have shrunk. Project Tiger began in 1973 to protect the tiger's forests. The bustard's grasslands are now protected areas in Gujarat, Rajasthan and Maharashtra.

Governments are not the only ones who protect habitats. **Sacred groves** are patches of forest that communities across India have left untouched for many generations. Nobody cuts trees there. Nobody hunts. Some groves are tiny and some are large. Many hold rare plants that have disappeared from the land all around them. They are protected not by law, but by agreement. Find out if there is one near you.

## Summary

- The variety of living things found in a region is called its biodiversity. Even an ordinary place has much more of it than a quick look suggests.
- Living things in a place depend on each other for food, shelter and other needs.
- Grouping means arranging things by the features they share. There is no one correct grouping. The useful one depends on the question being asked.
- Plants can be grouped by stem and height into herbs, shrubs and trees. Plants with weak stems are climbers or creepers.
- The pattern of veins in a leaf is called venation. It is either reticulate or parallel.
- Roots are of two kinds. A taproot has one main root. Fibrous roots form a spreading bunch.
- Seeds have either two cotyledons (dicots) or one cotyledon (monocots).
- These three features go together. Dicots generally have reticulate venation and a taproot. Monocots generally have parallel venation and fibrous roots. This is what makes the grouping useful — one observation lets you predict others.
- Animals can be grouped by how they move and which body parts they use. They can also be grouped in many other ways.
- Features that make a living thing fit to survive in a particular place are called adaptations.
- The place where a living thing lives is its habitat. Habitats are terrestrial or aquatic. Animals that live both ways are amphibians.
- Damage to habitats causes loss of biodiversity. This is why habitats are protected — by governments, and by communities.

## Learning Further

- Walk around your school with your teacher or the gardener. Label the plants with their local names. Make a list in your notebook. Next to each name, write its group — herb, shrub, tree, climber or creeper.

- Over one term, build a **class biodiversity register**. Every student adds what they see on field visits and on the way to school. Write the date and the place each time. A register kept for several years becomes very valuable, because it shows what is disappearing.

- Talk to the oldest person you know well. Ask which plants, birds and insects were common when they were your age. Write down what they say. Then find out what has changed in that area since then.

- Find out if there is a sacred grove, a protected forest or a bird sanctuary near your district. Who protects it? What is it being protected from?

- Read about one Indian scientist working on biodiversity today. Write a short report. You could start with Divya Mudappa, Vidya Athreya, Uma Ramakrishnan, Nandini Velho, Ghazala Shahabuddin, Usha Lachungpa or Divya Karnad.

- Grow chana and wheat again. This time keep one tray in bright light and one in a dark cupboard. Water both the same. Predict what will happen before you start. Then record what actually happens.
