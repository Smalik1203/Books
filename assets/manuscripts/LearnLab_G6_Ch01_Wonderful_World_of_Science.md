# 1. The Wonderful World of Science

## What Is Science?

Look around you right now. Something is happening everywhere you turn.

A fan is spinning. Sunlight is falling through a window and landing in a bright patch on the floor. Somewhere nearby, water is boiling, or a bird is calling, or dust is drifting through a beam of light. None of this is unusual. All of it is worth a second look.

You have been noticing things like this your whole life — why the moon seems to follow the car, why ice feels wet, why your shadow stretches long in the evening. Those questions were not childish. They were the beginning of something.

Now you are ready to go further.

Science is not a pile of facts to memorise. Science is a way of working. It means observing carefully, asking sharp questions, testing what you think you know, and being willing to change your mind when the evidence says you should.

## Science Is Not Kept in a Building

Science does not live in laboratories. Laboratories are useful, but science is wherever something is happening.

It is in the kitchen, where heat turns a soft batter into a firm, holey dosa. It is on the cricket field, where a ball bends in the air because of the way it was spun. It is in the soil under a mango tree, where organisms too small to see are breaking down fallen leaves. It is inside your own body, where your heart is beating right now without being told to.

## An Unfinished Map

Here is something surprising: science is not finished.

Think of what we know as a map of a huge, mostly unexplored land. Some regions are surveyed in fine detail. Others are sketched roughly. Some are still blank. And every time explorers map a new region, its edges open onto territory nobody knew existed. The map does not shrink the unknown — it reveals more of it.

Sometimes the mapmakers get it wrong. For centuries people were certain the Sun travelled around the Earth, because that is exactly what it looks like. Careful observation showed otherwise, and the map had to be redrawn. That correction was not a failure of science. It was science working as it should.

## The Most Important Tool

You will use many instruments in this book — thermometers, magnets, lenses, balances. But the tool that matters most is not one you can hold. It is **curiosity**.

Curiosity is what makes you look up at a night sky and wonder what the stars actually are, and how far away. It is what makes you notice that a flower on your balcony is shut at dawn and open by mid-morning, and then wonder how it knows.

**Why might this happen?** A flower has no eyes and no clock. What could it be responding to? You do not need the right answer yet — only a reasonable guess. That guess is called a *prediction*, and making one before you know the answer is a genuinely scientific act.

## One Idea in Many Places

Some of what you learn in this book will be useful the same week you learn it. You will understand why food spoils faster in May than in December, why a frayed electrical wire is dangerous, why boiling water before drinking it makes it safe.

But the real reward comes later, and it is stranger than that.

Leave a wet shirt on a line and by evening it is dry. Pour water into an earthen *matka* and the water inside stays cool even on a hot afternoon. Watch the sky before the monsoon and see clouds thickening where there was clear blue an hour ago.

Three ordinary things. They seem to have nothing to do with each other.

They are the same thing.

In all three, water is leaving a liquid surface and moving into the air as an invisible vapour. That is what dries the shirt. That is what cools the *matka* — water seeping through the clay escapes from its surface and carries warmth away with it. And that is where clouds come from, as vapour rising from oceans and rivers gathers high in the sky.

**Can you explain why?** If evaporation cools the *matka*, what does that suggest about why you feel cold when you step out of a swimming pool into the wind?

This is what happens as you learn more science. At first it looks like a long list of separate facts to be remembered. Then, slowly, the list starts folding in on itself. One idea turns out to explain four things you thought were unrelated. Then ten.

The subject gets simpler as you go deeper, not harder.

## What This Book Will Ask You

Begin with the Earth — as far as we know, the only place in the universe where anything is alive.

A seed pushes a pale shoot up through dark soil. A caterpillar seals itself away and comes out with wings. A calf that could barely stand in the morning is running across the field by the end of the month. Growing is so ordinary that we stop noticing it happening. But how does a plant build an entire body out of soil, water and air?

Everything that grows has to eat. In a country this large, that alone is a huge subject — pongal in one state, litti in another, a fish curry along the coast, a plate of rajma somewhere north. What are these dishes actually made of? And how could you find out for yourself, instead of being told?

Then there is water. You have jumped into a puddle after rain. But where was that water an hour before it fell on you, and how did it get up there? Cool it and it turns to ice. Heat it and it leaves as steam. What is going on?

And how hot is hot? When someone in your house has a fever, nobody wants a guess. They want a number.

Now look at what things are made of — paper, the metal of a key, a plastic scale, a rubber eraser, the magnet holding a cupboard shut, the cloth of your shirt. Each one was chosen for a reason. Could a key be made of rubber? Why not?

And when the Earth runs out of questions, there is the sky above it.

Every one of these has an answer. Not all of them are in this book.

## Working It Out Yourself

You already do science. You have simply never called it that.

Suppose the clock on your wall has stopped. Both hands are frozen at ten past four.

The first thing you do is ask a question, even if only in your head: *why has it stopped?*

Then, almost without noticing, you make a guess. The cell inside must be finished.

Making a guess is the easy part. What matters is what you do next. You take the clock down, open the back, and fit a new cell.

If the hands begin to move, your guess was right and the job is done.

But suppose they do not move.

Now stop and notice something. The clock is still stopped, so it feels as though nothing has been achieved. That is not true. You have learnt that the problem is *not* the cell — and a minute ago you did not know that. A test that proves you wrong still tells you something real.

So you make a second guess. Perhaps the cell is fine but is not pressing properly against the metal contacts. You push it in firmly and give it a moment. Still nothing.

A third guess, then: something inside is stuck. You look more closely and find the thin second hand has caught against the minute hand and cannot pass it.

Question. Guess. Test. Look honestly at what happened. Guess again if you must.

Nobody sat you down and taught you that sequence. Yet it is very close to what a scientist does when facing something no one has ever explained.

## The Steps Behind It

What you did with the clock has a name. It is called the **scientific method**, and written out plainly it looks like this:

1. **Observe** something that puzzles you or catches your interest.
2. **Ask a question** about it.
3. **Guess** a possible answer.
4. **Test** the guess, through an experiment or through further careful observation.
5. **Examine the result** and decide whether it actually answers your question.

That list looks tidier than real life ever is. Very often you finish step five only to discover you are back at step three with a fresh guess. Sometimes the test throws up something you were not even looking for, and that becomes a better question than the one you started with.

The order matters, but the neatness does not. What matters is that the guess comes *before* the test, and that you accept the result even when it is not the one you wanted.

## Everyone Does This

Scientists follow the scientific method for a living. But nobody owns it, and it is used every day by people who would never call themselves scientists.

A cook lifts the lid off the idli batter in the morning and finds it flat instead of risen. She asks why. Was the kitchen too cold overnight? Was the batter ground too thin? She will not solve it by worrying — she will change one thing tomorrow and watch what happens.

A tailor's machine starts skipping stitches. He does not replace the whole machine. He checks the needle first, then the thread tension, then the bobbin — one at a time, because changing everything at once would tell him nothing.

An electrician is called about a tubelight that flickers for a long time before it steadies. Is it the tube, the starter, or the wiring? Each of those can be tested separately.

None of them are in a laboratory. All of them are doing exactly what step three and step four describe: guess, then test — and crucially, test *one thing at a time*, so that the result actually means something.

## Curiosity, Company and a Long Road

Everything in this chapter rests on one habit, and it is not a difficult one. Notice things.

Noticing sounds too simple to matter. It is not. Most people walk past a hundred small oddities a day without registering a single one. The person who stops and says *hold on, that's strange* has done the hardest part already. Everything else — the questions, the guesses, the tests — follows from that pause.

And you do not have to do any of it alone. Science has a reputation for lonely geniuses, and it is mostly wrong. Discoveries today come from teams, often spread across many countries, arguing with one another and checking each other's work. Being told you are wrong by someone who has looked carefully at your evidence is not an insult. It is one of the most useful things that can happen to you.

So when you are stuck, ask someone. Compare what you saw with what your friend saw. If you disagree about what happened, that disagreement is worth more than either of your answers.

One last thing, and it is important. You will not find the answers to all your questions this year. Some of what you wonder about in Grade 6 you will only understand in Grade 9, or later, or possibly never — because nobody has worked it out yet.

That is not a warning. It is the best part.
