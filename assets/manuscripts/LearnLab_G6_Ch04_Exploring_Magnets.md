# 4. Exploring Magnets

Long ago, ships sailed from the coast of Kerala carrying pepper, cardamom and ginger. They went to lands far across the sea.

There were no engines then, and no maps of the open ocean. So how did a sailor know which way to go?

By day, he watched the sun. By night, he watched the stars. Sailors learnt the night sky so well that they could tell their direction from it.

But now think about a storm. Thick clouds cover the sky for two days. There is no sun. There are no stars. There is water in every direction, and every direction looks the same.

Which way is home?

For hundreds of years, this was a real and frightening problem. Then people found an answer. The answer was a small piece of stone that always turned the same way.

This chapter is about that stone, and about the magnets we use today.

> **Illustration:** A wooden sailing ship at night in a rough sea, seen from a little distance. Heavy clouds cover the whole sky — no stars and no moon are visible. Waves are high. A small lamp glows on the deck. The mood should be one of not knowing which way to go. No people need to be shown clearly.

## Magnets Around You

You have already met magnets many times, even if you did not notice them.

Some pencil boxes shut with a soft click. A magnet is doing that. The duster on a writing board may stay stuck to the board. A magnet is doing that too. Fridge doors, cupboard doors, purses, toys, stickers — all of these may have magnets hidden in them.

A magnet is a thing that pulls certain materials towards itself.

The first magnets were not made by people. They were found in the ground. They are pieces of a special rock called **lodestone**. A piece of lodestone pulls small bits of iron towards it. People discovered this a very long time ago.

Later, people learnt to make magnets from pieces of iron. Today magnets are made from several different materials.

The magnets in your school laboratory are made by people. So are the ones in pencil boxes and toys. All of these are **artificial magnets**.

Magnets are made in many shapes. Each shape suits a different job.

> **Illustration:** Five magnets drawn in a row, all in the same red-and-blue colouring with N and S marked: a bar magnet, a U-shaped magnet, a ring magnet, a disc magnet, and a cylindrical magnet. Label each one with its name below. Keep them all at roughly the same scale.

**Think:** Look around your classroom and your home. Where can you find a magnet doing a job?

## 4.1 Magnetic and Non-magnetic Materials

Bring a magnet near a steel pin and the pin jumps to it. Bring the same magnet near a rubber eraser and nothing happens.

So a magnet does not pull everything. It pulls only some things. Which ones?

### Activity 4.1 — What Sticks and What Does Not

**You will need:** a magnet, and many small objects made of different materials — a pencil, an eraser, a steel paper clip, a plastic scale, an iron nail, a glass marble, a coin, a piece of cloth, a key, a rubber band, a piece of wood, a sharpener.

**What to do:**

1. Look at each object. Write down what material it is made of.
2. **Now predict.** Before you test anything, write down whether you think it will stick to the magnet. Write Yes or No for every object.
3. Only after all your predictions are written, bring the magnet near each object, one by one.
4. Write down what really happened.

**Observe:** Compare the two columns. Where were you right? Where were you wrong?

**Think:** Look only at the objects that stuck. What do those objects have in common?

> **Layout:** A caution line — "Keep magnets away from small children. A swallowed magnet is dangerous."

Some objects were pulled towards the magnet and stuck to it. Others were not pulled at all.

Materials that are pulled towards a magnet are called **magnetic materials**.

Iron is a magnetic material. Steel is made mostly of iron, so steel is magnetic too. That is why a steel paper clip sticks. Two other metals, **nickel** and **cobalt**, are also magnetic. So are some mixtures of these metals with others.

Materials that are not pulled towards a magnet are called **non-magnetic materials**. Wood, rubber, plastic, glass, paper and cloth are all non-magnetic.

Here is something worth noticing. Not every metal is magnetic. Try a magnet on an aluminium vessel or a copper wire. Neither will stick. So "metal" and "magnetic" do not mean the same thing.

## 4.2 The Poles of a Magnet

Does a magnet pull equally hard everywhere along its length? Or are some parts stronger than others?

There is a neat way to find out. Iron filings are tiny bits of iron. They are light, so even a small pull will move them.

### Activity 4.2 — Where Is the Pull Strongest?

**You will need:** iron filings, a sheet of paper, a bar magnet.

**What to do:**

1. Place the bar magnet flat on the desk.
2. Cover it completely with the sheet of paper.
3. Sprinkle iron filings lightly over the paper, above the magnet.
4. Tap the paper gently a few times.
5. Watch where the filings gather.

**Observe:** Are the filings spread evenly? Or do they collect in some places more than others?

**Think:** What does this tell you about the magnet underneath?

> **Layout:** A caution box — "Iron filings are sharp and very fine. Never rub your eyes while using them. Wash your hands afterwards. Keeping the paper between the magnet and the filings also keeps the magnet clean — filings stuck directly to a magnet are very hard to remove."

***

**[ PAGE 6 ]**

## Two Ends, Two Poles

The filings do not spread evenly. They gather in thick bunches at the two ends of the bar magnet. In the middle, there are hardly any.

So the pull of a magnet is strongest at its two ends.

These two ends are called the **poles** of the magnet. Every magnet has two poles. One is called the **North pole**. The other is called the **South pole**. You will find out in a moment why those names were chosen.

> **Illustration:** A bar magnet lying flat, covered in iron filings. The filings form a thick bushy mass at each end, sticking out in all directions, and almost none in the middle. Mark the two ends N and S. Draw it large enough that the difference between the ends and the middle is obvious.

Try the same thing with a U-shaped magnet and with a ring magnet. You will find the same result. The filings gather at two places. Every magnet has two poles, whatever its shape.

Now here is a question. Suppose you break a bar magnet in half. Would you get one piece with only a North pole, and another with only a South pole?

You would not.

Each broken piece turns out to be a complete magnet, with a North pole and a South pole of its own. Break those pieces again and the same thing happens. Poles always come in pairs. A magnet with only one pole does not exist.

## 4.3 Finding Directions

We can now go back to the sailors in the storm.

### Activity 4.3 — Let the Magnet Choose

**You will need:** a bar magnet, thread, a table or a stand, a sheet of paper.

**What to do:**

1. Tie the thread around the middle of the bar magnet.
2. Hang it from the edge of a table, or from a stand. Move the thread a little until the magnet hangs level.
3. Make sure nothing made of iron or steel is nearby. Move away steel chairs, keys and pencil boxes.
4. Wait until the magnet stops turning on its own.
5. On the floor below, mark the spot under each end of the magnet. Join the two marks with a straight line.
6. Now give the magnet a gentle push so that it spins. Wait until it stops again.

**Observe:** Does it stop along the same line as before? Try it four or five times.

**Predict:** Before you push it a sixth time, say out loud where it will stop.

***

**[ PAGE 8 ]**

## Always the Same Line

However many times you spin it, the magnet stops along the same line.

Now find out what that line is. If you know where the sun rises, you know roughly where east is. Face east and stretch out your arms. North is on your left. South is on your right.

You will find that the hanging magnet stops along the north-south line, every single time.

One end always turns towards the north. That end is the **North pole** of the magnet. The other end always turns towards the south. That is the **South pole**. Now the names make sense. They are named after the direction each end looks for.

But why does this happen at all?

The reason is surprising. The Earth itself behaves like an enormous magnet. A hanging magnet is being pulled around by the Earth's own magnetism, until it settles along the north-south line.

Now do the activity once more, but hang a plain iron bar instead of a magnet. The iron bar stops wherever it likes. Sometimes east-west, sometimes in between. It has no fixed direction.

This gives us a useful test. Hang a metal bar freely. If it always settles north-south, it is a magnet. If it settles anywhere at all, it is not.

> **Illustration:** A bar magnet hanging from a thread tied at its middle, suspended from the edge of a wooden table, hanging level and still. On the floor below, two small chalk marks joined by a straight line, with the line labelled "North" at one end and "South" at the other.

## The Magnetic Compass

A hanging magnet works, but it is not much use on a rolling ship. It needs to be small, steady, and easy to read.

That is exactly what a **magnetic compass** is.

Inside a compass there is a thin magnet shaped like a needle. The needle sits balanced on a sharp pin, so it can turn very easily. Under the needle is a dial with the directions marked on it. A clear cover keeps out wind and rain.

Leave the compass on a flat surface and wait. The needle swings and then settles along the north-south line, just as the hanging magnet did. The end that points north is usually painted red, or marked in some other way.

To read it, turn the whole compass box gently — not the needle — until the N and S printed on the dial line up with the needle. Once they match, every direction on the dial is correct for that place.

This small device changed the world. With a compass, a ship could hold its course through cloud, fog and night. Sailors could cross open sea far from land, and still know which way was home.

> **Illustration:** A magnetic compass seen from directly above, drawn large. Show the round case, the clear cover, the printed dial with N, E, S, W and the in-between directions, and the needle balanced at the centre with one end clearly marked. Beside it, a small cut-away side view showing the needle resting on a pin.

## Make Your Own Compass

You do not need to buy a compass. You can make one.

### Activity 4.4 — A Compass in a Bowl

**You will need:** an iron sewing needle, a bar magnet, a small piece of cork, a bowl of water, a few steel pins.

**What to do:**

1. Lay the needle flat on a wooden table.
2. Hold one pole of the bar magnet at one end of the needle. Press lightly and slide it along the needle to the other end.
3. At the end of the needle, **lift the magnet right up** and away.
4. Bring the same pole back to the same starting end. Slide it along again in the same direction.
5. Repeat this thirty to forty times. Always stroke in one direction only. Always lift the magnet clear at the end of each stroke.
6. Test the needle. Bring it near some steel pins. If the pins cling to it, the needle has become a magnet.
7. Push the needle sideways through the piece of cork, so the needle stays level.
8. Float the cork in the bowl of water. The needle should stay above the water.
9. Wait for it to stop turning.

**Observe:** Note which way each end of the needle points. Now spin the cork gently and let it settle again. Do the ends point the same way as before?

> **Layout:** A caution line — "Sewing needles are sharp. Push the needle through the cork slowly, and point it away from your hand."

## An Old Indian Compass

Your floating needle is not a new idea.

Long before the modern compass came into common use, Indian sailors used something very similar. A piece of iron was magnetised and shaped like a fish. It was placed in a vessel of oil, where it could turn freely and settle along the north-south line.

It was called the ***matsya-yantra***, which means "fish instrument". Some people called it the *machchh-yantra*.

The oil served a purpose. It was thicker than water, so the fish did not swing wildly every time the ship rocked.

> **Illustration:** A shallow open bowl holding oil, seen from slightly above. A small flat fish-shaped piece of iron floats on the oil surface, pointing to one side. Keep the bowl and the fish simple and plain, as an old object would look.

**Think:** Your floating needle settles north-south. So does a bought compass. So did the *matsya-yantra*. What do all three have inside them?

Notice that all three solve the same problem in the same way. A magnet is allowed to turn freely, and then it is left alone to find its own direction. Everything else — the cork, the oil, the pin, the dial — is there only to let the magnet turn easily and to help us read the answer.

## 4.4 Pushing and Pulling

So far, magnets have pulled things towards them. Now let us see what a magnet does to another magnet.

### Activity 4.5 — Two Magnets

**You will need:** two bar magnets with their poles marked, five or six round pencils, a flat table.

**What to do:**

1. Call the magnets A and B.
2. Lay the round pencils side by side on the table. Rest magnet A across them, so it can roll easily.
3. Hold magnet B in your hand. Slowly bring one end of it near one end of magnet A. **Do not let them touch.**
4. Watch magnet A carefully. Which way does it move?
5. Now turn magnet B around. Bring its other end near the same end of magnet A.
6. Watch again.

**Observe:** Did magnet A move towards magnet B both times? Or did it move away one of those times?

**Think:** Look at the pole markings. What was different between the two tries?

> **Illustration:** Two drawings side by side. In each, a bar magnet rests on a row of round pencils, with a hand holding a second bar magnet nearby. In the first, the N of one faces the S of the other, and a short arrow shows the resting magnet rolling towards the hand. In the second, N faces N, and the arrow shows it rolling away. Mark all poles clearly.

## Like Poles and Unlike Poles

The result is always the same.

When the North pole of one magnet faces the South pole of another, the two are pulled together. This pulling together is called **attraction**.

When two North poles face each other, they push apart. Two South poles do the same. This pushing apart is called **repulsion**.

So we can say it simply:

- **Unlike poles attract.** North and South pull together.
- **Like poles repel.** North and North push apart. South and South push apart.

Now repeat the activity, but use a plain iron bar in place of magnet B.

Something different happens. The iron bar attracts magnet A whichever end you use. It never pushes it away.

This is more useful than it looks. Think carefully about what it means.

A magnet attracts a plain piece of iron. A magnet also attracts the unlike pole of another magnet. So attraction alone cannot tell you whether the thing in your hand is a magnet or just a piece of iron. Both would attract.

But only a magnet can push another magnet away. A plain piece of iron never repels anything.

So repulsion is the sure test. **If something repels a magnet, it must itself be a magnet.**

## A Magnet Near a Compass

A compass needle is a magnet too. So it should follow the same rules.

### Activity 4.6 — Making the Needle Move

**You will need:** a magnetic compass, a bar magnet with poles marked.

**What to do:**

1. Put the compass on a flat table, away from anything made of iron or steel.
2. Wait for the needle to settle along the north-south line.
3. Slowly bring the North pole of the bar magnet towards the north end of the needle.
4. Watch the needle. Does it turn? Which way?
5. Move the magnet away. Let the needle settle again.
6. Now bring the South pole of the bar magnet towards the north end of the needle.
7. Watch again.

**Observe:** The needle behaves differently in the two cases. Describe each one.

**Think:** Does this match what you found in Activity 4.5?

When the North pole of the magnet came close to the north end of the needle, the needle swung away. Two like poles repelled each other.

When the South pole came close to the north end of the needle, the needle swung towards it. Two unlike poles attracted each other.

The compass needle follows exactly the same rule as any other magnet. This is also why a compass gives a wrong reading when a magnet, or a large piece of iron, is kept near it.

## Can a Magnet Work Through Things?

A magnet pulls a paper clip across a gap of air. But what if something is put in the way?

### Activity 4.7 — Put Something in Between

**You will need:** a compass, a bar magnet, and flat sheets of wood, cardboard, plastic and glass.

**What to do:**

1. Set up the compass and magnet as in Activity 4.6, so the needle is clearly turned aside.
2. **Predict** what will happen if you slide a piece of wood into the gap between them. Write your prediction first.
3. Now stand the piece of wood upright between the magnet and the compass. Do not move the magnet or the compass.
4. Watch the needle.
5. Take the wood away. Try cardboard, then plastic, then glass.

**Table 4.2: Does the magnet still work?**

| S. no. | Material placed in between | What happened to the needle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Wood | |
| 2 | Cardboard | |
| 3 | Plastic | |
| 4 | Glass | |

The needle hardly changes at all. Whatever you put in the way, the magnet still turns the needle.

So the effect of a magnet passes straight through non-magnetic materials.

## 4.5 Things to Try

Now that you know how magnets behave, here are some things to make and try. Work out for yourself why each one works.

**A magnetic garland.** Hold a bar magnet above a heap of steel U-clips. Touch one clip to the magnet. Now touch a second clip to the first one. Keep going. How long a chain can you hang?

**A ball in a maze.** Draw a maze on a sheet of card and glue thin strips along the lines to make walls. Put a small steel ball inside. Now hold a magnet under the card and move it. Can you steer the ball out without touching it?

**A clip in water.** Drop a steel paper clip into a glass of water. Get it out using a magnet, without wetting your fingers or the magnet.

**Two racing cars.** Glue a bar magnet on top of each of two matchbox cars. Push them towards each other. Now turn one magnet around and try again. What changes?

> **Illustration:** Four small scenes in a two-by-two arrangement: (a) a hanging chain of steel clips below a bar magnet held in a hand, (b) a card maze with a small steel ball inside and a hand holding a magnet beneath the card, (c) a glass of water with a paper clip being lifted up the inside of the glass by a magnet held outside it, (d) two small matchbox cars with bar magnets on top, drawn just about to meet.

**Think:** In the maze and in the glass of water, the magnet never touches the thing it moves. Which activity in this chapter already explained that?

## Looking After Magnets

A magnet does not stay strong for ever. Treated badly, it grows weak and finally stops working. Treated well, it lasts for years.

**Do not drop a magnet, hammer it, or heat it.** Any of these will weaken it, and hard knocks or strong heat can ruin it completely.

**Store bar magnets in pairs.** Lay the two magnets side by side, with the North pole of one next to the South pole of the other. Place a small piece of wood between them so they do not snap together. Then lay a small piece of soft iron across each end. Stored this way, a pair of magnets keeps its strength much longer.

**Keep magnets away from certain things.** Mobile phones, watches, television remotes, computers and bank cards can all be damaged by a magnet kept close to them.

> **Illustration:** Two bar magnets lying side by side on a flat surface, with N and S marked so that the N of one is beside the S of the other. A thin wooden strip sits between them. A short bar of soft iron lies across each end, joining the two magnets. Label the wooden strip and the two iron pieces.

**Think:** Why should a compass never be stored right next to a strong magnet?

## Summary

- A magnet pulls certain materials towards itself. The first magnets were pieces of a rock called lodestone, found in the ground. The magnets we use today are made by people.
- Materials pulled towards a magnet are called magnetic materials. Iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are magnetic. Materials not pulled towards a magnet are non-magnetic. Not every metal is magnetic.
- The pull of a magnet is strongest at its two ends. These ends are called the North pole and the South pole.
- Poles always come in pairs. Break a magnet and each piece has both poles. A magnet with a single pole does not exist.
- A magnet that is free to turn always comes to rest along the north-south line. This happens because the Earth itself behaves like a giant magnet.
- A magnetic compass uses this. Its needle is a magnet that can turn freely, so it settles along the north-south line and shows us direction.
- Unlike poles attract each other. Like poles repel each other.
- A magnet attracts plain iron as well, so attraction does not prove that something is a magnet. Only a magnet can repel another magnet, so repulsion is the sure test.
- The effect of a magnet passes through non-magnetic materials such as wood, cardboard, plastic and glass.

## Learning Further

- Collect three or four different magnets. Use each one to pick up steel pins, and count how many pins each magnet can hold. Which magnet is strongest? Discuss with your friends why some magnets are stronger than others.

- Make a flying butterfly. Cut a butterfly out of paper and glue a steel paper clip to its body. Tie a thread to the clip and tape the other end of the thread to a table. Now hold a magnet above the butterfly, just far enough that the thread pulls tight. The butterfly will hang in the air without anything touching it. Move the magnet slowly and watch what happens.

- Find out about the Maglev train. It floats above its track instead of resting on rails. Which idea from this chapter is it using?

- Magnets are made in many shapes — bar, ring, disc, cylinder, horseshoe and sphere. Find out why. What job does each shape do well?

- Find out how magnets are used in hospitals. Start by finding out what the letters MRI stand for.

- Take your homemade compass outside. Use it to find which direction your classroom window faces. Then check your answer against where the sun rises.
