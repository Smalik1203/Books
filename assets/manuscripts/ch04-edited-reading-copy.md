# 4. Exploring Magnets

Long ago, ships sailed from the coast of Kerala carrying pepper, cardamom and ginger. They went to lands far across the sea.

Those sailing ships had no engines, and finding a course across open water was difficult. So how did a sailor know which way to go?

By day, he watched the sun. By night, he watched the stars. Sailors learnt the night sky so well that they could tell their direction from it.

But now think about a storm. Thick clouds cover the sky for two days. There is no sun. There are no stars. There is water in every direction, and every direction looks the same.

Which way is home?

A naturally magnetic stone, supported so it could turn freely, offered a direction reference when the sky was hidden. A direction alone did not tell sailors their position or the route home.

This chapter is about that stone, and about the magnets we use today.

> **Illustration:** A wooden sailing ship at night in a rough sea, seen from a little distance. Heavy clouds cover the whole sky — no stars and no moon are visible. Waves are high. A small lamp glows on the deck. The mood should be one of not knowing which way to go. No people need to be shown clearly.

## Magnets Around You

You have already met magnets many times, even if you did not notice them.

Some pencil boxes shut with a soft click. A magnet is doing that. The duster on a writing board may stay stuck to the board. A magnet is doing that too. Fridge doors, cupboard doors, purses, toys, stickers — all of these may have magnets hidden in them.

A **magnet** produces a magnetic field: a region in which it can exert magnetic forces. It can attract iron and can attract or repel another magnet. A force may act even when friction prevents movement.

The first magnets were not made by people. They were found in the ground. **Lodestone** is naturally magnetised magnetite, an iron-containing mineral. A piece of lodestone pulls small bits of iron towards it. People discovered this a very long time ago.

Later, people learnt to make magnets from pieces of iron. Today magnets include steel magnets, ceramic ferrite magnets and magnets made from alloys containing neodymium.

The magnets in your school laboratory are made by people. So are the ones in pencil boxes and toys. All of these are **artificial magnets**.

Magnets are made in many shapes. Each shape suits a different job.

> **Illustration:** Five magnets drawn in a row, all in the same red-and-blue colouring with N and S marked: a bar magnet, a U-shaped magnet, a ring magnet, a disc magnet, and a cylindrical magnet. Label each one with its name below. Keep them all at roughly the same scale.

**Think:** Look around your classroom and your home. Where can you find a magnet doing a job?

## 4.1 Magnetic and Non-magnetic Materials

Bring a magnet near a steel pin and the pin jumps to it. Bring the same magnet near a rubber eraser and nothing happens.

So a magnet does not pull everything. It pulls only some things. Which ones?

### Activity 4.1 — What Sticks and What Does Not

**You will need:** a magnet, and many small objects made of different materials — a pencil, an eraser, a steel paper clip, a plastic scale, an iron nail, a glass marble, a coin, a piece of cloth, a key, a rubber band, a piece of wood, a sharpener.

**What to do:**

1. Look at each object. Write down what material it is made of.
2. **Now predict.** Before you test anything, write down whether you think it will stick to the magnet. Write Yes or No for every object.
3. Only after all your predictions are written, bring the magnet near each object, one by one.
4. Write down what really happened.

**Observe:** Compare the two columns. Where were you right? Where were you wrong?

**Think:** Look only at the objects that stuck. What do those objects have in common?

> **Layout:** A caution line — "Keep magnets away from small children. A swallowed magnet is dangerous."

In these school tests, **magnetic materials** are materials, such as iron, that respond strongly enough to a magnet for us to notice the attraction. They need not already be permanent magnets.

Iron is a magnetic material. Steel is made mostly of iron. Many kinds of steel are magnetic, though some stainless steels are not. That is why a steel paper clip sticks. Two other metals, **nickel** and **cobalt**, are also magnetic. So are some mixtures of these metals with others.

We call materials with no noticeable response in these tests **non-magnetic materials**. This does not mean that every magnetic effect on them is exactly zero. Wood, rubber, plastic, glass, paper and cloth are all non-magnetic.

Here is something worth noticing. Not every metal is magnetic. Try a magnet on an aluminium vessel or a copper wire. Neither will stick. So "metal" and "magnetic" do not mean the same thing.

## 4.2 The Poles of a Magnet

Does a magnet pull equally hard everywhere along its length? Or are some parts stronger than others?

Iron filings are tiny bits of iron. Their pattern gives us evidence about the magnetic field. Keep the paper level and sprinkle a thin, even layer so that the starting arrangement does not decide the result.

### Activity 4.2 — Where Is the Pull Strongest?

**You will need:** iron filings, a sheet of paper, a bar magnet.

**What to do:**

1. Place the bar magnet flat on the desk.
2. Cover it completely with the sheet of paper.
3. Sprinkle iron filings lightly over the paper, above the magnet.
4. Tap the paper gently a few times.
5. Watch where the filings gather.

**Observe:** Are the filings spread evenly? Or do they collect in some places more than others?

**Think:** What does this tell you about the magnet underneath?

> **Layout:** A caution box — "Iron filings are sharp and very fine. Never rub your eyes while using them. Wash your hands afterwards. Keeping the paper between the magnet and the filings also keeps the magnet clean — filings stuck directly to a magnet are very hard to remove."

***

## Two Ends, Two Poles

The filings do not spread evenly. They gather in thick bunches at the two ends of the bar magnet. In the middle, there are hardly any.

The pattern suggests a stronger effect near the ends. It is not a force meter: the filings become temporarily magnetised, turn into alignment and attract one another into chains.

The strongest regions near the ends are the **poles** of this bar magnet. The simple magnets studied here have a North and a South pole; specially made magnets may have several pole regions. One is called the **North pole**. The other is called the **South pole**. You will find out in a moment why those names were chosen.

> **Illustration:** A bar magnet lying flat, covered in iron filings. The filings form a thick bushy mass at each end, sticking out in all directions, and almost none in the middle. Mark the two ends N and S. Draw it large enough that the difference between the ends and the middle is obvious.

A U-shaped magnet has poles at its two tips. In many school ring magnets, one flat face is North and the other is South. Pole positions depend on how a magnet is made; they need not look like the ends of a bar.

Now here is a question. Suppose you break a bar magnet in half. Would you get one piece with only a North pole, and another with only a South pole?

You would not.

Each broken piece turns out to be a complete magnet, with a North pole and a South pole of its own. Break those pieces again and the same thing happens. Cutting divides the material, not the two poles into separate objects. Each piece still has a North and a South pole. Do not break magnets to test this.

## 4.3 Finding Directions

We can now go back to the sailors in the storm.

### Activity 4.3 — Let the Magnet Choose

**You will need:** a bar magnet, thread, a table or a stand, a sheet of paper.

**What to do:**

1. Tie the thread around the middle of the bar magnet.
2. Hang it from the edge of a table, or from a stand. Move the thread a little until the magnet hangs level.
3. Make sure nothing made of iron or steel is nearby. Move away steel chairs, keys and pencil boxes.
4. Wait until the magnet stops turning on its own.
5. On the floor below, mark the spot under each end of the magnet. Join the two marks with a straight line.
6. Now give the magnet a gentle push so that it spins. Wait until it stops again.

**Observe:** Does it stop along the same line as before? Try it four or five times.

**Predict:** Before you push it a sixth time, say out loud where it will stop.

***

**[ PAGE 8 ]**

## Always the Same Line

Compare your repeated trials. Does the same end return to the same direction? A sticking support or a nearby magnet can change the result.

Now find out what that line is. If you know where the sun rises, you know roughly where east is. Face east and stretch out your arms. North is on your left. South is on your right.

Away from nearby magnets and iron objects, it settles along the magnetic north-south line. This is usually close to geographic north-south, but not exactly the same.

One end always turns towards the north. That end is the **North pole** of the magnet. The other end always turns towards the south. That is the **South pole**. Now the names make sense. They are named after the direction each end looks for.

But why does this happen at all?

Earth has a magnetic field. It exerts a turning effect, called **torque**, on a freely suspended magnet. The magnet rotates into alignment with the local field; it is not being carried towards the North Pole. Earth does not contain a giant bar magnet.

Repeat with an iron bar, if your teacher has one. Record its direction without prescribing a result. Iron can retain magnetisation, and its shape and Earth's field can affect alignment. Failure to turn is not proof that a bar is unmagnetised.

This gives us a useful test. Hang a metal bar freely. If the same end repeatedly points towards magnetic north, that is evidence that the bar is magnetised. Check that it turns freely and that no nearby magnet is disturbing it.

> **Illustration:** A bar magnet hanging from a thread tied at its middle, suspended from the edge of a wooden table, hanging level and still. On the floor below, two small chalk marks joined by a straight line, with the line labelled "North" at one end and "South" at the other.

## The Magnetic Compass

A hanging magnet works, but it is not much use on a rolling ship. It needs to be small, steady, and easy to read.

That is exactly what a **magnetic compass** is.

Inside a compass there is a thin magnet shaped like a needle. The needle sits balanced on a sharp pin, so it can turn very easily. Under the needle is a dial with the directions marked on it. A clear cover keeps out wind and rain.

Leave the compass on a flat surface and wait. The needle swings and then settles along the north-south line, just as the hanging magnet did. The end that points north is usually painted red, or marked in some other way.

To read it, turn the whole compass box gently — not the needle — until the marked north-seeking end points to N on the dial. Matching only the needle's line could put the dial backwards. The dial now gives magnetic directions; these can differ from geographic directions.

This small device changed the world. With a compass, a ship could hold its course through cloud, fog and night. It supplied a direction reference; sailors still needed information about their position and planned route.

> **Illustration:** A magnetic compass seen from directly above, drawn large. Show the round case, the clear cover, the printed dial with N, E, S, W and the in-between directions, and the needle balanced at the centre with one end clearly marked. Beside it, a small cut-away side view showing the needle resting on a pin.

## Make Your Own Compass

You do not need to buy a compass. You can make one.

### Activity 4.4 — A Compass in a Bowl

**You will need:** a steel sewing needle, a bar magnet, a small piece of cork, a bowl of water, a few steel pins.

**What to do:**

1. Before stroking, test whether the needle attracts a pin. Record the result in your notebook, then lay the needle flat on a wooden table.
2. Hold one pole of the bar magnet at one end of the needle. Press lightly and slide it along the needle to the other end.
3. At the end of the needle, **lift the magnet right up** and away.
4. Bring the same pole back to the same starting end. Slide it along again in the same direction.
5. Repeat this thirty to forty times. Always stroke in one direction only. Always lift the magnet clear at the end of each stroke.
6. Put the bar magnet well away. Repeat the pin test and compare with your before-test. Attraction alone is not conclusive; check the floating needle next.
7. Ask an adult to secure the needle flat on top of the cork with a small piece of tape, so it stays level.
8. Float the cork in the bowl of water. The needle should stay above the water.
9. Wait for it to stop turning.

**Observe:** Gently turn the cork and let it settle several times. Does the same needle end return towards magnetic north, checked with a compass well away from the bowl? What evidence is stronger than pin attraction alone?

> **Layout:** A caution line — "Sewing needles are sharp. Push the needle through the cork slowly, and point it away from your hand."

## What Makes the Needle Work?

The stroking magnet does not transfer a magnetic substance to the needle. Its field helps align small magnetic regions inside the steel. Some alignment remains after the magnet is removed. This is **magnetisation**.

Using one pole and repeating the same stroke encourages a consistent alignment. Thirty to forty strokes is a starting procedure, not a guaranteed threshold: the needle material and magnet strength affect the result.

A fish-shaped floating model can use the same principle. The drawing is a model, not evidence of how a historical instrument was built.

A liquid can slow down a turning magnet. The essential idea is to support the magnet so it can turn freely. A solid piece of iron will sink unless its shape or a floating support keeps it at the surface.

> **Illustration:** A shallow open bowl holding oil, seen from slightly above. A small flat fish-shaped piece of iron floats on the oil surface, pointing to one side. Keep the bowl and the fish simple and plain, as an old object would look.

**Think:** Compare your bowl compass with a bought compass. Which part is magnetised? Which part supports it? How could you test whether either needle is sticking?

Notice that all three solve the same problem in the same way. A magnet is allowed to turn freely, and then it is left alone to find its own direction. The cork provides floating support; a pin provides a pivot. Liquid slows turning. The dial labels directions. These parts do different jobs.

## 4.4 Pushing and Pulling

So far, magnets have pulled things towards them. Now let us see what a magnet does to another magnet.

### Activity 4.5 — Two Magnets

**You will need:** two bar magnets with their poles marked, five or six round pencils, a flat table.

**What to do:**

1. Call the magnets A and B.
2. Lay the round pencils side by side on the table. Rest magnet A across them, so it can roll easily.
3. Hold magnet B in your hand. Slowly bring one end of it near one end of magnet A. **Do not let them touch.**
4. Watch magnet A carefully. Which way does it move?
5. Now turn magnet B around. Bring its other end near the same end of magnet A.
6. Watch again.

**Observe:** Did magnet A move towards magnet B both times? Or did it move away one of those times?

**Think:** Look at the pole markings. What was different between the two tries?

> **Illustration:** Two drawings side by side. In each, a bar magnet rests on a row of round pencils, with a hand holding a second bar magnet nearby. In the first, the N of one faces the S of the other, and a short arrow shows the resting magnet rolling towards the hand. In the second, N faces N, and the arrow shows it rolling away. Mark all poles clearly.

## Like Poles and Unlike Poles

Compare your observations with the pole markings.

When the North pole of one magnet faces the South pole of another, the two are pulled together. This pulling together is called **attraction**.

When two North poles face each other, they push apart. Two South poles do the same. This pushing apart is called **repulsion**.

So we can say it simply:

- **Unlike poles attract.** North and South pull together.
- **Like poles repel.** North and North push apart. South and South push apart.

Now repeat the activity, but use a plain iron bar in place of magnet B.

An initially unmagnetised iron bar is attracted by either pole. The nearby magnet aligns magnetic regions in the iron, inducing magnetisation. The nearer iron end behaves as the unlike pole. Much of this alignment may disappear when the magnet is removed.

Compare two explanations for attraction: the object could already be a magnet, or it could become magnetised nearby.

A magnet attracts a plain piece of iron. A magnet also attracts the unlike pole of another magnet. So attraction alone cannot tell you whether the thing in your hand is a magnet or just a piece of iron. Both would attract.

For our magnet-or-iron candidates, repulsion rules out initially unmagnetised iron. Attraction does not distinguish the two explanations.

For the magnets and plain iron bars in these experiments, repulsion is the sure test. **A bar that repels one pole of a magnet is itself magnetised.**

## A Magnet Near a Compass

A compass needle is a magnet too. So it should follow the same rules.

### Activity 4.6 — Making the Needle Move

**You will need:** a magnetic compass, a bar magnet with poles marked.

**What to do:**

1. Put the compass on a flat table, away from anything made of iron or steel.
2. Wait for the needle to settle along the north-south line.
3. Mark a position diagonally beside the north-seeking end, clear of the needle. Slowly approach that position with the North pole, keeping the magnet horizontal.
4. Watch the needle. Does it turn? Which way?
5. Move the magnet away. Let the needle settle again.
6. Reverse the bar magnet and approach the same marked position with its South pole. Keep the distance and height the same.
7. Watch again.

**Observe:** Sketch the needle direction in each trial. Did reversing the pole change its direction? Did it return when the magnet was removed?

**Think:** Does this match what you found in Activity 4.5?

The needle turns in response to both Earth's field and the nearby magnet's field. The effect acts on the whole needle, producing torque about its pivot.

Reversing the nearby magnet reverses its contribution to the field. A sideways approach makes turning easier to see than an approach exactly along the needle's line.

A compass responds to the local magnetic field, including fields from nearby objects. This is also why a compass gives a wrong reading when a magnet, or a large piece of iron, is kept near it.

## Can a Magnet Work Through Things?

A magnet pulls a paper clip across a gap of air. But what if something is put in the way?

### Activity 4.7 — Put Something in Between

**You will need:** a compass, a bar magnet, and flat sheets of wood, cardboard, plastic and glass.

**What to do:**

1. Set up the compass and magnet as in Activity 4.6, so the needle is clearly turned aside.
2. **Predict** what will happen if you slide a piece of wood into the gap between them. Write your prediction first.
3. Stand the wood in the gap. Keep both the magnet and compass still.
4. Watch the needle.
5. Take the wood away. Try cardboard, then plastic, then glass.

**Table 4.2: Does the magnet still work?**

| S. no. | Material placed in between | What happened to the needle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Wood | |
| 2 | Cardboard | |
| 3 | Plastic | |
| 4 | Glass | |

Compare the needle directions with and without each thin sheet, at the same separation. Little change is evidence that these sheets do not noticeably screen the field in this setup.

This result is limited to the tested materials and thicknesses. A magnetic field is not a substance flowing through a sheet. Increasing the gap can reduce the effect even without a sheet.

## 4.5 Things to Try

Now that you know how magnets behave, here are some things to make and try. Work out for yourself why each one works.

**A magnetic garland.** Hold a bar magnet above a heap of steel U-clips. Touch one clip to the magnet. Now touch a second clip to the first one. Keep going. How long a chain can you hang?

**A ball in a maze.** Draw a maze on a sheet of card and glue thin strips along the lines to make walls. Put a small steel ball inside. Now hold a magnet under the card and move it. Can you steer the ball out without touching it?

**A clip in water.** Drop a steel paper clip into a glass of water. Get it out using a magnet, without wetting your fingers or the magnet.

**Two racing cars.** Secure a bar magnet on each toy car with removable tape or an elastic band. Push them towards each other. Now turn one magnet around and try again. What changes?

## Looking After Magnets

A magnet's strength depends on its material and magnetic alignment. Normal use does not use up magnetism like fuel. Heat or strong impacts can disturb that alignment.

**Do not drop a magnet, hammer it, or heat it.** Strong impacts or enough heat can damage a magnet or reduce its magnetisation; the effect depends on the material. Do not heat or hammer magnets as a classroom test.

**Store bar magnets in pairs.** Lay the two magnets side by side, with the North pole of one next to the South pole of the other. Place a small piece of wood between them so they do not snap together. Then lay a small piece of soft iron across each end. Keepers provide a path through soft iron linking unlike poles, helping preserve magnetisation in traditional school magnets. Follow the supplied storage advice for other types.

**Storage precautions.** Keep strong magnets away from compasses, mechanical watches, magnetic-stripe cards and magnetically stored data. Follow the maker’s advice for electronic devices; not every device is affected in the same way.

> **Illustration:** Two bar magnets lying side by side on a flat surface, with N and S marked so that the N of one is beside the S of the other. A thin wooden strip sits between them. A short bar of soft iron lies across each end, joining the two magnets. Label the wooden strip and the two iron pieces.

**Think:** Why should a compass never be stored right next to a strong magnet?

## Keywords

Artificial magnet · Attraction · Bar magnet · Lodestone · Magnetic compass · Magnetic material · Non-magnetic material · North pole · Poles · Repulsion · Ring magnet · South pole · U-shaped magnet

## Summary

- A magnet produces a magnetic field and can attract iron or attract or repel another magnet. Lodestone is naturally magnetised magnetite; artificial magnets are made by people.
- Materials showing strong attraction in our school tests are called magnetic materials. Iron, nickel, cobalt and many kinds of steel are magnetic. Materials not pulled towards a magnet are non-magnetic. Not every metal is magnetic.
- A bar magnet pulls most strongly near its two ends, called the North pole and the South pole.
- Each piece of a broken bar magnet still has both poles. Breaking does not separate a North pole from a South pole.
- Away from nearby magnetic disturbances, a freely turning magnet tends to align with Earth's local magnetic field. This is close to, but not necessarily along, geographic north-south.
- A magnetic compass uses this. Its needle is a magnet that can turn freely, so it settles along the north-south line and shows us direction.
- Unlike poles attract each other. Like poles repel each other.
- A magnet attracts plain iron as well, so attraction does not prove that something is a magnet. For our magnet-or-iron candidates, repulsion identifies an already magnetised bar.
- Thin wood, cardboard, plastic and glass did not noticeably screen the field in our fixed-gap test. This does not establish a rule for all barriers.

## Learning Further

- Collect three or four different magnets. Use identical pins, the same contact region and the same lifting procedure for each magnet. Repeat three times. Which lifts most pins under those conditions? Explain why this is a performance comparison, not a measurement independent of shape or contact.

- Make a flying butterfly. Cut a butterfly out of paper and glue a steel paper clip to its body. Tie a thread to the clip and tape the other end of the thread to a table. Now hold a magnet above the butterfly, just far enough that the thread pulls tight. The butterfly can remain below the magnet without touching it. Upward magnetic force balances its weight and the downward pull of the taut thread. Move the magnet slowly and watch what happens.

- Find out about the Maglev train. It floats above its track instead of resting on rails. Find a diagram from a railway or museum source. Identify what provides upward support and what keeps the train aligned; do not assume every design uses two repelling permanent magnets.

- Magnets are made in many shapes — bar, ring, disc, cylinder, horseshoe and sphere. Find out why. What job does each shape do well?

- Find out how magnets are used in hospitals. Find what MRI stands for on a hospital or university page. Identify one use of its strong magnetic field and explain why a magnet attracting iron does not explain how an image is formed.

- Take your homemade compass outside. Use it to find which direction your classroom window faces. Then check your answer against where the sun rises.
