Looking Closely:
How Science Knows

1.1What Changes Now

For the last few years science has been asking you to look. To notice that the moon does not rise at the same time each night, that a cut apple browns and a cut lemon does not, that a bicycle is harder to push uphill than along. You asked questions, you tried things, and you found out how a good many things work.

From here the question shifts. It is no longer only what we know about the world, but how we know it — how an observation becomes a measurement, how a measurement becomes a pattern, how a pattern gets written down in symbols so that somebody three thousand kilometres away can check it, and what happens on the day it turns out to be wrong.

That last part matters more than it sounds. A great deal of what follows in Grades 9 and 10 is not new facts about nature but better habits of asking: being exact about words, careful about units, honest about what a measurement can and cannot settle, and willing to give up a comfortable idea when the evidence goes against it.

What the foot of the page is telling you

Look at the bottom of this page before you turn it. There are two marks down there, and they are not decoration. One is a solid slab carrying the page number. The other is an outline of the same shape, and it carries nothing at all.

Read them as a pair and they are the whole method. The solid one is what has been settled — measured, checked, written down, and now countable. The empty one is the same shape waiting to be filled: the question not yet answered, which is the larger half of science and always will be. Both run off the edge of the paper rather than stopping neatly at the margin, because neither the evidence nor the questions end where this book does.

Boxes with a rule above and below are asides: a thread of curiosity to follow, or a note. Boxes with a tinted ground are matter to work through — an example, a question put to you, or a step past the syllabus. None of them is optional reading; they are simply doing different jobs.
