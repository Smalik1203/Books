Meet a Scientist
Meghnad Saha
1893–1956

Asked why stars come in different colours, Saha did not try to account for every atom inside one. He treated a whole star as a hot gas, threw away its motion and nearly all of its chemistry, and kept three things: temperature, pressure, and whether atoms had lost electrons. From those three he wrote down, in 1920, an equation linking what a star is made of to how hot it is — and astronomers could suddenly read a star's temperature off its light. He turned the same habit of stripping a problem down on rivers and flood control in later life, and was elected to Parliament in 1952.

1.3Saying Exactly What You Mean

Science borrows most of its words from ordinary speech and then quietly narrows them. In conversation, work is anything that tires you out and force is roughly the same as effort. In physics, holding a heavy bag perfectly still is no work at all, however much your arm disagrees. Neither meaning is the correct one; they are simply different words that happen to be spelled alike.

The narrowing is not fussiness. Thousands of people in dozens of languages have to be able to read the same result and take the same meaning from it, without meeting, and often centuries apart. That only works if every load-bearing word has one agreed sense.

You have met this before without noticing. In the kitchen a tomato is a vegetable; in botany it is a fruit. Neither the cook nor the botanist is mistaken. They are sorting the same object for different purposes — one by how it tastes and what it is served with, the other by where the seeds sit — and each sorting is exactly right for the questions its owner asks.
