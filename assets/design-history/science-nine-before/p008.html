1.4Laws, Theories and Principles

Three words get used almost interchangeably outside science and mean quite different things inside it. Sorting them out is worth ten minutes, because a great deal of confused argument comes from mixing them up.

Three different jobs

A law states a pattern that nature is observed to keep, usually in a few words or an equation. It says what happens, not why.

A theory explains why that pattern holds. It is built from evidence gathered over a long time and is expected to account for many separate observations at once.

A principle is a broad rule that holds across many situations and is used to reason about a new one.

Newton's laws of motion are laws in exactly that sense. They tell you that a bus braking sharply will throw a standing passenger forwards, and by how much, without saying a word about why matter behaves that way. Atomic theory is a theory: it explains why elements combine in fixed ratios, why a compound has properties neither of its parts had, and why a chemical equation balances — three facts that look unrelated until the theory joins them. Conservation of energy is a principle: you can apply it to a falling stone, a boiling kettle or a climbing child without needing a separate law for each.

What a theory is not

In everyday speech, it's only a theory means it is a guess somebody has not bothered to check. In science the word means close to the opposite. A theory is what an idea is promoted to after it has survived a great deal of checking, made predictions that came true, and explained things it was not invented to explain.

Nothing arrives as a theory. Ideas start as hunches, become hypotheses that can be tested, and are called theories only once the evidence is substantial. Calling something a theory in this book is a statement of confidence, not a hedge.

None of which makes a theory permanent. A theory is the best account we have of the evidence in hand, and evidence keeps arriving. What protects it is not certainty but the fact that anything replacing it has to explain everything the old one explained, and then something more.
