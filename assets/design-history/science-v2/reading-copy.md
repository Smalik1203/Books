# 2. Diversity in the Living World

Go outside your classroom. Stand still for two minutes.

Look for grass in a crack, insects near a wall, or birds overhead. Choose two plants. Compare their leaves. What differences can you describe?

A planted tree and a plant growing without anyone sowing it both belong in your record. You do not need to know how something arrived before you can observe it.

This chapter is about that variety. How much of it is there? How do we make sense of it? And why are the living things in one place so different from the living things in another?

## Living Things Need Each Other

The living things in a place are not just sharing space. They are joined to each other.

A tree can provide shade for smaller plants and nesting places for birds. Some birds feed on insects on its bark. A fruit-eating bird may carry a seed away; the seed can grow if it reaches suitable conditions. Fallen leaves provide food for decomposers, which help return nutrients to the soil.

Removing a tree can remove shade, nest sites and a source of fallen leaves. The effects depend on which organisms use it and what other resources are available nearby.

## 2.2 Making Sense of the Variety

Your records may contain many kinds of living things. Grouping them can help you compare a feature without searching through every entry.

In a stationery shop, keeping pens together helps a customer find a pen. Arranging goods by price answers a different question: what can I buy with this amount of money?

Scientists do the same with living things. Putting things into groups by the features they share is called **grouping**.

## Herbs, Shrubs and Trees

Compare your records with the descriptions below. Your sample may not contain all three forms, and young or pruned plants may be harder to classify.

Plants with soft, non-woody stems are called **herbs**. Many are small, such as mint, coriander and tomato, but height alone does not define a herb.

**Shrubs** are woody plants that usually branch close to the ground, often with several main stems. A rose bush is an example. Observe the branching as well as the height.

**Trees** are woody plants that usually develop a main trunk supporting a crown of branches. Mango is an example. A young mango is still a tree even when it is shorter than a mature tomato plant.

Woody stems support branches and can thicken as a plant grows. Look at the base of a tree and then a herb: what differences can you describe without damaging either?

> **Illustration:** Three plants drawn side by side against a common ground line, at true relative scale so the height difference is obvious: (a) a mango tree, (b) a rose plant, (c) a tomato plant. For each, draw a short arrow pointing to the stem near the ground and label the arrows "one thick hard stem", "several hard stems, branching low", "one soft green stem". Caption: Tree, Shrub, Herb.

A banana plant is a large herb. Its trunk-like structure is a **pseudostem**, made from tightly overlapping leaf bases rather than a woody trunk. Its height does not make it a tree.

## Plants That Cannot Stand Up

Not every plant can hold itself up.

Some plants have stems that are too weak to stand. So they climb up something else — a wall, a fence, a stick, or another plant. These are called **climbers**. A money plant is a climber. So is a grape vine.

Other plants also have weak stems. But instead of climbing, they spread flat along the ground. These are called **creepers**. Pumpkin and watermelon plants are creepers.

Climbing and trailing describe growth habits, not a separate woody/non-woody division. A grape vine is a woody climber; a pumpkin has non-woody trailing stems. A plant can therefore be described in more than one way.

> **Illustration:** Two plants shown side by side. Left: a climber with a thin stem winding around a vertical bamboo stick, with small curling tendrils gripping the stick — include a small inset circle enlarging one tendril. Right: a creeper spreading flat across the ground with large leaves and a fruit resting on the soil. Label the two "Climber" and "Creeper".

## Lines on a Leaf

Hold a leaf up against the light. You will see thin lines running through it. These lines are **veins**. The pattern the veins make is called **venation**.

Veins contain tissues that transport water and dissolved substances. They carry water into the leaf and sugars made in the leaf to other parts of the plant. They also help support the leaf.

### Activity 2.5 — Take a Leaf Print

**You will need:** fallen leaves of at least four different plants, thin paper, a pencil with soft lead.

**What to do:**

1. Put a leaf on the desk with its back side facing up. The veins stand out more on that side.
2. Place a sheet of thin paper over the leaf.
3. Hold the paper still. Rub the side of the pencil lead flat across it, using light strokes.
4. Check the print against the leaf. If the veins are unclear, adjust the paper or pressure and try again.
5. Do this for every leaf. Write the plant's name next to each print.

**Observe:** Put all your prints side by side.

**Think:** Group the prints by their vein patterns. How many groups does your sample support? Explain your rule. If all the patterns look alike, compare with another group’s leaves.

> Editorial transition

Compare the vein pattern separately from the leaf outline. Two leaves can have different outlines yet share a vein pattern. Point to the lines that support your grouping; another group should be able to follow your rule without knowing the plant names.

## Two Patterns

Compare your prints with these examples. Two common patterns are shown here; your sample may contain only one.

In **reticulate venation**, veins branch to form a net. A thick middle vein is visible in the hibiscus leaf; finer veins branch and join around it. Mango, neem and peepal also have net-like venation.

In other leaves, many veins run alongside one another. This pattern is called **parallel venation**. In a grass blade they run along the length of the leaf. A banana leaf has a strong middle vein, with many parallel side veins running out towards its edges. Parallel venation does not mean that a leaf has no middle vein.

> **Illustration:** Three leaves drawn large and flat, with veins clearly picked out in a darker tone: (a) a hibiscus leaf showing one thick middle vein with a branching net of finer veins on both sides, (b) a banana leaf showing many parallel veins running from base to tip, (c) a single blade of grass showing the same parallel pattern at a smaller scale. Label the patterns "Reticulate venation" and "Parallel venation" and caption each leaf with its plant.

These two patterns help you compare many of the leaves around you. Look closely: real leaves sometimes show exceptions.

Predict the venation of wheat or maize from the examples. Then inspect a leaf or a clear photograph. Was your prediction supported? Leaf shape alone is not proof of its vein pattern.

## Under the Ground

Roots hold plants in place and take up water and dissolved minerals. You can observe their growth as seeds germinate.

### Activity 2.6 — Grow Your Own Roots

**You will need:** a handful of chana (chickpea) seeds, a handful of wheat seeds, two shallow trays or plates, cotton cloth or cotton wool, water.

**What to do:**

1. Soak both kinds of seed in water overnight.
2. Spread a wet cloth in each tray. Put the chana seeds in one tray. Put the wheat seeds in the other.
3. Keep both trays in the same place. Keep the cloth damp, not flooded, and check it every morning for six days.
4. From day three, inspect the seedlings daily. Record when each germinates and sketch the roots without tearing them.
5. On day six, compare several seedlings of each kind. Keep any that germinated late and observe them for a few more days. Do not pull roots out of the cloth; examine them gently.

**Observe:** Draw what grew from each kind of seed in your notebook. Use the same scale for both drawings.

**Think:** Could drawings alone tell you which plant is harder to pull out? What would you need to keep alike in a comparison of root anchorage?

> Editorial transition

Give each seedling a small identifying label and date each sketch. This lets you follow one seedling through time instead of mistaking differences between seedlings for changes in one plant. Place a ruler beside it without disturbing the roots; include a scale in your drawing.

Count age from germination as well as recording the calendar date. If one seed germinated on Monday and another on Thursday, drawings made on Friday show different stages of growth. Compare seedlings of similar age before deciding that a difference belongs to the two kinds of plant.

> **Layout:** Two bordered drawing boxes, each about 55 × 70 mm, labelled "Chana" and "Wheat".

## Two Kinds of Root

Compare several seedlings of each kind. Did they germinate at the same time? Root systems change as seedlings develop, so record the age as well as the shape.

A chana seedling usually develops one main root with smaller side roots. A main root of this kind is a **taproot**. Mustard and radish also develop taproots. The enlarged edible part of a radish includes a swollen taproot that stores food.

As the wheat seedling develops, several thin roots appear. Later, more roots grow from the base of the stem, forming a spreading bunch without one dominant main root. These are called **fibrous roots**. Grass, maize and lemongrass have them.

> **Illustration:** Two young plants shown side by side, each with its roots fully visible below a cut-away soil line: (a) a chana seedling with one thick central root going straight down and thinner side roots branching from it, (b) a wheat seedling with a spreading bunch of thin roots of similar thickness. Label "Taproot" with an arrow to the thick central root, and "Fibrous roots" with an arrow to the bunch. Draw both at the same scale.

Both root systems anchor plants and take up water and dissolved minerals. Their branching patterns differ; their jobs overlap.

A taproot can grow deep and reach water below the surface. A deep root system can help a plant through a dry spell, although root depth also depends on the species and the soil.

Many fibrous root systems spread through the upper soil. A dense network helps hold loose soil together and take up water near the surface. Some fibrous roots also reach deep into the ground.

## The Two Go Together

Pool the class records. Count how many support a connection and how many do not. Compare the result with these common patterns in flowering plants.

Many familiar flowering plants with reticulate venation develop taproots; those with parallel venation commonly develop fibrous root systems.

*Sadabahar* has net-like veins and a taproot. Chana has net-like veins and a taproot. Lemongrass has parallel veins and fibrous roots. Wheat has parallel veins and fibrous roots.

These are typical associations, not rules without exceptions. Age, how a plant was propagated, and unusual features can affect what you observe. Keep exceptions in the record.

## What Is Inside a Seed

A soaked chana can separate into two fleshy seed leaves called **cotyledons**. They store food for the young plant. Between them is the rest of the embryo, including a tiny root and shoot. Compare your specimen with the labelled illustration. Trace each label to the part it names, then locate that part in your specimen if it is visible.

In maize, one thin cotyledon lies beside the embryo’s tiny shoot and root. The larger pale region is stored food called the **endosperm**; it is not a second cotyledon. Failure to split the grain is not itself proof of the cotyledon count.

Cotyledon number, leaf venation and root type are associated features. The comparison does not show that cotyledon number causes the other two.

Among flowering plants, those whose seeds have two cotyledons are called **dicotyledons**. We say **dicots** for short.
Plants whose seeds have one cotyledon are called **monocotyledons**, or **monocots**.

> **Illustration:** Two seeds drawn large and cut open. Left: a soaked chana seed opened into its two halves, hinged apart like a book, with the small root and shoot visible between them. Label "Cotyledons" with arrows pointing to both halves. Right: a maize grain shown in vertical section with its single thin cotyledon picked out in a contrasting tone and labelled. Caption: "Dicot seed (chana)" and "Monocot seed (maize)".

## What Movement Tells Us

Animals move in many ways. They walk, run, hop, crawl, fly, swim and glide. Some do more than one, like a pigeon.

Sorting them this way shows us something interesting. Animals that are not alike at all may still move in the same way, using similar body parts. A housefly and a pigeon are very different creatures. But both have wings, and both fly. A fish and a dolphin look alike in water, even though a dolphin is not a fish.

That is a clue about something you will meet soon in this chapter. The way an animal is built has a lot to do with *where* it lives.

Movement is not the only way to group animals. We can also group them by what they eat, by where they live, or by what covers the body — scales, feathers, fur, or exposed skin. As with plants, the best grouping depends on the question we are asking.

## Built for the Place

Look at a plant from a hot desert.

Consider a cactus adapted to a hot, dry habitat. Its fleshy stem stores water and its waxy surface reduces water loss. In many cacti, leaves are reduced to spines and the green stem carries out photosynthesis.

Now look at a deodar tree, high in the Himalayas.

On snowy mountain slopes, snow can load branches heavily. The conical form and sloping branches of a deodar can help shed snow. Temperature, soil and water also affect where a tree can grow.

## Two Camels, Two Deserts

Compare the two kinds of camel in the illustration. Which visible differences could matter in cold weather?

Camels live in the hot sandy desert of Rajasthan. Camels also live in the cold, high, rocky desert of Ladakh. Both places are deserts. Both are dry, with little food and little shelter. But they are dry in very different ways. And the two camels are not the same.

The Rajasthan camel is tall, with long legs. Its two-toed feet have broad, spreading pads. They spread its weight, so it does not sink into loose sand. It has one hump. Its coat is thin.

The Ladakh camel is shorter and heavier. Its sturdy legs also end in broad, padded, two-toed feet. It has two humps. It grows a thick shaggy coat, especially at the head and neck, for winters that fall far below freezing.

> **Illustration:** Two camels drawn side by side at the same scale for direct comparison. Left: a one-humped camel on sand, long-legged, with a short thin coat — draw an arrow to its broad flat hoof and label it. Right: a two-humped camel on snowy rocky ground, shorter and heavier, with a thick shaggy coat and long hair at the neck — draw an arrow to its smaller, harder hoof and label it. Caption: "Hot desert camel" and "Cold desert camel".

You may have heard that a camel stores water in its hump. That is not true. The hump stores fat. The camel lives off that fat when there is no food. When a camel uses much of this stored fat, its humps can become smaller and less upright.

Camels save water in other ways. They pass very little urine. Their dung is dry enough to burn. They can allow their body temperature to rise during the day, reducing the water needed for cooling. They do sweat when necessary. How long a camel can go without drinking depends on heat, activity and the food available.

## Adaptations

The cactus stem, the deodar's sloping branches, the camel's padded feet and hump — all of these are examples of one idea.

Inherited features that help organisms survive and reproduce in their usual environment are called **adaptations**. They develop in populations over generations; an individual does not acquire a needed feature simply by trying.

Adaptations are not always about extreme places, and they are not always easy to spot. A fish has a smooth body that slips through water easily, and fins to steer and push with. A duck has skin joining its toes. That turns each foot into a paddle. A pigeon's toes are not joined by broad webbing. Its separate toes help it grip supports and walk.

Rhododendrons include low shrubs and tall trees in different mountain regions. Their features reflect both inherited differences and growing conditions. Even plants of the same species may grow differently in a windy, exposed place and in a sheltered one. A change in one plant's growth is not the same as an inherited adaptation developing over generations.

Two plants of the same species grow in different places, one windy and one sheltered. If their heights differ, what other factors would you check before attributing the difference to wind?

## Habitat

The place where a living thing lives is called its **habitat**.

A habitat includes the conditions and resources an organism uses. Plants need light, water and minerals; animals need food as well as suitable conditions. A habitat need not be safe at all times, and an animal may use different places to feed or breed.

A habitat can be small: damp soil beneath a fallen log differs from the dry path beside it. Such differences help explain why nearby places can hold different living things.

Habitats can be sorted into two broad kinds.

Living things on land live in **terrestrial** habitats. Forests, deserts, grasslands and mountains are terrestrial habitats. So is the ground under your feet.

Living things in water live in **aquatic** habitats. Ponds, lakes, rivers and oceans are aquatic habitats.

Some animals use both land and water. Frogs belong to a group called **amphibians**; many amphibians spend part of their lives in each. But using both habitats does not make every animal an amphibian. A crocodile does so too, and it is a reptile.

> **Layout — Know a scientist box:**

**Salim Ali (1896–1987)** spent many years travelling across India, watching birds. He recorded where they lived, when they arrived, and where they went next. Watching carefully like this, year after year, is how scattered sightings turn into real knowledge. His fieldwork and advocacy helped strengthen bird conservation in India. His books also made it possible for ordinary people to name the birds around them. He is remembered as the Birdman of India.

## When a Habitat Is Lost

Ask your parents, your grandparents, or an older neighbour one simple question. Which birds, insects or plants did they see often as children, but rarely see now?

Record what the person remembers and where they lived. Memory is a starting point for a question, not proof of a change in population. Look for dated records or photographs to compare.

Habitat damage can reduce food, shelter or breeding sites. Some organisms may move; others may not be able to reach suitable places. Populations can decline or disappear locally. To investigate a decline, check both changes in habitat and how the observations were made.

Habitat loss has threatened animals such as the Bengal tiger and the Great Indian bustard. Project Tiger began in 1973 to protect tigers and their habitats. The cheetah was declared extinct in India in 1952; a reintroduction programme began in 2022. Protecting suitable habitat is essential to conservation.

Governments and communities both protect habitats. **Sacred groves** are patches of vegetation protected through local cultural or religious traditions. Rules about cutting, collecting and hunting vary. Some groves preserve old trees and habitat missing from nearby cleared land. Some also have legal protection. Find out how a grove near you is protected.

## Summary

- The variety of living things found in a region is called its biodiversity. Even an ordinary place has much more of it than a quick look suggests.
- Living things in a place depend on each other for food, shelter and other needs.
- Grouping means arranging things by the features they share. Different rules answer different questions; each must be applied consistently.
- Woody stems and branching help distinguish trees and shrubs from non-woody herbs. Climbing and trailing describe growth habits, which can overlap with these descriptions.
- The pattern of veins in a leaf is called venation. Two common patterns are reticulate and parallel.
- Two common root systems are taproot and fibrous root systems. A taproot has one main root. Fibrous roots form a spreading bunch.
- Among flowering plants, dicots have two cotyledons and monocots have one.
- These three features go together. Dicots generally have reticulate venation and a taproot. Monocots generally have parallel venation and fibrous roots. These associations support predictions that must be checked.
- Animals can be grouped by how they move and which body parts they use. They can also be grouped in many other ways.
- Adaptations are inherited features that help organisms survive and reproduce. They develop in populations over generations, not because an individual tries to change.
- The place where a living thing lives is its habitat. Habitats are terrestrial or aquatic. Many amphibians use both land and water, but not all animals that do so are amphibians.
- Damage to habitats causes loss of biodiversity. This is why habitats are protected — by governments, and by communities.
