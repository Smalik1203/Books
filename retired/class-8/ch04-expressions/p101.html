Using What You Know

The chapter left you with four things. Brackets can always be removed. Every term of one bracket multiplies every term of the other. Three particular products are worth knowing by heart. And an identity holds for numbers just as well as for letters. Nothing below needs anything else.

What is different is that no question says which of the four to reach for, and several of them look at first as though they need information you have not been given. Try each one before reading what follows it.

Two numbers add to $10$ and multiply to $21$. Without finding either of them, work out $a^2 + b^2$ and $(a - b)^2$.

You are given the sum and the product, and the square of a sum contains both of them: $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$. Rearranged, it says that $a^2 + b^2$ is the square of the sum less twice the product.

$a^2 + b^2$ $= (a+b)^2 - 2ab = 100 - 42 = 58$
$(a-b)^2$ $= a^2 + b^2 - 2ab = 58 - 42 = 16$

So $a - b$ is $4$ or $-4$, and with a sum of $10$ the numbers are $7$ and $3$. Notice that finding them was the last thing that happened, and was not asked for. The two answers came out of the two given numbers directly.

Find $49 \times 51$ in your head. Then find $4.9 \times 5.1$, and say what changed and what did not.

$49$ and $51$ are each one away from $50$, so their product is a difference of two squares with $a = 50$ and $b = 1$.

$49 \times 51$ $= (50-1)(50+1) = 2500 - 1 = 2499$
$4.9 \times 5.1$ $= (5 - 0.1)(5 + 0.1) = 25 - 0.01 = 24.99$

What changed is only the place of the decimal point. Each factor was divided by ten, so the product was divided by a hundred, and $2499$ became $24.99$. What did not change is the shape of the calculation: the identity does not care whether $a$ and $b$ are whole numbers.
