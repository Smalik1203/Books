Show that $x^2 - 6x + 10$ is greater than zero for every value of $x$, including the negative ones and the fractions.

Trying values will not settle this: there are infinitely many to try. What settles it is finding a square, because a square is never negative.

The first two terms, $x^2 - 6x$, are the beginning of $(x-3)^2 = x^2 - 6x + 9$. So write the $10$ as $9 + 1$.

rewrite $x^2 - 6x + 10 = (x^2 - 6x + 9) + 1$
as a square $= (x-3)^2 + 1$

Whatever $x$ is, $(x-3)^2$ is zero or more, so the whole thing is $1$ or more, and it is never zero. It is smallest when $x = 3$, which the original form does not show at all. Making a square appear where none was visible is the single most useful thing the identities do.

A student writes: $(x + y)^2$ is always bigger than $4xy$. The statement is nearly right. Find what is wrong with it, and say exactly when the two are equal.

Move everything to one side and the claim becomes a statement about $x^2 - 2xy + y^2$, which is a square.

difference $(x + y)^2 - 4xy = x^2 - 2xy + y^2 = (x-y)^2$
so $(x + y)^2 \geq 4xy$ always

A square is never negative, so $(x + y)^2$ is never less than $4xy$. But a square can be zero, and $(x-y)^2$ is zero exactly when $x = y$: at $x = y = 5$ both sides come to $100$. So the claim should read at least rather than bigger than — and notice that disproving it took one value, while proving the corrected version took an argument.

Multiply four consecutive whole numbers and add $1$. Try it with $3, 4, 5, 6$ and then with $7, 8, 9, 10$. Then show that the answer is always a perfect square.

The arithmetic first: $3 \times 4 \times 5 \times 6 = 360$, and $361 = 19^2$. Then $7 \times 8 \times 9 \times 10 = 5040$, and $5041 = 71^2$. Twice is not a proof, and the proof is in the choice of which pair to multiply first.

Call the numbers $n$, $n+1$, $n+2$, $n+3$. Pair the outer two and the inner two.

outer pair $n(n+3) = n^2 + 3n$
inner pair $(n+1)(n+2) = n^2 + 3n + 2$

The two differ by exactly $2$. Writing $m$ for $n^2 + 3n$, the product is $m(m+2)$, and adding one gives $m^2 + 2m + 1$, which is $(m+1)^2$.
