6.6When the Two Lines Are Parallel

Two lines in a plane that never meet, however far they are produced either way, are called parallel. We write $m \parallel n$. The definition is a statement about the whole infinite length of both lines, and that is exactly why it cannot be checked by looking. You can see that two lines have not met yet, but not that they never will.

So we need some property of parallel lines that can be checked locally, near a crossing, and that is what the transversal gives us. Draw two parallel lines and a transversal, and measure a pair of corresponding angles. Do it again with a different pair of parallels, a different transversal. Corresponding angles come out equal every time.

This one we do not prove. It is taken as the starting point: an axiom, a statement assumed rather than deduced. Something has to be assumed about parallel lines before anything can be deduced about them, and this is the cleanest thing to assume.

The Corresponding Angles Axiom

If a transversal cuts two parallel lines, each pair of corresponding angles is equal.

And conversely: if a transversal cuts two lines so that a pair of corresponding angles is equal, the two lines are parallel.

Take a moment over the second half. It is the useful one. The first half tells you what parallel lines do. The second is the only way you will ever establish that two lines are parallel, since you cannot follow them to infinity to check. Every proof of parallelism in this book, and in the chapters on quadrilaterals to come, ends by producing a pair of equal corresponding angles — or something that leads to one.

Everything else about parallels now follows. We prove the two main consequences.

Alternate Interior Angles

If a transversal cuts two parallel lines, each pair of alternate interior angles is equal.

In Fig. 6.6, take $m \parallel n$ and prove $\angle 4 = \angle 6$.

1 $\angle 2 = \angle 6$ corresponding, $m \parallel n$
2 $\angle 2 = \angle 4$ vertically opposite at $P$
3 $\angle 4 = \angle 6$ both equal $\angle 2$

Two facts, one about parallels and one about crossings, and the result drops out. Notice that the second line of the proof does not use the parallels at all — vertically opposite angles are equal whatever the lines are doing. Only the first line spends the axiom.
