What a family shares is a direction. That is why it is possible to speak of the direction of a line without saying where the line is. It is also why, on a page of ruled paper, you need to check only one ruling against your set square to know about all of them.

Example 7

In Fig. 6.8, $AB \parallel CD$. A point $E$ lies between the two lines, and $\angle BAE = 44^\circ$, $\angle ECD = 31^\circ$. Find $\angle AEC$.

As it stands there is nothing to work with: $\angle AEC$ is not part of any transversal figure. So make one.

Solution.

Step 1 through $E$ draw the line $k$ parallel to $AB$, and so parallel to $CD$ as well Section 6.7
Step 2 $AE$ is a transversal of $AB$ and $k$; $CE$ is a transversal of $CD$ and $k$
Step 3 $\angle AEk = 44^\circ$ alternate interior, $AB \parallel k$
Step 4 $\angle kEC = 31^\circ$ alternate interior, $CD \parallel k$
Step 5 $\angle AEC = 44^\circ + 31^\circ$ the ray $Ek$ lies inside
Answer $\angle AEC = 75^\circ$

The single line $k$ has turned one hard figure into two easy ones. Adding a line that was not given is called a construction, and it is the single most useful move in geometry. Nothing forced us to draw $k$; we drew it because it made the transversal facts apply.

Think and Reflect
?
  1. In Example 7, suppose $E$ were moved so that $\angle AEC$ became a reflex angle. Would the construction still work? Draw it and see what its Step 5 would have to become.
  2. A transversal cuts two parallel lines at right angles. What are all eight angles? Which of the four named pairs are still telling you anything new?
In the second, when every angle is $90^\circ$ the theorems are all still true — they have simply stopped being informative. That is worth noticing about theorems in general.
