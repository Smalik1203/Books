## 6.4 Where Two Lines Cross

### Think and Reflect

1. The angle opposite the $90^\circ$ one is $90^\circ$ (vertically
   opposite), and each of its neighbours is $180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$
   (linear pair). All four are right angles, so the two lines are
   perpendicular.
2. **No.** Two of the four angles at a crossing are either vertically
   opposite, and so equal, or neighbours, and so add to $180^\circ$. But
   $70^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ are not equal, and $70 + 120 = 190$, not $180$.

### Exercise Set 6.1

1. - (i) $35^\circ$: complement $55^\circ$, supplement $145^\circ$
   - (ii) $72^\circ$: complement $18^\circ$, supplement $108^\circ$
   - (iii) $90^\circ$: no complement (it would be $0^\circ$), supplement $90^\circ$
   - (iv) $116^\circ$: no complement, since it is already more than
     $90^\circ$; supplement $64^\circ$

2. $x + x = 180^\circ$ gives $x = 90^\circ$. An angle equal to its own
   complement: $y + y = 90^\circ$, so **yes**, $y = 45^\circ$.

3. $(2x + 15) + (3x - 25) = 180$, so $5x - 10 = 180$ and $x = 38$. The
   angles are $91^\circ$ and $89^\circ$.

4. - (i) **False.** Supplementary is a fact about two numbers:
     $30^\circ$ in one figure and $150^\circ$ in another are supplementary
     and not adjacent.
   - (ii) **True.** That is the linear pair property.
   - (iii) **False.** Two obtuse angles add to more than $180^\circ$.
   - (iv) **True.** The proof uses only the two linear pairs, whatever the
     angle of crossing.

5. $2 + 3 + 7 = 12$ parts make $360^\circ$, so one part is $30^\circ$. The
   angles are $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$ and $210^\circ$.

6. Two neighbouring angles add to $180^\circ$, not $200^\circ$, so the two
   must be vertically opposite, and so equal: $100^\circ$ each. The other two
   are $180^\circ - 100^\circ = 80^\circ$ each. The four angles are
   $100^\circ$, $80^\circ$, $100^\circ$, $80^\circ$.

## 6.6 When the Two Lines Are Parallel

### Think and Reflect

1. **Yes, the construction still works.** Move $E$ to the other side of
   $A$ and $C$. The line $k$ through $E$ is still parallel to both lines, and
   $\angle AEk$ and $\angle kEC$ are still alternate to $\angle BAE$ and
   $\angle ECD$, which are now obtuse. The ray $Ek$ that points towards $B$
   and $D$ now lies inside the reflex angle, so Step 5 becomes
   *reflex* $\angle AEC = \angle BAE + \angle ECD$. The sum is now more than
   $180^\circ$, as a reflex angle must be.
2. Every one of the eight angles is $90^\circ$. The four named pairs are all
   still true — corresponding, alternate and co-interior angles are all
   $90^\circ$ — but none tells you anything new, since every angle is equal
   to, and supplementary to, every other.

### Exercise Set 6.2

1. Three more angles are $73^\circ$: the angle vertically opposite it, and
   the corresponding and alternate angles at the other crossing. The other
   four are $180^\circ - 73^\circ = 107^\circ$, each a linear pair with a
   $73^\circ$ angle.

2. The lines are parallel when the alternate interior angles are equal:
   $4x - 6 = 2x + 30$, so $x = 18$, and each angle is $66^\circ$.

3. - (i) **Yes.** Each alternate exterior angle is vertically opposite an
     alternate interior angle, so equal alternate exterior angles give equal
     alternate interior angles, and those make the lines parallel.
   - (ii) **Not by itself.** Parallel lines need the co-interior pair to add
     to $180^\circ$. Two equal angles do that only when each is $90^\circ$;
     equal angles of $70^\circ$, say, add to $140^\circ$, and the lines meet.
   - (iii) **Yes.** Supplementary co-interior angles make the lines
     parallel.
   - (iv) **Yes.** Take the line both are perpendicular to as the
     transversal. The corresponding angles are both $90^\circ$, so the lines
     are parallel.

4. Draw through $T$ the line parallel to $PQ$, which is parallel to $RS$ as
   well. It cuts $\angle PTS$ into a part alternate to $\angle QPT$ and a part
   alternate to $\angle TSR$, so $\angle PTS = \angle QPT + \angle TSR$ and
   $\angle TSR = 137^\circ - 52^\circ = 85^\circ$.

5. $a$ and $b$ are both parallel to $c$, so $a \parallel b$ (Section 6.7).
   The transversal therefore makes the same corresponding angle with $b$:
   $64^\circ$ (and $116^\circ$ on the other side of it).

6. Let the co-interior angles be $2x$ and $2y$, and let their bisectors meet
   at $O$.
   - $2x + 2y = 180^\circ$ — co-interior angles, parallel lines
   - $x + y = 90^\circ$ — halving
   - the bisectors and the transversal form a triangle with angles $x$ and
     $y$ at the parallel lines
   - its third angle is $180^\circ - (x + y) = 90^\circ$ — angle sum

   So the bisectors meet at right angles.

## 6.8 The Angles of a Triangle

### Think and Reflect

1. The three exterior angles add to $360^\circ$. Each exterior angle makes a
   linear pair with the interior angle at its vertex, so the three are
   $180^\circ - \angle A$, $180^\circ - \angle B$ and $180^\circ - \angle C$.
   Their sum is $540^\circ - (\angle A + \angle B + \angle C)$, which is
   $540^\circ - 180^\circ = 360^\circ$.
2. $\angle QOR = 90^\circ + \tfrac{1}{2}\angle P$ falls as $\angle P$ falls,
   towards $90^\circ$. It never reaches $90^\circ$, since $\angle P$ is never
   $0$.

### Exercise Set 6.3

1. The third angle is $180^\circ - 54^\circ - 67^\circ = 59^\circ$. The
   exterior angles are $126^\circ$, $113^\circ$ and $121^\circ$.

2. $2 + 3 + 4 = 9$ parts make $180^\circ$, so one part is $20^\circ$. The
   angles are $40^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $80^\circ$; all are acute, so the
   triangle is acute-angled.

3. Call the first angle $x$. The second is $\tfrac{1}{2}x$ and the third is
   $x - 30$. Then $x + \tfrac{1}{2}x + x - 30 = 180$, so $x = 84$. The
   angles are $84^\circ$, $42^\circ$ and $54^\circ$.

4. **No.** The exterior angles of any triangle add to $360^\circ$, and
   $100 + 120 + 150 = 370$. (The interior angles would be $80^\circ$,
   $60^\circ$ and $30^\circ$, which add to only $170^\circ$.)

5. $\angle A = 118^\circ - 62^\circ = 56^\circ$ (exterior angle theorem), and
   $\angle ACB = 180^\circ - 118^\circ = 62^\circ$ (linear pair). So
   $\angle B = \angle ACB$: the triangle has two equal angles.

6. The exterior angle is the sum of the two interior opposite angles, so the
   other one would be $105^\circ - 105^\circ = 0^\circ$. A triangle has no
   angle of $0^\circ$, so this is impossible.

## End-of-Chapter Exercises

1. $x = (90 - x) - 24$, so $2x = 66$ and the angle is $33^\circ$.

2. $(5x - 8) + (3x + 12) = 180$, so $8x + 4 = 180$ and $x = 22$. The angles
   are $102^\circ$ and $78^\circ$.

3. **Yes, one of them.** The $38^\circ$ angle lies between two of the
   lines, and where those two lines cross, the angle vertically opposite it
   is also $38^\circ$. The other four depend on where the third line runs.

4. **Flagged.** Read with the usual lettering (A and C to the left of the
   transversal, B and D to the right), $\angle AGH$ and $\angle GHD$ are
   alternate interior angles and must be equal, so a ratio of $5 : 4$ is
   impossible. The answer the chapter intends takes them as co-interior
   (A and D on the same side of $EF$): $5k + 4k = 180$, so $k = 20$, and the
   angles are $100^\circ$ and $80^\circ$. The question needs a figure or a
   corrected letter.

5. Let $\angle ABC$ and $\angle DEF$ have $BA \parallel ED$ and
   $BC \parallel EF$, in the same sense. Produce $ED$ to meet $BC$ at $G$.
   - $\angle ABC = \angle DGC$ — corresponding, $BA \parallel GD$,
     transversal $BC$
   - $\angle DGC = \angle DEF$ — corresponding, $GC \parallel EF$,
     transversal $GE$
   - so $\angle ABC = \angle DEF$

6. Let the corresponding angles be $2x$ at each crossing, with bisectors
   making $x$ with the transversal.
   - $2x = 2x$ — corresponding angles, parallel lines
   - each bisector makes the angle $x$ with the transversal, on the same
     side and in the same position
   - these are equal corresponding angles for the two bisectors, so the
     bisectors are parallel — converse of the axiom

7. $\angle A = \angle B + 15$ and $\angle C = \angle B - 25$. Then
   $3\angle B - 10 = 180$, so $\angle B = 63\tfrac{1}{3}^\circ$,
   $\angle A = 78\tfrac{1}{3}^\circ$ and $\angle C = 38\tfrac{1}{3}^\circ$.
   The angles are not whole numbers; the arithmetic is right.

8. The ladder, the wall and the ground make a triangle with a right angle at
   the foot of the wall. The ladder makes $180^\circ - 90^\circ - 63^\circ = 27^\circ$
   with the wall, by the angle sum of a triangle.

9. $\angle A = 180^\circ - 70^\circ - 50^\circ = 60^\circ$, so
   $\angle BAD = \angle DAC = 30^\circ$. In triangle $ABD$,
   $\angle ADB = 180^\circ - 70^\circ - 30^\circ = 80^\circ$; in triangle
   $ADC$, $\angle ADC = 180^\circ - 50^\circ - 30^\circ = 100^\circ$. And
   $80 + 100 = 180$, a linear pair.

10. **Not parallel**, since $87 + 91 = 178$, not $180$. They meet on the side
    of the transversal where these two angles are: a triangle there would
    have a third angle of $2^\circ$, while on the other side the two angles
    add to $360 - 178 = 182$, more than a triangle allows.

11. The exterior angle at $B$ is $180^\circ - \angle B$, so its half is
    $90^\circ - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle B$; likewise at $C$.
    - in triangle $BOC$: $\angle BOC = 180^\circ - (90^\circ - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle B) - (90^\circ - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle C)$
    - so $\angle BOC = \tfrac{1}{2}(\angle B + \angle C)$
    - and $\angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ - \angle A$ — angle sum
    - so $\angle BOC = 90^\circ - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle A$

    Example 10 gives $90^\circ + \tfrac{1}{2}\angle A$ for the interior
    bisectors. The two answers add to $180^\circ$: at $B$ (and at $C$) the
    interior and exterior bisectors are at right angles, so the
    quadrilateral they make has two right angles, and its other two angles
    make up the remaining $180^\circ$.

12. $174 < 180$, so the lines are not parallel, and two lines in a plane
    that are not parallel meet. If they met on that side, the triangle
    formed would have angles adding to $174^\circ$ at the transversal and a
    third angle of $6^\circ$ — possible. On the other side the two angles
    add to $360 - 174 = 186$, more than $180^\circ$, so no triangle can
    close there. They meet on the $174^\circ$ side.

13. **Ten.** Let the smallest angle be $s$, the largest $2s$ and the middle
    one $m$, with $s \le m \le 2s$. From $s + m + 2s = 180$,
    $m = 180 - 3s$. Then $s \le 180 - 3s$ gives $s \le 45$, and
    $180 - 3s \le 2s$ gives $s \ge 36$. So $s$ is one of
    $36, 37, \ldots, 45$: ten triangles, from $36^\circ, 72^\circ, 72^\circ$
    to $45^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ$.

14. The proof is Exercise Set 6.2 Q6's, word for word: the two halves add to
    $90^\circ$ because co-interior angles between parallels are
    supplementary. Both rest on that property. **Flagged**: the two
    questions are the same, so the comparison it asks for has nothing to
    find.

15. Call the angles $a - d$, $a$ and $a + d$. Their sum is $3a = 180$, so
    the middle one is $a = 60^\circ$, whatever $d$ is.

16. If the co-interior angles add to $180^\circ$, the lines are parallel.
    Between parallel lines alternate interior angles are equal, so they
    cannot differ by $20^\circ$. The two conditions contradict each other.

17. Going round the point the six angles are $a$, $b$, $c$, $a$, $b$, $c$,
    since each is vertically opposite the one three places on. So
    $2(a + b + c) = 360^\circ$ and $a + b + c = 180^\circ$. The first,
    third and fifth are $a$, $c$ and $b$: $180^\circ$.

    With four lines the eight angles are $a, b, c, d, a, b, c, d$, and
    $a + b + c + d = 180^\circ$. The first, third, fifth and seventh add to
    $2(a + c)$, which is $180^\circ$ only when $a + c = 90^\circ$. **Not in
    general**: lines at $0^\circ$, $30^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$
    give angles $30^\circ, 30^\circ, 30^\circ, 90^\circ, \ldots$, and
    $2(30 + 30) = 120$. What does hold is that any four angles in a row add
    to $180^\circ$.
