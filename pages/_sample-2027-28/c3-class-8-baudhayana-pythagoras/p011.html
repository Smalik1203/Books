Nobody ever found that proof. Mathematicians tried for three hundred years and failed, and the claim came to be called Fermat's Last Theorem. For most of that time it was not a theorem at all. It was a guess that nobody could prove true or false.

In 1963 a ten-year-old in England named Andrew Wiles read about it in a library book, and decided he would be the one to prove it. He proved it in 1994, at forty-one, using mathematics that had not existed when he was ten.

Think and Reflect

A square can be split into two squares in infinitely many ways. A cube cannot be split into two cubes at all.

A single example of $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$ would have answered Fermat's question for ever. Why did three hundred years without finding one not answer it?

The start and the end of this chapter teach one lesson. Baudhāyana's rule was proved in one paragraph by cutting a square into four triangles, and it has been used for twenty-eight centuries. Fermat's question looked similar, but it took three hundred years. You cannot tell which questions are easy just by looking at them. Here the difference is a picture: two squares can be cut up and laid over a third, but there is no such picture for cubes.
