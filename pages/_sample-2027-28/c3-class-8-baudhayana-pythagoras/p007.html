Example 1

A right-angled triangle has short sides $9$ cm and $12$ cm. Find its hypotenuse.

Solution.

Step 1 $c^2 = 9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225$ the theorem
Step 2 $c = \sqrt{225} = 15$ cm take the square root
Answer The hypotenuse is $15$ cm.

Check quickly: $15$ is longer than both $9$ and $12$, as the hypotenuse must be, and shorter than $9 + 12$, as any side of a triangle must be.

Example 2

A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse $41$ m and one short side $40$ m. Find the other.

Solution.

Step 1 $40^2 + b^2 = 41^2$ the theorem
Step 2 $b^2 = 1681 - 1600 = 81$ subtract the known square
Step 3 $b = 9$ m take the square root
Answer The other short side is $9$ m.

Here the hypotenuse went on the right of the equals sign, so the working became a subtraction. Adding $40^2$ and $41^2$ would have been a mistake.

2.6.1 Is this triangle right-angled?

The theorem also works the other way round, and that is the part the altar builders used. If the three sides of a triangle make $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ true, then the angle opposite the longest side is a right angle. So you can test whether a triangle has a right angle using only a tape measure and some arithmetic.

$13$, $84$, $85$ $169 + 7056 = 7225 = 85^2$ — right-angled
$9$, $12$, $16$ $81 + 144 = 225$, but $16^2 = 256$ — not right-angled

The second was chosen to be close: $9$, $12$, $15$ would give a right angle. Making the longest side one centimetre longer makes that angle bigger than $90^\circ$.
