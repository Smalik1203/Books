2.7Triples

Most right-angled triangles have a side that is not a whole number. Two sides of $1$ give a hypotenuse of $\sqrt{2}$; two sides of $1$ and $2$ give $\sqrt5$. So triangles whose three sides are all whole numbers are useful, and the Śulbasūtra lists several.

$(3, 4, 5)$ $9 + 16 = 25$
$(5, 12, 13)$ $25 + 144 = 169$
$(8, 15, 17)$ $64 + 225 = 289$
$(7, 24, 25)$ $49 + 576 = 625$
$(12, 35, 37)$ $144 + 1225 = 1369$
$(15, 36, 39)$ $225 + 1296 = 1521$

Three whole numbers with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ are called a Baudhāyana triple, and also a Pythagorean triple.

Look at the list again. One entry is different from the others. Divide $(15, 36, 39)$ by $3$ and it becomes $(5, 12, 13)$, which is already there: the same triangle, three times as large.

This gives a rule, and the rule turns a few triples into as many as you like. If $(a, b, c)$ is a triple, so is $(ka, kb, kc)$ for any whole number $k$:

work it out $(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2a^2 + k^2b^2 = k^2(a^2 + b^2)$
and $a^2+b^2 = c^2$ $= k^2c^2 = (kc)^2$
