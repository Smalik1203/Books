The shape left in the middle is a square. Its four sides are all hypotenuses, so they are equal. Its corners are right angles too. Where two triangles meet on a side of the big square, the two angles there are the triangle's two acute angles. These add to $90^\circ$, so $90^\circ$ is left for the corner between them.

Now find the area of the big square in two ways.

as one square $(a+b)^2$
as its pieces $4 \times \tfrac12 ab + c^2 = 2ab + c^2$

Both are the area of the same square, so they are equal. In Chapter 6 of Part I, we cut a square into four to find what $(a+b)^2$ equals:

$$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \;=\; 2ab + c^2$$

Take $2ab$ away from both sides. What is left is the theorem.

The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem

In a right-angled triangle with short sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, $\ a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

The square on the hypotenuse is the two squares on the other sides together. It is named after Baudhāyana, who stated it around 800 BCE, and Pythagoras, who stated it in Greece about three hundred years later.

In the picture, the theorem is about paper: cut up the two smaller squares, and the pieces will exactly cover the larger one. The algebra says the same thing without scissors.

2.6Finding a Side You Cannot Reach

The theorem is mostly used to find a missing side. It links three lengths, so if you know any two, you can find the third. Often the third is a length you cannot measure with a tape.

Before doing any arithmetic, decide which side is the hypotenuse. It is the side opposite the right angle, and it is always the longest of the three. If you know the hypotenuse, subtract. If you are looking for it, add.
