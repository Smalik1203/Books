So $(3,4,5)$ alone gives $(6,8,10)$, $(9,12,15)$, $(30,40,50)$ and as many more as you like: there is no last Baudhāyana triple. A triple whose three numbers have no common factor is called primitive. It is not a larger copy of a smaller triple. Five of Baudhāyana's six are primitive, and the sixth is $(5,12,13)$ multiplied by $3$.

Where do the primitive ones come from? Take any two whole numbers $m$ and $n$ with $m$ the larger, and build three numbers from them.

A machine for making triples

$m^2 - n^2$, $\ 2mn$, $\ m^2 + n^2$ is a Baudhāyana triple for every choice of $m > n$. With $m=2, n=1$ it makes $(3, 4, 5)$; with $m=3, n=2$, $\ (5, 12, 13)$; with $m=4, n=1$, $\ (15, 8, 17)$; with $m=4, n=3$, $\ (7, 24, 25)$; and with $m=6, n=1$, $\ (35, 12, 37)$.

One rule gives every primitive triple on Baudhāyana's list. And the rule can be checked in three lines with the identities from Part I:

$$(m^2-n^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 = m^4 - 2m^2n^2 + n^4 + 4m^2n^2 = m^4 + 2m^2n^2 + n^4 = (m^2+n^2)^2.$$

2.8One Question, Three Hundred Years

There are infinitely many triples of whole numbers whose squares add to a square. In the 1630s the French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat, reading a book about exactly these triples, asked the next question: could it be done with cubes? Are there whole numbers with $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$? With fourth powers? With hundredth powers?

He believed the answer was no. He said that
