7.4A Pinch of History

In ancient India, writing expressions with symbols and solving equations made from them was an important part of mathematics. This area was called bijaganita, and today we call it algebra. Ganita means calculation, so bijaganita is calculation with seeds. The word bija means seed. A whole tree is hidden inside a seed, as the answer to a problem is hidden inside an unknown number.

We have already met Brahmagupta’s work on integers and fractions. In Chapter 18 of his book Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628 CE), he also explained how to add, subtract and multiply unknown numbers written as letters, much as we use $x$ or $y$ today. This is one of the earliest known works on algebra anywhere in the world.

In the 8th century, Indian mathematical ideas were translated into Arabic. They influenced a well-known mathematician, Al-Khwarizmi, who lived in what is now Iraq. Around 825 CE he wrote a book called Hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala, which means “calculation by restoring and balancing”. Restoring meant removing a subtracted term from one side of an equation by adding it to both sides. Balancing meant taking away equal terms from both sides. These are the moves we used in Section 7.2.

These ideas spread further. By the 12th century, Al-Khwarizmi’s book had been translated into Latin and taken to Europe, where it became very popular. The word al-jabr in its title gave us the word algebra, which we still use today.

Today we use letters of the alphabet for unknowns. From the time of Brahmagupta, Indian mathematicians used short syllables such as ya, ka, ni, pi and lo for different unknowns. Ya was short for yavat-tavat, meaning “as much as needed”. Ka and ni began the names of colours: kalaka (black) and nilaka (blue). A known number, rupa, was marked ru. So ya 2 means two of the unknown, which we write as $2x$, and ru 1 means the known number 1.
