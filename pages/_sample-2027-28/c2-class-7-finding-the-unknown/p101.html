Using What You Know

The chapter showed you how to keep the two sides of an equation equal while you change it, and how to turn a problem into an equation. What changes now is that a question may not ask for the unknown at all, or may not look like an equation.

Given that $5a - 3 = 17$, what is the value of $10a + 4$?

Adding 3 to both sides gives $5a = 20$. Multiplying both sides by 2 gives $10a = 40$, and adding 4 gives $10a + 4 = 44$. We could have found that $a = 4$ first, but we did not need to: $10a$ is just twice $5a$.

Asha thinks of a number, adds 7 to it, and multiplies the result by 3. She gets 45. Meena says the number is 8, because $45 \div 3 = 15$ and $15 - 7 = 8$. Is Meena right? Why does her way work?

Let the number be $x$. Then $3(x + 7) = 45$. Dividing both sides by 3 gives $x + 7 = 15$, and subtracting 7 from both sides gives $x = 8$. So Meena is right. Her way undoes Asha’s steps in reverse order: the last step, multiplying by 3, is undone first. Solving the equation does exactly the same thing.

Can the equation $2(x + 3) = 2x + 5$ have a solution?

Opening the bracket gives $2x + 6 = 2x + 5$. Subtracting $2x$ from both sides leaves $6 = 5$, which is false whatever $x$ is. So no value of $x$ makes the two sides equal, and the equation has no solution. If the RHS had been $2x + 6$, every value of $x$ would have been a solution.
