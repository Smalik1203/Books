Then came the idea this chapter needs most. Brahmagupta (c. 628 CE) treated zero and the negative numbers as real numbers. In a modern coordinate system the origin is zero, and the negative axes hold the numbers below zero. Without negative numbers, we would have only a quarter of the plane.

His work was translated into Arabic as the Sindhind, and it took the Ujjayinī meridian with it. In Arabic geography this became ‘Arin’, the zero of longitude for early Arabic maps, and the negative numbers went with it. Al-Bīrūnī (c. 1000 CE) came to India, studied the Siddhāntas, and used Indian trigonometric methods to fix the coordinates of cities across Asia. He also improved the astrolabe, a hand-held instrument for finding a ship’s position from the stars. Ömar Khayyām (c. 1100 CE), who knew Indian algebra and the decimal system, was the first to solve algebra problems with geometry, using coordinates in a plane.

These ideas reached Europe in the 12th century. The last step was taken by René Descartes (1637 CE), after work by Fermat (1636 CE). He saw that any point of a flat surface is fixed by just two numbers: its distances from two perpendicular lines. Shapes could now be written as equations, and equations drawn as shapes. Geometry and algebra were joined.

The name of the plane also comes from him. Descartes wrote in Latin as Renatus Cartesius, and the word Cartesian comes from Cartesius.

But Descartes did not draw the diagram you see in this chapter. He drew one axis and measured from it. He did not require the second axis to be perpendicular, and he did not use negative coordinates at all. Over the next hundred years, other mathematicians added the two axes at right angles, the four quadrants and their signs. In this way they finally used the negative numbers that Brahmagupta had described a thousand years before.

Two numbers, two directions and one fixed starting point: everything in this chapter is built from these three things.
