Now the general rule. The coordinates of a point $P$ in 2-D space are written $(x, y)$, where $x$ is the perpendicular distance of $P$ from the y-axis, measured along the x-axis, and $y$ is the perpendicular distance of $P$ from the x-axis, measured along the y-axis. We call $x$ the x-coordinate and $y$ the y-coordinate of the point $(x, y)$.

Try it on Fig. 1.4. The point $S (3, -5)$ lies in Quadrant IV, with x-coordinate 3 and y-coordinate $-5$; the point $Q (-5, 3)$ lies in Quadrant II, with x-coordinate $-5$ and y-coordinate 3. The same two numbers, in the other order, give a completely different point.

Copy Fig. 1.4 and mark $S$ and $Q$ on your own diagram. Then add a point $P$ anywhere in Quadrant I and a point $R$ anywhere in Quadrant III, and write down their coordinates.

The bracket $(x, y)$ is an ordered pair, and the word ordered is important. Two numbers on their own are just a pair. $(3, -5)$ names a point because we always read the first number along the x-axis and the second along the y-axis. If we changed that rule, every point in the chapter would move.

The two numbers also have older names, which are still used. The x-coordinate is the abscissa, from the Latin for cut off, because it is the length cut off along the horizontal axis. The y-coordinate is the ordinate. These words were in use before anyone wrote the two numbers side by side in a bracket.

Look carefully at what the definition measures. The abscissa of $P$ is its distance from the y-axis, not from the x-axis, and the ordinate is its distance from the x-axis. This seems backwards at first. But when you move in the direction of the x-axis, you start from the y-axis, so the distance you cover is measured from the y-axis, the line you started on.

So the system is complete. Every point of the plane has exactly one pair of coordinates, and every pair of numbers names exactly one point. No point is without a pair, and no pair is without a point. Because of this, any question about shapes can be written as a question about numbers.
