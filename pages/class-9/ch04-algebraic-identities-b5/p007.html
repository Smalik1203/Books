Back to the pattern

With both identities in hand, return to the very first page of this chapter. Three consecutive square numbers, add the outer two, subtract twice the middle one — and the answer was always 2. Arithmetic confirmed it again and again but could never say why. Algebra can, and it takes one line.

The move that unlocks it is choosing how to name the numbers. Three consecutive whole numbers are not $n$, $n + 1$, $n + 2$; they are more usefully written with the middle one at the centre:

$$n - 1, \qquad n, \qquad n + 1$$

Now the outer two squares are $(n - 1)^2$ and $(n + 1)^2$ — one needing each identity. Add them:

$(n - 1)^2 + (n + 1)^2 = \left(n^2 - 2n + 1\right) + \left(n^2 + 2n + 1\right)$

$= 2n^2 + 2$

Watch what happened to the middle terms: $-2n$ from the first bracket and $+2n$ from the second cancel exactly. That cancellation is the whole secret of the pattern. Now subtract twice the middle square, $2n^2$:

$$\left(2n^2 + 2\right) - 2n^2 = 2$$

The $n$ has vanished. Whatever number you started from — 4, or 40, or four million — the answer is 2, because nothing in the working depended on which $n$ you chose. That is a proof: the thing no amount of checking could have given you.

Naming the numbers well did most of the work. Had you written them as $n$, $n + 1$, $n + 2$, the answer would still be 2 — but the cancellation would have been far harder to see.

The second identity earns its keep the same way the first did. Where $(a + b)^2$ lets you square a number by splitting it into a round part and a small one, $(a - b)^2$ does the same job for a number sitting just below a round one — which is often the easier split of the two.
