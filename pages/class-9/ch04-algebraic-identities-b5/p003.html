The four pieces make up the whole square, so their areas must add to its area:

$$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$

But look at what that argument used: a picture of lengths. Every quantity in it had to be positive — you cannot draw a square whose side is $-2$ units. So the drawing proves the identity for positive $a$ and $b$, and nothing more. Does it survive other kinds of number?

Example 2

Take two negative numbers: $a = -2$ and $b = -3$.

$(a + b)^2$ $= (-5)^2 =$ 25
$a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ $= 4 + 12 + 9 =$ 25

The two sides agree. Now try a pair of rational numbers, say $a = -\frac{2}{3}$ and $b = \frac{3}{4}$. Then $a + b = \frac{-8 + 9}{12} = \frac{1}{12}$, so the left-hand side is $\frac{1}{144}$. The right-hand side gives

$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = \dfrac{4}{9} - 1 + \dfrac{9}{16} = \dfrac{64 - 144 + 81}{144} = \dfrac{1}{144}$

Agreement again — but two successes prove nothing. You met that limit on the first page, where arithmetic kept confirming a pattern it could not explain. What settles it is algebra: multiply the bracket out.

$(a + b)^2 = (a + b)(a + b)$

$= a(a + b) + b(a + b)$

$= a^2 + ab + ba + b^2$

$= a^2 + 2ab + b^2$

No particular numbers appear in that chain, so it cannot fail for an awkward choice. That is what earns an equation the name identity.

Algebraic identity

An algebraic identity is an equation that is true for every value of the variables occurring in it. An ordinary equation need not be: $x + 5 = 12$ is true only when $x = 7$.
