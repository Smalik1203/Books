Special cases and generalisation in mathematics

The idea of a special case comes up often in mathematics. A special case comes from a general result when we add an extra condition. The general result is then called a generalisation of the special case. Generalising is a very important part of mathematics. Here are a few examples.

A square is a special case of a rectangle. So the formula for the area of a square, $A = a^2$, is a special case of the formula for the area of a rectangle, $A = ab$: put $b = a$. In the same way, the formula for the perimeter of a square is a special case of the formula for the perimeter of a rectangle.

An isosceles right-angled triangle is a special case of a right-angled triangle. So from any theorem about all right-angled triangles, we can take out a special case for isosceles right-angled triangles. Take a right-angled triangle $ABC$ with $\angle C = 90^\circ$. Then $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, by the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem. The special case $a = b$ is the isosceles triangle, and it gives $c^2 = 2a^2$, so $c = a\sqrt{2}$. The general theorem, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, has become the special case $c = a\sqrt{2}$ for isosceles right-angled triangles.

Here is another example. Compare these two identities from algebra:

$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab$

$(a + b + c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca$

Can you see that the first identity is a special case of the second — put $c = 0$ in the second — and that the second is a generalisation of the first? You will meet many more special cases and generalisations in later years.

Brahmagupta’s formula generalises Heron’s formula

Heron’s formula and Brahmagupta’s formula look alike, and that is no accident. Brahmagupta’s formula generalises Heron’s formula: Heron’s formula is a special case of Brahmagupta’s.

A triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$ can be thought of as a 4-gon $ABCD$ whose fourth side has length zero, $d = 0$. We have $AB = a$, $BC = b$, $CD = c$, and the vertices $A$ and $D$ are the same point. (If the vertices were planets, this would be two planets colliding and becoming one large planet!)

Every triangle is cyclic, because we can draw a circle through any three points that are not in a straight line. So this 4-gon is cyclic too, and Brahmagupta’s formula must apply to it. Let us see what comes out. With $d = 0$, the semi-perimeter of the 4-gon is

$s = \frac{1}{2}(a + b + c + 0) = \frac{1}{2}(a + b + c)$,

the same as the semi-perimeter of the triangle. Now apply Brahmagupta’s formula:

area of the triangle $= \sqrt{(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)(s - d)} = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}$, since $d = 0$.

This is Heron’s formula! So Brahmagupta’s formula can be seen as a generalisation of Heron’s formula.
