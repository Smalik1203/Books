No height is given, so half base times height cannot start. But Heron’s formula needs only the sides: $s = \frac{1}{2}(13 + 20 + 21) = 27$, and the area is $\sqrt{27 \times 14 \times 7 \times 6} = \sqrt{15876} = 126$ cm$^2$. Now every altitude follows from $\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = 126$. The area is fixed, so the longest base goes with the shortest height: the altitude to the $21$ cm side is $\frac{2 \times 126}{21} = 12$ cm. Heron’s formula gave the area without a height, and the area then gave the heights.

In the 4-gon $ABCD$, $AB = 25$ cm, $BC = 60$ cm, $CD = 52$ cm and $DA = 39$ cm, and the diagonal $AC$ is $65$ cm (Fig. 6.56). Find the area of $ABCD$.

A 4-gon’s area cannot be found from its four sides alone — Fig. 6.27 showed that. Here we also have a diagonal, which cuts the 4-gon into two triangles, and Heron’s formula would work for each. But look at the numbers first. $25^2 + 60^2 = 625 + 3600 = 4225 = 65^2$, so by the converse of the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem, $\angle B = 90^\circ$. And $39^2 + 52^2 = 1521 + 2704 = 4225 = 65^2$, so $\angle D = 90^\circ$ too. The area is $\frac{1}{2} \times 25 \times 60 + \frac{1}{2} \times 39 \times 52 = 750 + 1014 = 1764$ cm$^2$.

There is a check. Both right angles stand on $AC$, so $B$ and $D$ lie on the circle with diameter $AC$, and $ABCD$ is cyclic. Brahmagupta’s formula applies: $s = \frac{1}{2}(25 + 60 + 52 + 39) = 88$, and the area is $\sqrt{63 \times 28 \times 36 \times 49} = \sqrt{3111696} = 1764$ cm$^2$. The two methods agree.

A round plate of radius $14$ cm is to be cut along a circle with the same centre, into an inner disc and an outer ring of equal area. What is the radius of the cut?

It is tempting to cut halfway, at $7$ cm. But a disc of radius $7$ cm has only a quarter of the area, since area goes with the square of the radius. Let the cut have radius $x$ cm. The inner disc must be half the plate: $\pi x^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times \pi \times 14^2$, so $x^2 = 98$ and $x = \sqrt{98} = 7\sqrt{2} \approx 9.9$ cm. Again $\pi$ cancels. For a plate of any size, the cut that halves the area is at $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ of the radius, about $0.71$ of the way out.
