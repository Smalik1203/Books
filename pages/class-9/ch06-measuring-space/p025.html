6.10Area of a Circle

A circle is a very basic shape. It is easy to draw if you have a rope and a peg to tie it to. People have used the circular shape in their settlements, buildings and tools for thousands of years.

Why were people so fond of circular shapes? Was it only for practical reasons, or could there have been other reasons too? What uses have people found for the circular shape?

Early on, people faced the problem of finding the area $A$ enclosed by a circle. They may have needed to know how much grain a round tower could hold (its cross section is a circle), or how much land a round garden or building took up. They needed such facts to plan their cities, to fix taxes, and so on.

Early societies understood well that the area $A$ must be proportional to the square of the circumference $C$. Here is why.

A square of side $a$ has perimeter $P = 4a$ and area $A = a^2$. So $P^2 : A = (4a)^2 : a^2 = 16a^2 : a^2 = 16 : 1$. The ratio $P^2 : A$ is $16 : 1$ for every square; it does not depend on the size of the square.

An equilateral triangle of side $a$ has perimeter $P = 3a$ and area $A = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2$. So $P^2 : A = (3a)^2 : \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2 = 9a^2 : \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2 = 36 : \sqrt{3}$. The ratio is $36 : \sqrt{3}$ for every equilateral triangle, whatever its size.

In the same way, for many shapes, the ratio $P^2 : A$ stays fixed when we change the scale. Its value depends only on the shape, not on the size.

This reasoning suggests that for a circle too, with circumference $C$ and area $A$, the ratio $C^2 : A$ must be a fixed number. But what number?

Well before $1500$ BCE, the Babylonians had found by measuring that this number is close to $12$: that is, $C^2 : A \approx 12 : 1$. So their formula for the area of a circle was $A \approx \dfrac{C^2}{12}$.

Around $1500$ BCE, the ancient Egyptians found another formula, which looks like the one above but is much more accurate: $A \approx \left(\dfrac{8d}{9}\right)^2$, where $d$ is the diameter of the circle. Since $d = 2r$, this can also be written as $A \approx \dfrac{64}{81} \times 4r^2$, that is, $A \approx \dfrac{256}{81}r^2$. Remarkably, the same formula appears in the Baudhāyana Śulbasūtra ($800$ BCE), in a geometric method for constructing a square with nearly the same area as a circle.
