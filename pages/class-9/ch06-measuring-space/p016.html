Suppose two polygons $P$ and $Q$ have equal area. Is it always possible to cut one of them with straight cuts into two or more pieces, and rearrange the pieces to cover the other exactly? Try it for familiar shapes:

  1. a square and a rectangle that is not a square, with equal area;
  2. two triangles of different shapes but equal area;
  3. a triangle and a square with equal area.

Make a conjecture of your own about this.

Now think of different rectangles with perimeter $40$ units. The sides do not have to be whole numbers.

  1. How many such rectangles are there?
  2. Among them, is there one with the largest area? What are its sides?
  3. Is there one with the smallest area? What are its sides?

Does either answer surprise you?

6.8.1 Heron’s formula

You already know one formula for the area of a triangle: half the base times the height. Are there others? There are several. For now we look at Heron’s formula, found by the Greek mathematician and inventor Heron. He taught at the Museum in Alexandria, a city of ancient Egypt on the banks of the Nile.

Heron’s formula finds the area of a triangle from the lengths of its three sides. First work out the semi-perimeter $s$, which is half the perimeter. Then put $s$ and the three sides into one expression.

Heron’s formula

If $\triangle ABC$ has sides $BC = a$, $CA = b$ and $AB = c$, and $s = \frac{1}{2}(a + b + c)$, then

$$\text{area}(\triangle ABC) = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}.$$
