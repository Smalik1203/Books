For equilateral triangles, the ratio of perimeter to side is $3 : 1$. Make the triangle larger or smaller, and the ratio stays fixed at $3 : 1$.

What about a circle? Its perimeter is usually called its circumference. How is the circumference related to the diameter? Is the ratio of the circumference $C$ to the diameter $D$ the same for circles of all sizes (Fig. 6.5)? What do you think?

6.2Perimeter of a Circle — the C/D Ratio

People realised long ago that the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter does not change when the size of the circle changes. Let us call this ratio the $C/D$ ratio of the circle.

What is the value of the $C/D$ ratio? How would you estimate it?

You can estimate the $C/D$ ratio at home. Take a cotton reel with thin thread wound round it, and measure the diameter $D$ of the reel as carefully as you can. Unwind the thread, then wind it tightly round the reel $20$ times. Unwind it again, measure its length $L$, and work out $\dfrac{L}{20D}$. This is the ratio we want. The thinner the thread, the better the estimate.

Do the experiment! Is your ratio between $3$ and $4$? Is it between $3.1$ and $3.2$?

The $C/D$ ratio can also be estimated by pure geometry, with no measuring at all. Can you imagine how?

From ancient approximations to Mādhava’s exact formula

Mathematicians have been fascinated by circles, and by the $C/D$ ratio, since ancient times and in many parts of the world. Today we call the ratio $\pi$ (we say “pi”, to rhyme with “pie”). It links many areas of mathematics, and it connects the straight-edged world of polygons with the curves we see in nature. Early civilisations used values that were good enough for building and for trade. Later, finding the ratio more and more exactly became a way for mathematicians to test how powerful their methods were.
