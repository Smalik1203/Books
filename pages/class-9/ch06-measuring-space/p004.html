In Mesopotamia, around $1900$ BCE, mathematicians moved beyond the rough whole number $3$. They saw that a circle has a perimeter a little longer than the regular hexagon drawn inside it (Fig. 6.6). They concluded that $\pi$ must be larger than $3$, and they took $\pi$ to be $3 + \frac{1}{8} = 3.125$.

By $250$ BCE, Archimedes of Syracuse brought a new level of rigour to the problem. He trapped the value of $\pi$ between the perimeters of polygons drawn inside and outside the circle. With a hexagon inside and a hexagon outside, he showed that $\pi$ lies between $3$ and $2\sqrt{3} \approx 3.46$ (Fig. 6.7). Working his way up to polygons with $96$ sides, he found that

$$3\frac{10}{71} \lt \pi \lt 3\frac{1}{7}.$$

In about $150$ CE, Ptolemy of Alexandria refined Archimedes’ calculations for his tables of astronomy, and used the value $\frac{377}{120} \approx 3.14167$ for $\pi$.
