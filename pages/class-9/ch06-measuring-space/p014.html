6.8Area of a Triangle

Next we work out a formula for the area of a triangle. You met the formula in Class 8, so we go over the idea briefly. One way is to take a right-angled triangle first, and then other triangles. In both cases we put the triangle inside a rectangle, and use the formula for the area of a rectangle (Fig. 6.20A).

In the first case, the triangle $ABC$ is half of the rectangle $ABCD$. In the second, the perpendicular $EJ$ cuts the rectangle $HFGI$ into two rectangles, and the triangle $EFG$ is half of each. Here $FG = FJ + JG$, so $b = b_1 + b_2$. Either way, a triangle with base $b$ units and height $h$ units has half the area of the rectangle with base $b$ units and height $h$ units. That is, its area is $\frac{1}{2}bh$ sq. units.

Is there a gap in this argument, as there was for the parallelogram? What would we do if the angle $EFG$ were obtuse, and the triangle were shaped like the triangle $EFG$ in Fig. 6.20B? Work out the answer to this question.

A neater way to find the formula for the area of a triangle is to see that two congruent copies of a triangle fit together to make a parallelogram. In Fig. 6.21A, the triangles $ABC$ and $A'B'C'$ are congruent. They fit together as in Fig. 6.21B to make a parallelogram.
