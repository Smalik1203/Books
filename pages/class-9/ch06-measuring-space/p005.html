Soon after, in China, the circle-cutting method of Liu Hui ($263$ CE) prepared the way for two discoveries by Zu Chongzhi ($480$ CE). Zu pushed the polygon method to $24\,576$ sides! He used it to find the Yuelü (Approximate Ratio) $\frac{22}{7} \approx 3.1429$ for $\pi$, and the Milü (Close Ratio) $\frac{355}{113} \approx 3.1415929$ for $\pi$. The fraction $\frac{355}{113}$ is so close to $\pi$ that it remained the most accurate value of $\pi$ anywhere in the world for over $800$ years. It is now known that no fraction with a denominator less than $15\,000$ comes as close to $\pi$ as this one!

In $499$ CE, Āryabhaṭa gave the value $\frac{62832}{20000} = 3.1416$ for $\pi$. He called this value āsanna, meaning “approaching” or “approximate”. This suggests he understood that the ratio could not be written exactly as one simple fraction. Brahmagupta ($628$ CE) suggested the value $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.1623$ for $\pi$. It is a little less accurate, but he chose it because it is neat and easy to work with in equations. For centuries afterwards, $\sqrt{10}$ was the approximation most used in the Arab world and in medieval Europe.

Over the years that followed, many great mathematicians studied $\pi$ and its approximations. But none found an approximation as accurate as Zu’s $\frac{355}{113}$, or one as simple as $\frac{22}{7}$, $3.1416$ and $\sqrt{10}$, until the work of Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma, who changed everything. Mādhava saw that $\pi$ is not just a number to be approximated by fractions. It is a limit to be reached. He found the first exact formula for $\pi$:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \cdots$$

Is that not a beautiful formula? It is written as an infinite series: a sum that goes on for ever. It changed mathematics, because it moved the search for $\pi$ from cutting circles into polygons to adding up numbers. With it, Mādhava began the part of mathematics now called calculus. His infinite series let him find $\pi$ to $11$ decimal places, $3.14159265359$. The ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter had opened a window onto a whole new area of mathematics.

Over time, better and better infinite series gave more and more digits of $\pi$, more and more quickly. The key steps after Mādhava were made by Nīlakaṇṭha (c. $1500$), Machin ($1706$), Ramanujan ($1914$), and the Chudnovsky brothers ($1988$), who developed Ramanujan’s ideas. Using their methods, we now know $\pi$ to hundreds of trillions of digits! You will learn more about calculus in later classes.

In $1706$, the Welsh mathematician William Jones used the Greek letter $\pi$ for the $C/D$ ratio, because $\pi$ is the first letter of the Greek word perimetros, perimeter. The Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler (say “oiler”) made the symbol popular, and we still use it today.
