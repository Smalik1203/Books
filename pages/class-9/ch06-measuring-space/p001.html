Measuring Space:Perimeter and Area

Fig. 6.1 shows the start of a $4 \times 100$ m relay race. The lanes are marked out, and the first runner of each team is waiting at the start. Look at where they are standing. They are not on one starting line.

The runners in the outer lanes seem to start ahead of those in the inner lanes, and yet all of them finish at the same line. Why? The distance between the starting points of two lanes next to each other is called the stagger, and the stagger carries on all the way to the outermost lane. Does the stagger give anyone an unfair advantage — the runners in the outer lanes, or those in the inner lanes? And how do the organisers work out how long each stagger should be?

To answer questions about the stagger on an athletics track, we first need a way to find the length around a circle. That is where this chapter begins.

6.1Perimeter of a Shape

Take any shape. Its perimeter is the total length around its border. Picture a tiny insect walking along the border, never turning back, until it returns to where it started. The distance it walks is the perimeter of the shape.

So a square with side $a$ units has perimeter $4a$ units, and an equilateral triangle with side $a$ units has perimeter $3a$ units (Fig. 6.2A and 6.2B).
