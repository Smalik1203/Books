The familiar formula in use today

The ancient Greeks also knew that $\dfrac{A}{r^2}$ is a constant, but did not know what that constant was. As you have seen, two different civilisations took it to be about $\dfrac{256}{81}$.

Finally, in about $250$ BCE, Archimedes showed that the constant is exactly $\pi$ — the same constant that appears in the formula for the perimeter of a circle! So $A = \pi r^2$. He put it this way: “The area of a circle is equal to the area of a right-angled triangle whose two shorter sides are equal to the radius and to the circumference of the circle.” That is,

$$\text{area of circle} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{circumference} \times \text{radius} = \frac{1}{2} \times 2\pi r \times r = \pi r^2.$$

Archimedes’ proof is very clever. It uses a property shared by all regular polygons: the area of a regular polygon is half its perimeter times the radius of the circle that fits tightly inside it (Fig. 6.36). (This is proved in the same way as the formula for the area of a triangle in terms of the radius of its incircle.)

Archimedes did a thought experiment. He asked what happens as the number of sides of the regular polygon grows larger and larger. Working out the details, he arrived at $A = \pi r^2$.

Perhaps the most visual and easy-to-understand explanation of why a circle of radius $r$ has area $\pi r^2$ was first given by Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī (c. $1500$), in his commentary on the Āryabhaṭīya (Fig. 6.37).
