Why the other options are wrong

2 A probability is never more than 1, and $\tfrac54 = 1.25$. The other three all lie between 0 and 1, and 0 itself is the probability of an impossible event.
6 A sum of 8 comes from $(2, 6)$, $(3, 5)$, $(4, 4)$, $(5, 3)$ and $(6, 2)$: five of 36 pairs. Option (a) treats the eleven sums as equally likely, and option (b) is $\tfrac{4}{36}$, which leaves out $(4, 4)$.
7 One red ball is gone, so 3 of the 9 left are red: $\tfrac39 = \tfrac13$. Option (a) is $\tfrac{4}{10}$, the probability before the draw. Option (b) takes a red ball away but keeps 10 balls, and (c) keeps all 4 red balls among 9.
8 The multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12 and of 4 are 4, 8, 12. The number 12 is in both, so the event is $\{3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12\}$, six numbers. Option (a) counts 12 twice.
10 Neither die shows a 6 in $5 \times 5 = 25$ of the 36 pairs, so at least one 6 has probability $1 - \tfrac{25}{36} = \tfrac{11}{36}$. Option (b) is $\tfrac16 + \tfrac16$, which counts the double six twice.
13 There are ten multiples of 3 and six of 5, but 15 and 30 are in both lists. So $14$ of the $30$ numbers qualify. Option (a) is $\tfrac{16}{30}$, the count before 15 and 30 are taken off once.
16 A pin is not a coin. Its two positions are not equally likely, so nothing gives you $0.5$, and the 500 drops are exactly the evidence to use: $\tfrac{310}{500} = 0.62$. Option (b) is the gambler's fallacy, and (c) forgets that trials are how a probability is estimated.
20 A is true: two sets of trials rarely give the same count. R is false: a theoretical probability comes from counting outcomes, and the number of trials plays no part in it. So the answer is (c).
22 A is false: an even chance does not promise one success in every two trials, and runs of the same result happen. R is true. So the answer is (d).
