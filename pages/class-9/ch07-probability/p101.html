Using What You Know

The chapter left you with a short list. A probability is a number from 0 to 1. Experimental probability counts what happened; theoretical probability counts what could happen. The two meet as the trials pile up. A sample space lists every outcome once, and an event is a part of that list.

Every question below can be answered from that list alone. What is new is that none of them says which item to reach for — and two of them can be answered without any arithmetic at all, if you see what is being asked. Try each one before you read on.

A bag holds 12 marbles, some red and the rest blue. The probability of drawing a red one is $\tfrac13$. How many are blue?

Most probability questions hand you the counts and ask for the probability. This one runs the other way, and the formula works just as well read backwards.

given $P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{\text{red}}{12} = \dfrac13$
so $\text{red} = \dfrac{12}{3} = 4$
blue $12 - 4$ 8

Eight blue. Check it the other way round before trusting it: 4 red out of 12 is $\tfrac13$, as required.

What made this work is that the formula is an equation, not an instruction. It relates three quantities — favourable, possible, and the probability — and any two of them fix the third. Given the counts you find the probability. Given the probability and the total you find the count. Given the probability and a count you find the total. The same three lines of algebra serve all three questions.

A coin is tossed 8 times and comes up heads every time. A student says the coin is certainly biased. Another says it is certainly fair, because the probability of heads is always $\tfrac12$. Both are overstating. What can you actually say?

Neither student is right, and the interesting part is why each is wrong in a different direction.

The first treats evidence as proof. Eight heads from a fair coin is unlikely — it happens about once in every 256 attempts — but unlikely is not impossible, and once in 256 is not never. The run is evidence of bias, not proof of it.
