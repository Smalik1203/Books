Why the other options are wrong

B2 R is the law of large numbers, correctly stated, and it is true. A is the gambler's fallacy, and it is false: the coin carries no record of the five heads. The proportion settles not because tails are owed but because the five are diluted by the thousands of tosses that follow. A true reason attached to a false claim still leaves the claim false.
B3 Statement (i) reads a run as proof. From a fair coin, 530 heads in 1000 is an ordinary result, so the trials do not establish bias. (ii) is just the definition of experimental probability, and (iii) is what a fair coin would give — which leaves (c).
B5 The red ball is gone, so 3 of the 9 that remain are red and the answer is $\tfrac13$. Options (b) and (c) are both $\tfrac25$, the probability before the draw — the trap is to keep using a sample space the first draw has already changed.
C1 Counting "at least one 6" directly means adding three overlapping cases. Counting its opposite means one: 5 choices on each die, so $25$ of the $36$ pairs have no 6, and $1 - \tfrac{25}{36} = \tfrac{11}{36}$. Option (b) comes from adding $\tfrac16 + \tfrac16$, which counts the double six twice.
C4 Ten multiples of 3 and six of 5 make sixteen, but 15 and 30 are in both lists and have been counted twice. Take them off once: $14$ of $30$, or $\tfrac{7}{15}$. Option (c) is the uncorrected count.
C6 Three things happen at once, with 2, 2 and 6 outcomes, so the sample space has $2 \times 2 \times 6 = 24$ entries — HH1, HH2, and so on to TT6. Option (a) adds the three counts instead of multiplying them; (b) forgets one of the coins; (d) is $6 \times 6$, the answer for two dice, and dice are not what was rolled.
C7 A pin is not a coin. Its two positions are not equally likely, so nothing entitles you to $0.5$, and the 500 drops are exactly the evidence the question asks you to use: $\tfrac{310}{500} = 0.62$. Option (c) is the gambler's fallacy again, and (d) mistakes a hard theoretical probability for no probability at all.
