Ask a friend to call the result of a coin you are about to toss. They will say heads or tails — and they will be right about half the time.

Now ask them why they chose the one they did. Is there any answer they could give that would make them right more often?

7.1.2 The probability scale

Every probability is a number between 0 and 1. Nothing is ever less likely than impossible, and nothing is ever more likely than certain, so the scale has two ends and everything else lies between them.

Read the scale from the middle outwards. A probability of $\tfrac12$ means an even chance: the event is as likely to happen as not. Below $\tfrac12$ it is less likely than not; above, more likely. At the very ends the uncertainty runs out altogether — 0 is impossible and 1 is certain.

Saying your school has a probability of $0.75$ of winning the hockey match says something precise. It says that in situations like this one, the school wins about three times in every four. It is more likely than not, but you would be unwise to treat it as settled: one match in four still goes the other way.

Probabilities are written as fractions, as decimals or as percentages, and the three say exactly the same thing. $\tfrac34$, $0.75$ and $75\%$ are one number wearing three coats. Use whichever makes the arithmetic in front of you easiest, and do not let the change of dress persuade you that the quantity has changed.

The Probability Scale

The probability of any event is a number $P$ with $0 \le P \le 1$. An impossible event has $P = 0$; a certain event has $P = 1$; an event as likely as not has $P = \tfrac12$.
