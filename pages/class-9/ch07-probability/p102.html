The second has the gambler's fallacy inside out. It is true that a fair coin gives $\tfrac12$ every toss, but that is a statement about a coin already known to be fair. Here the fairness is exactly what is in question, so the argument assumes what it is trying to show.

What you can say is this: the run is weak evidence that something is wrong with the coin, and the way to strengthen or dismiss it is more tosses. That is the law of large numbers used as an instrument rather than a slogan.

Say the coin is tossed 800 more times. If it is fair, the proportion of heads will drift towards $\tfrac12$ and the eight will be lost in the crowd. If it is weighted, the proportion will settle somewhere else and stay there. Either way the answer arrives, and it arrives from the same evidence — more of it.

Two dice are rolled and the numbers added. A student writes the sample space as $\{2, 3, 4, \ldots, 12\}$ and concludes that $P(\text{sum} = 2) = \tfrac{1}{11}$. The list is complete and the arithmetic is right. The answer is wrong. Why?

The list $\{2, 3, \ldots, 12\}$ really does contain every possible sum, and it really does contain eleven of them. The fault is not in the list. It is in the assumption underneath the formula.

Favourable over possible only works when the outcomes are equally likely, and these are not. There is one way to make a sum of 2 — both dice show 1 — but six ways to make a 7:

$1{+}6$,   $2{+}5$,   $3{+}4$,   $4{+}3$,   $5{+}2$,   $6{+}1$

The equally likely outcomes are not the sums but the pairs, and there are $6 \times 6 = 36$ of them. Counted properly:

$P(\text{sum} = 2)$ $\dfrac{1}{36}$
$P(\text{sum} = 7)$ $\dfrac{6}{36} = \dfrac16$

This is the most useful mistake in the chapter, because it is invisible. Every step after the bad list is correct, and the answer still comes out wrong. When a sample space is handed to you, ask not only whether it is complete but whether its entries are the things that are equally likely.

A spinner has eight equal sectors numbered 1 to 8. What is the probability that it stops on a number that is both even and greater than 5?

Take the two conditions one at a time and then keep only what satisfies both.

even $\{2, 4, 6, 8\}$
