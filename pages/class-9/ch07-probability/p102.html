In a survey of 200 people, 150 said they owned a bicycle. In a second survey, of 20 people at a cycle repair shop, 19 said they owned one. Both give a probability. Which would you use to estimate the figure for the town, and why?

Three coins are tossed together. Which is more likely — exactly two heads, or three heads? Answer by listing, not by feel.

A box holds 5 pens: 3 blue and 2 black. One is drawn and not put back, then a second is drawn. Explain why the probability that the second is blue cannot be worked out until you know what the first one was.

An event has probability $0.35$. What is the probability that it does not happen? State the rule you used, and say why it must be true.

A spinner is divided into three sectors marked 1, 2 and 3, but sector 1 covers half the disc and the other two a quarter each. A student says $P(1) = \tfrac13$, since there are three outcomes. Correct that, and give all three probabilities.

A word about the third and the seventh. Both look like questions about dice and pens, and both are really questions about the sample space — whether the outcomes being counted are equally likely, and whether the list has changed between one draw and the next. Those two checks catch most of the mistakes made in this subject, and they are worth running before any arithmetic.

The ninth is the same pair of checks in a third disguise. It is set on a spinner rather than a die, and the outcomes are printed on the disc where you can see them, which is exactly what makes the wrong answer tempting: three numbers are visible, so three seems to be the count that matters. It is not. What matters is how much of the disc each number owns.

None of these nine yields to a remembered formula, which is deliberate. Each turns on a decision made before the formula — what the outcomes are, whether they are equally likely, and whether the evidence given is the evidence the question needs. Settle those and the arithmetic is a line.
