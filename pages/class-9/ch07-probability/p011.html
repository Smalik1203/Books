So the two routes of this section are not rivals after all. They are two ways to the same number, and the law says they meet. Where the theory is available, it tells you where the experiment is heading. Where it is not — the drawing pin, the favourite fruit — the experiment is all you have, and the law tells you that collecting more evidence is worth the trouble.

It also explains why small samples mislead. Ten rolls of a fair die can easily give three 4s, suggesting $0.3$. Nothing is wrong with the die; ten is simply too few for the settling to have happened.

How many trials count as many? There is no fixed number, and it depends on how close you need to be. Fifty rolls will usually put you within a few hundredths of the true figure; five thousand will put you within a few thousandths. What the law promises is the direction, not the speed — that more evidence moves you towards the answer, never away from it.

A die has just been rolled four times and gave a 4 every time. The law of large numbers says the fraction of 4s settles to $\tfrac16$ in the long run.

Does that mean a 4 is now less likely on the fifth roll, so the fraction can come back down? Think carefully before reading on.

The gambler's fallacy

The answer is no, and getting it wrong is so common that the mistake is named.

Example 6

You are playing Snakes and Ladders with a fair die and have just rolled three 6s in a row. What is the probability that the next roll is a 6?

It is tempting to argue that four 6s in a row is a rare thing, so the fourth must be unlikely. That argument is about a different question.

Solution.

Step 1 1 favourable outcome (the face 6) favourable
Step 2 6 possible outcomes possible
Step 3 $\dfrac{1}{6}$, about 0.167 probability
Answer The probability is $\tfrac16$, exactly as it was on the first roll and on every roll before it

Both statements are true at once, and that is what makes this hard. Before any rolling, four 6s in a row is indeed unlikely. But three of them have now happened; they are facts, not possibilities. What remains is one roll of a fair die, and the die has no way of knowing what it did before. It has no memory, no record, and no obligation to even things up.
