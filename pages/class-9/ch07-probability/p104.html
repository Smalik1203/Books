Exactly three heads is four times as likely. The reason is that "four heads" names one outcome while "exactly three heads" names four — the one tail can arrive in four different places, and order is what the sample space is made of.

Feel says otherwise, and it is worth knowing why. Three heads and four heads sound like neighbouring results, so they sound about equally likely. But "four heads" is a description that fits one outcome only, while "exactly three" fits a small family of them, and the probability follows the size of the family rather than the sound of the description.

A box holds 5 pens: 3 blue and 2 black. One is drawn and not put back, then a second is drawn, and nobody looks at the first. Is the second pen more likely, less likely or just as likely to be blue as the first? Answer by listing the pairs.

Every sample space so far has stayed still. This one does not, and that is the whole point of the question.

Give the pens names — $B_1$, $B_2$, $B_3$ for the blue ones and $K_1$, $K_2$ for the black — so that each pen is an outcome of its own. A pair of draws is two different pens in order: 5 choices for the first and 4 left for the second, so $5 \times 4 = 20$ pairs, all equally likely.

first is blue 3 choices, then 4 pens left: $3 \times 4 = 12$ pairs $\tfrac{12}{20} = \tfrac35$
second is blue 3 choices for it, then 4 for the first: $4 \times 3 = 12$ pairs $\tfrac{12}{20} = \tfrac35$

Just as likely. The first draw does change what is left in the box, but nobody looked, so the list of pairs is all there is to go on — and it is balanced. Read the pairs from the second pen's side, and each of the 5 pens is as likely as any other to be the one drawn second.

In a class of 40, the probability that a student picked at random wears glasses is $0.35$. How many students do not wear glasses? Find it two ways.

The first way goes through the opposite event. The rule from the end of the chapter, $P(\text{not } E) = 1 - P(E)$, gives $1 - 0.35 = 0.65$, and applying a rate to the class is multiplication: $0.65 \times 40 = 26$.

The second way counts the event itself and takes it away: $0.35 \times 40 = 14$ students wear glasses, and $40 - 14 = 26$ do not. The two ways agree.

The first is worth having, because "not" is often far easier to count than the thing itself. Asked for the probability that a whole number from 1 to 50 is not a multiple of 7, you could list forty-three numbers — or notice that only seven of them are multiples, and write $1 - \tfrac{7}{50} = \tfrac{43}{50}$.
