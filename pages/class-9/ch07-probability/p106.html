The pens, and a sample space that moves

Every sample space so far has stayed still. This one does not, and that is the whole point of the question.

Before any draw there are 5 pens, 3 of them blue. After one is drawn and kept, there are 4 — and which 4 depends on what came out.

first was blue 2 blue of 4 left $\tfrac24 = \tfrac12$
first was black 3 blue of 4 left $\tfrac34$

Two different answers, so the question as asked has no single one. The draws are not independent: the first result changes the situation the second happens in. Put the pen back and this vanishes — the box is restored, the sample space is what it was, and $\tfrac35$ holds for both draws.

Notice how unlike the gambler's fallacy this is. There, the past did not affect the future and people thought it did. Here it genuinely does, because something physical changed. Telling those two apart is the skill; the test is whether the trial altered what is available next time.

The complement

If an event has probability $0.35$, the probability that it does not happen is $1 - 0.35 = 0.65$.

The rule is that $P(\text{not } E) = 1 - P(E)$, and it is true because the event and its opposite between them cover every outcome exactly once. Their probabilities are two shares of the same whole, so they must add to 1 — the same fact the fruit bar showed, where the four widths filled the bar.

It is worth having, because "not" is often far easier to count than the thing itself. Asked for the probability of at least one head in three tosses, you can find seven favourable outcomes — or notice that the only failure is TTT, and write $1 - \tfrac18 = \tfrac78$.

The spinner, and what "outcome" counts

Counting the numbers printed on the disc gives three, and dividing by three gives $\tfrac13$ — but only if the three are equally likely, and here they are plainly not. Half the disc is marked 1.

What the pointer actually meets is area, so area is what carries the probability: $P(1) = \tfrac12$, and $P(2) = P(3) = \tfrac14$. They add to 1, as they must.
