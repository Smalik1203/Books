What this chapter builds instead is a way of estimating likelihood that anyone can check and anyone can repeat. Not because instinct is wrong, but because a number arrived at from evidence can be argued about — and the argument can be settled.

7.1.1 What is randomness?

An action is random when you know every outcome it could have, and still cannot say which one it will have. Both halves matter. If you do not know the outcomes, you are simply ignorant; if you do know which one is coming, there is nothing to measure.

Toss a coin and you know it will come down heads or tails. Roll a die and you know the face will read 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. In neither case can you say in advance which. Draw a name from the box and you know it will be someone in the school — but not who.

An action of this kind is called an experiment, or a trial, and each thing it could produce is an outcome. The words are borrowed from the laboratory, and they are borrowed on purpose: a probability is something you can go and measure, not something you settle by argument.

Randomness is not the same as ignorance, and the difference is worth keeping straight. Nobody knows which name will come out of the lucky-draw box, but that is not because the information is hidden somewhere — there is no fact of the matter until the slip is drawn. Contrast that with a sealed envelope holding tomorrow's timetable: the answer already exists, and you simply have not seen it.

Probability handles the first kind. It is the mathematics of situations that are genuinely unsettled, not of secrets.

The distinction shows up in the language people use. We say we do not know who will win the match, and we say we do not know the capital of a country we have never read about, and the two are not the same kind of not knowing. Only the first has a probability attached to it.
