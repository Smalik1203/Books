7.2.3 Reading data as probability

Coins and dice are convenient because their outcomes are equally likely by construction. Most of the world is not like that. There is no symmetry that tells you how many people prefer mangoes, or how far a tyre runs before it is replaced. For those, the only route is the first one: collect evidence and divide.

Example 4

You ask all 50 students in your class for their favourite fruit. Twenty say mango, fifteen say apple, ten say banana and five say grapes. Pick one student at random. What is the probability that their favourite is mango?

The trials here are the students, and the event is "this student says mango". Nothing about the situation is symmetrical — mango is more popular than grapes, and only the data can say by how much.

students saying mango 20
students in all 50
probability $\dfrac{20}{50}$ 0.4

So there is a $0.4$, or $40\%$, chance that a student picked at random from this class likes mango best.

Drawn this way the four probabilities are the four widths, and they add to the whole bar — which is another way of seeing that the probabilities of all the outcomes must add to 1. Every student likes exactly one of the four best, so between them the four answers use up all the certainty there is.

It also shows what a probability found this way can and cannot be asked to do. These four numbers describe the fifty students who answered. Whether they describe the school depends entirely on how those fifty were chosen — and that question is not answered by the bar, however neatly it divides.
