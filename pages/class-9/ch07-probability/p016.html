Write the sample space before the probability. The counting is the hard part, and it is the part a formula cannot do for you.

The tree diagram

When an experiment happens in stages — toss a coin, then toss it again — the outcomes multiply, and a list written by hand starts to miss things. A tree diagram makes missing them difficult.

Draw a branch for every outcome of the first stage. From the end of each of those, draw a branch for every outcome of the second. Every path from the root to a tip is then one complete outcome, and the tips are the sample space.

Example 7

A fair coin is tossed twice. List the sample space, and find the probability of two heads.

The first toss gives H or T. From each of those the second toss gives H or T again, which is two branches from each of two — four paths in all.

sample space $S = \{\,\text{HH},\ \text{HT},\ \text{TH},\ \text{TT}\,\}$
$n(S)$ 4
two heads $\{\,\text{HH}\,\}$ $\tfrac14$

So $P(\text{HH}) = \tfrac14$. Notice that HT and TH are two different outcomes, not one: heads-then-tails is a different history from tails-then-heads, even though both leave you with one of each.

The same picture answers questions the list alone would not. Reading the four tips, exactly two of them — HT and TH — have one head and one tail, so that event has probability $\tfrac24 = \tfrac12$. Getting at least one head takes three of the four tips, giving $\tfrac34$.
