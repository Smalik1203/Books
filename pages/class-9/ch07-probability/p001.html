7

The Measure of Chance

7.1Introduction

Probability is a kind of measurement. That is the whole idea, and it is worth saying before anything else, because it is easy to mistake this chapter for a chapter about guessing. It is not. A ruler measures length, a jug measures volume, and probability measures how likely something is. The three are more alike than they look.

Consider three questions:

Is it going to rain today?

Will our school win the inter-school hockey match tomorrow?

Will I be chosen in the monthly lucky draw, when every name in the school is written on a slip and one slip is picked?

Nobody can answer any of them with certainty. But notice how much we do know. In each case the possible outcomes are already settled: it will rain or it will not; the team will win, draw or lose; one particular name comes out of the box. What is unsettled is only which of them happens.

That is the situation this chapter is about — one where the outcomes are known and the outcome is not. You could answer with words: impossible, certain, less likely, more likely, equally likely. Those words are useful, and they are where everyone starts. But words cannot be compared closely. Is "quite likely" more or less than "a good chance"? Two people will not agree, and there is no way to settle it.

A number can be compared. By the end of this chapter you will be able to put one on each of those three questions, and say exactly what it means.

There is a second reason for wanting a number rather than a word. Two people can look at the same sky and reach opposite conclusions — one sees the cloud thinning and expects a clear afternoon, the other sees it thickening and reaches for an umbrella. Both are reading the same evidence and weighing it by instinct. That kind of judgement is called subjective probability, and it is not worthless: an experienced farmer's guess about rain is worth more than a stranger's. But it cannot be checked, and two of them cannot be compared.
