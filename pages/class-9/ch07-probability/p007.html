7.2.2 Theoretical probability

The other route never touches a die at all. If every outcome is equally likely — if there is no reason for the world to prefer one over another — then the probability of an event is simply the share of the outcomes that count as favourable.

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{number of possible outcomes}}$$

The condition in front of that formula is not decoration. Equally likely is an assumption, and the whole calculation rests on it. For a well-made die it is a good assumption: the faces are alike, the cube is balanced, and nothing distinguishes 4 from 5. For a drawing pin it is a bad one, which is why the pin had to be dropped rather than reasoned about.

Example 2

Roll a fair six-sided die. What is the theoretical probability of getting a 4?

Count the two numbers the formula asks for. The possible outcomes are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 — six of them. The favourable ones are those that count as success: just the single face 4.

favourable 1   (the face 4)
possible 6   (the faces 1 to 6)
probability $\dfrac{1}{6}$ 0.167

So $P(4) = \tfrac16 \approx 0.167$, or about $16.7\%$.

Compare that with the $0.16$ measured in Example 1. The two are close, and they are answering different questions: one reports what 50 rolls did, the other what a fair die must do in the long run. That they nearly agree is the first hint of something this chapter returns to.

The word fair was doing quiet work in that example. When we call a coin or a die fair, we mean it is symmetrical — that there is no reason for it to come down one way more often than another. The usual word for this is unbiased. An unbiased die is not one that behaves perfectly; it is one with no reason to behave otherwise.

The distinction matters, because the theoretical probability was found by assuming it. Divide favourable by possible and you have assumed the outcomes are equally likely; nothing in the arithmetic checks that they are. Put a lump of lead under the 6 and the formula gives $\tfrac16$ just as cheerfully, and is wrong.
