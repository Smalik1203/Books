What this chapter established
  1. Probability measures likelihood, on a scale from 0 to 1. Impossible is 0, certain is 1, and an even chance is $\tfrac12$.
  2. An action is random when its outcomes are known and its result is not.
  3. Experimental probability is relative frequency: how often the event happened, over how many trials. It needs no assumptions and works where nothing is symmetrical.
  4. Theoretical probability is favourable outcomes over possible outcomes. It needs the outcomes to be equally likely, and says what a fair object must do.
  5. The law of large numbers joins the two: as trials grow, the experimental figure settles towards the theoretical one.
  6. Past results do not bend the next trial. Believing they do is the gambler's fallacy.
  7. A sample space lists every outcome once. An event is a subset of it, and a tree diagram is how you list a sample space without missing anything.
  8. The probabilities of all the outcomes of an experiment add to 1, so an event and its opposite share the whole: $P(\text{not } E) = 1 - P(E)$.
  9. A sample estimates the population it was drawn from — and only if it was drawn fairly. A biased sample gives a precise answer to a different question.
