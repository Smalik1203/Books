7.3.2 Events

An event is any outcome, or any collection of outcomes, that you have decided to care about. In the language of sets, an event is a subset of the sample space.

Rolling a die has six outcomes. "Rolling a 3" is an event with one outcome in it. "Rolling an even number" is an event with three. "Rolling a number under 7" is an event containing all six.

$S$ $\{\,1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\,\}$
an even number $\{\,2, 4, 6\,\}$ $\tfrac36 = \tfrac12$
more than 4 $\{\,5, 6\,\}$ $\tfrac26 = \tfrac13$
less than 7 $\{\,1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\,\}$ $\tfrac66 = 1$
more than 6 $\{\ \}$ $\tfrac06 = 0$

The last two are the ends of the scale from the start of the chapter, arrived at from a different direction. An event containing every outcome is certain; an event containing none is impossible. The scale and the counting agree, as they must.

Once the sample space is written down, the theoretical probability of an event is nothing more than a count of how many outcomes fall inside it, over how many there are altogether. All the difficulty is in the listing — which is why the next pages are about a way of listing that does not let you miss anything.

Write the sample space before the probability. The counting is the hard part, and it is the part a formula cannot do for you.

The tree diagram

When an experiment happens in stages — toss a coin, then toss it again — the outcomes multiply, and a list written by hand starts to miss things. A tree diagram makes missing them difficult.

Draw a branch for every outcome of the first stage. From the end of each of those, draw a branch for every outcome of the second. Every path from the root to a tip is then one complete outcome, and the tips are the sample space.
