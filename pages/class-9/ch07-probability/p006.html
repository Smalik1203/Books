7.2.1 Experimental probability

Do something many times, count how often the event happened, and divide. That fraction is the relative frequency of the event, and it is the experimental probability.

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of times the event occurred}}{\text{total number of trials}}$$

Everything about the definition is honest about what it is. It reports what happened. It makes no claim that the coin is fair, or the die true, or the drawing pin symmetrical — and that is exactly its strength. For a drawing pin there is no reasoning available at all: nothing about its shape tells you how often it lands point up. You have to drop it and count.

Example 1

A die is rolled 50 times and lands on 4 on eight of those rolls. What is the experimental probability of rolling a 4?

Take the definition exactly as it stands. The event is "rolls a 4". It happened 8 times, and there were 50 trials.

Solution.

Step 1 the event occurred 8 times times it occurred
Step 2 there were 50 trials trials
Step 3 $\dfrac{8}{50}$, which is 0.16 relative frequency
Answer $P(4) = \tfrac{8}{50} = \tfrac{4}{25} = 0.16$, or $16\%$

Now read what that does and does not say. It says that in these 50 rolls, a 4 came up on 16 of every 100 — a fact about what happened. It does not say the die is loaded, and it does not say the next roll has a $0.16$ chance. Roll the same die 50 more times and you will almost certainly get a different count.

That last point is the thing to hold on to. An experimental probability is a measurement, and like any measurement it comes with a margin. Measure a table with a short ruler and you will be a little out; measure a probability with few trials and you will be a little out too. The remedy in both cases is the same: measure more.

7.2.2 Theoretical probability

The other route never touches a die at all. If every outcome is equally likely — if there is no reason for the world to prefer one over another — then the probability of an event is simply the share of the outcomes that count as favourable.

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{number of possible outcomes}}$$
