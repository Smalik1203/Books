From a sample to a school

Now the useful part. Suppose the school has 1500 students and you must order fruit for all of them. You cannot ask 1500 people; you asked 50. Can the 50 speak for the 1500?

Cautiously, yes — and this is what a great deal of applied statistics is. The 50 students are a sample; the 1500 are the population. If the sample resembles the population, a probability measured on the sample is an estimate of the same probability in the population.

Example 5

Using the class of 50, estimate how many of the school's 1500 students have mango as their favourite fruit.

The probability found from the sample was $0.4$. Read that as a rate: four students in every ten. Applying a rate to a larger group is multiplication.

estimated probability $\dfrac{20}{50} = 0.4$
population 1500 students
estimate $0.4 \times 1500$ 600

So about 600 students in the school are likely to prefer mango.

The word about is carrying real weight. Six hundred is not a count; it is an estimate built on the assumption that the rest of the school is like your class. If your class happens to be the one that went to the mango festival last week, the estimate will be too high, and no amount of careful arithmetic afterwards will rescue it.

Sampling

A probability measured on a sample estimates the probability in the whole population — provided the sample is representative of it. A larger sample gives a better estimate; a biased sample gives a confident wrong answer.

How a sample is chosen therefore matters more than how large it is. Asking 50 students at random beats asking 500 who all queued at the same stall, because the queue has already sorted them by what they like.
