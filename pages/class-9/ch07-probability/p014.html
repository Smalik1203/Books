7.3Outcomes, Listed

Both formulas so far have asked you to count outcomes. For a die that is easy; there are six and everyone can name them. For two coins tossed together, or a die and a coin at once, it is easy to miss one — and a missed outcome makes every probability on the page wrong. So it is worth being systematic.

7.3.1 The sample space

The sample space of an experiment is the list of everything it could produce. It is written $S$, usually inside braces with the outcomes separated by commas, and the number of outcomes in it is the sample size, written $n(S)$.

tossing a coin   $S = \{\,\text{H},\ \text{T}\,\}$,   $n(S) = 2$

rolling a die   $S = \{\,1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\,\}$,   $n(S) = 6$

a hockey match   $S = \{\,\text{win},\ \text{draw},\ \text{lose}\,\}$,   $n(S) = 3$

Two rules govern the list, and both are easy to state and easy to break.

Writing a Sample Space

Every possible outcome must appear, and no outcome may appear twice.

Miss one and the denominator of every probability is too small, so all your answers come out too large. List one twice and the reverse happens. Neither error announces itself: the arithmetic afterwards is perfectly correct, and the answer is perfectly wrong.

How fine the list should be is a matter of judgement rather than rule. For the question "will it rain?" the space $\{\text{rain},\ \text{no rain}\}$ is enough. For "will the match be played?" it is not, because drizzle and a downpour do very different things to a hockey pitch, and the space needs to tell them apart. The sample space must be detailed enough for the question you are actually asking, and no more.
