Counting the numbers printed on the die gives three, and dividing by three gives $\tfrac13$ — but only if the three are equally likely, and here they are plainly not. Half the faces are marked 1.

What the die actually lands on is a face, and the six faces are equally likely, so faces are what carry the probability: $P(1) = \tfrac36 = \tfrac12$, $P(2) = \tfrac26 = \tfrac13$ and $P(3) = \tfrac16$. They add to 1, as they must.

A word about the third and the seventh. Both look like questions about dice and pens, and both are really questions about the sample space — whether the outcomes being counted are equally likely, and whether the list has changed between one draw and the next. Those two checks catch most of the mistakes made in this subject, and they are worth running before any arithmetic.

The ninth is the same pair of checks in a third disguise. It is set on a die, like the third, and the numbers are printed on the faces where you can see them. That is exactly what makes the wrong answer tempting: three numbers are visible, so three seems to be the count that matters. It is not. What matters is how many of the six faces each number owns.

None of these nine yields to a remembered formula, which is deliberate. Each turns on a decision made before the formula — what the outcomes are, whether they are equally likely, and whether the evidence given is the evidence the question needs. Settle those and the arithmetic is a line.
