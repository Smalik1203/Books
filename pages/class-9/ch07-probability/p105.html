Two surveys, one of them useless

Both give a probability, and the arithmetic of each is sound: $\tfrac{150}{200} = 0.75$ and $\tfrac{19}{20} = 0.95$. The question is which estimates the town.

The second sample was taken at a cycle repair shop. Everyone there has already been sorted by the very thing being measured — you do not queue at a repair shop without a bicycle. The sample cannot represent the town, and no increase in its size would help: a thousand people at that shop would give an answer closer to 1, not closer to the truth.

So use the first, and notice what settled it. Not the sample size, though 200 beats 20. It was how the sample was chosen. A biased sample does not give a vague answer; it gives a precise answer to a different question.

And the different question here is a real one, worth asking on its own terms: what proportion of people at a cycle repair shop own a bicycle? The answer, near enough, is all of them, and the survey establishes it. Nothing about the 20 responses is false. They simply describe a queue, not a town.

This is the flaw that made the first survey worth taking, and it is the flaw to look for whenever a figure is quoted at you. Ask who was asked, and where. A survey of shoppers taken in a market at eleven on a Tuesday morning will find the town oddly free of people who work in offices.

Three coins, listed

Eight outcomes, from two choices made three times over — $2 \times 2 \times 2$. Listing them costs a line and settles the question:

HHH   HHT   HTH   HTT   THH   THT   TTH   TTT

exactly two heads HHT, HTH, THH $\tfrac38$
three heads HHH $\tfrac18$

Exactly two heads is three times as likely. The reason is that "three heads" names one outcome while "exactly two heads" names three — the two heads can arrive in three different orders, and order is what the sample space is made of.

Feel says otherwise, and it is worth knowing why. Two heads and three heads sound like neighbouring results, so they sound about equally likely. But "three heads" is a description that fits one outcome only, while "exactly two" fits a small family of them, and the probability follows the size of the family rather than the sound of the description.
