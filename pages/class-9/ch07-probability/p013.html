What is worth noticing is that the die was doing the same work then as now. A game whose point was that conduct decides your fate still handed the movement to chance — and the chance had to be fair, or the moral would not land. The people who made these boards understood randomness well enough to rely on it.

Fair and unbiased

That reliance is worth naming, because the whole of theoretical probability leans on it. A coin is fair, or unbiased, when it is symmetrical enough that there is no reason for it to come down more often on one side than the other. The same goes for a die, a spinner, or a name drawn from a box.

Bias can also enter through the throwing rather than the object. A coin spun from the same starting face by a careful hand is not being tossed at random, even if the coin itself is perfect. This is why we say the trial must be conducted without interference — the fairness has to hold for the whole procedure, not just for the metal.
