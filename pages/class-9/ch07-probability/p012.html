The Gambler's Fallacy

Believing that past results change the probability of the next independent trial. They do not. A run of one outcome makes neither that outcome nor its opposite any likelier next time.

Then how does the fraction ever settle to $\tfrac16$, if no roll is ever pushed towards it? Not by correction, but by dilution. The three extra 6s are never cancelled — they simply become a smaller and smaller part of a growing total. Ten thousand rolls later, three of anything hardly shows.

Where the board came from

The Snakes and Ladders in that example is not a modern invention. It began in India as Moksha Patam, a game played with dice in which the board was a moral diagram. Every ladder stood for a virtue and every snake for a vice, and the player's climb towards liberation was helped or undone accordingly. Versions of it were played across the subcontinent for centuries, painted on cloth long before anyone printed a board.

What is worth noticing is that the die was doing the same work then as now. A game whose point was that conduct decides your fate still handed the movement to chance — and the chance had to be fair, or the moral would not land. The people who made these boards understood randomness well enough to rely on it.

Fair and unbiased

That reliance is worth naming, because the whole of theoretical probability leans on it. A coin is fair, or unbiased, when it is symmetrical enough that there is no reason for it to come down more often on one side than the other. The same goes for a die, a spinner, or a name drawn from a box.

Bias can also enter through the throwing rather than the object. A coin spun from the same starting face by a careful hand is not being tossed at random, even if the coin itself is perfect. This is why we say the trial must be conducted without interference — the fairness has to hold for the whole procedure, not just for the metal.
