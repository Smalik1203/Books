Three $x$-tiles stand to the right of the $x^2$-tile and four lie below it, and that is exactly what forces the twelve unit tiles into a 3 by 4 block. Once the whole thing closes up into a rectangle, its sides can be read off — $x + 3$ across and $x + 4$ down.

There are only three ways to split 7 into two positive whole numbers: 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4. Try each. How many close up, and what does that tell you?

That one picture holds two statements at once, depending on which way you read it. Multiplying and factorising are not two skills but one, run in opposite directions.

What Fig. 4.7 shows
  1. Read as a product: the two linear expressions $x + 3$ and $x + 4$ multiply to give $(x + 3)(x + 4) = x^2 + 7x + 12$.
  2. Read as a factorisation: the expression $x^2 + 7x + 12$ breaks into the linear factors $x + 3$ and $x + 4$, giving $x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)$.
