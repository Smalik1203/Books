What Fig. 4.7 shows
  1. Read as a product: the two linear expressions $x + 3$ and $x + 4$ multiply to give $(x + 3)(x + 4) = x^2 + 7x + 12$.
  2. Read as a factorisation: the expression $x^2 + 7x + 12$ breaks into the linear factors $x + 3$ and $x + 4$, giving $x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)$.

These are the same identity, met from the two directions this chapter keeps returning to. Multiplying out is the easy direction: you always can. Factorising is the hard one, because you are being asked to find the two numbers that fit — and the tiles turn that search into something you can see. If the pieces close into a rectangle, you have found them.

Every rectangle so far has had sides of the form $x + \text{something}$, so exactly one $x^2$-tile appeared each time. Change that and the picture grows.
