Example 7

Factorise $50p^2 + 60pq + 18q^2$.

What are $a$ and $b$ here? You would need a term whose square is $50p^2$, and that is $\sqrt{50}\,p$ — a surd, and awkward to carry through the working.

So look again before reaching for a square root.

Solution.

Step 1 $50p^2 + 60pq + 18q^2 = 2\left(25p^2 + 30pq + 9q^2\right)$ all three terms share a factor of 2
Step 2 $25p^2 = (5p)^2$, $9q^2 = (3q)^2$, and twice $5p \times 3q$ is $30pq$ the bracket is a perfect square
Step 3 $50p^2 + 60pq + 18q^2 = 2\left[(5p)^2 + 2(5p)(3q) + (3q)^2\right]$ so $a = 5p$, $b = 3q$
Answer $50p^2 + 60pq + 18q^2 = 2(5p + 3q)^2$

That is worth remembering as a habit: take out any common factor first. It can turn an expression that seems to need surds into one that does not.

Think and Reflect

Everything so far has been about $(a + b)^2$. Nothing in the argument said $b$ had to be positive — so replace $b$ by $-b$ throughout the identity. What comes out?

Since $(-b)^2 = b^2$ and $2a(-b) = -2ab$, only the middle term changes sign — a second identity, free of charge:

$$(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$$

It is an identity for the same reason the first one is: no particular values were used in getting it. And it does all the same jobs, namely expanding brackets, squaring numbers in the head, and spotting perfect squares to factorise.

One consequence is worth noticing at once. The differences $a - b$ and $b - a$ are the same size and opposite in sign, and squaring throws a sign away — so $(a - b)^2$ and $(b - a)^2$ are equal. It never matters which of the two numbers you subtract from which, provided you are going to square the result. The unsquared difference has no such freedom.
