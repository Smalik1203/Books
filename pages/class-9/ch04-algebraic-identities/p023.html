One case is worth carrying away on its own. If $x + y + z = 0$, the whole left-hand side is zero whatever the second bracket contains, so $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 3xyz$. Three numbers that add to nothing have cubes adding to three times their product — which is not a thing anyone would guess, and which turns several forbidding-looking questions into one line of arithmetic.

Example 15

Three numbers add to 10, multiply to 25, and their squares add to 38. What do their cubes add to?

Solution.

Step 1 $x + y + z = 10$, $xyz = 25$ and $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 38$ call them $x$, $y$ and $z$
Step 2 $10\left(38 - (xy + xz + yz)\right) = x^3 + y^3 + z^3 - 3(25)$ substituting into the identity
Step 3 $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 455 - 10(xy + xz + yz)$ $xy + xz + yz$ is still missing
Step 4 $(x + y + z)^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + 2(xy + xz + yz)$ the squared identity, Section 4.4
Step 5 $100 = 38 + 2(xy + xz + yz), \quad\text{so}\quad xy + xz + yz = 31$
Step 6 $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 455 - 10(31) = 455 - 310$ putting that back
Answer $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 145$

Notice that the three numbers were never found. They did not need to be: the identities work with the sums and products directly.

4.8Simplifying Rational Expressions

Everything so far has been about turning expressions into products. Here is where that pays off. A rational expression is one algebraic expression divided by another, and simplifying it means the same thing as simplifying a fraction of numbers: cancel whatever the top and the bottom have in common.

You cannot cancel a term, only a factor. So before anything can be cancelled, both the numerator and the denominator have to be factorised — which is exactly what this chapter has been teaching you to do.

The distinction between a term and a factor is where most of the marks are lost, so it is worth stating plainly. In $\dfrac{x + 3}{x}$ nothing cancels: the $x$ on top is added to something, not multiplying it. In $\dfrac{x(x + 3)}{x}$ the $x$ does cancel, because there it multiplies the whole bracket. Same symbols, different job.
