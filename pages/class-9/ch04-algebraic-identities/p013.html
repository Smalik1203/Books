Identities can also be read straight off a picture, without a word of algebra. The two rows of squares in Fig. 4.6 are one such statement. The four squares in the top row have sides $a + b + c$, $a + b - c$, $a - b + c$ and $a - b - c$; the three in the bottom row have sides $2a$, $2b$ and $2c$.

What identity does Fig. 4.6 state? The squares are drawn to scale, so compare the two rows by area, not by counting shapes.

The same statement can be built out of tiles. Three shapes are needed: a square of side $x$, a rectangle $x$ by 1, and a unit square. Then $x + 3$ is one $x$-tile beside three unit tiles, and $x + 4$ is one $x$-tile beside four. Fig. 4.7 lays out their product.

  • one $x^2$-tile
  • seven $x$-tiles — three to the right, four below
  • twelve unit tiles, in a 3 by 4 array
The whole rectangle is $x + 3$ by $x + 4$.
