The same statement can be built out of tiles. Three shapes are needed: a square of side $x$, a rectangle $x$ by 1, and a unit square. Then $x + 3$ is one $x$-tile beside three unit tiles, and $x + 4$ is one $x$-tile beside four. Fig. 4.7 lays out their product.

  • one $x^2$-tile
  • seven $x$-tiles — three to the right, four below
  • twelve unit tiles, in a 3 by 4 array
The whole rectangle is $x + 3$ by $x + 4$.

The layout is doing something specific: the $7x$ has been split as $3x + 4x$. Three $x$-tiles stand to the right of the $x^2$-tile and four lie below it, and that is exactly what forces the twelve unit tiles into a 3 by 4 block. Once the whole thing closes up into a rectangle, its sides can be read off — $x + 3$ across and $x + 4$ down.

Suppose the $7x$ were split as $2x + 5x$ instead. Can the tiles still be closed up into a rectangle? Try the other splittings too, and see which ones work.

There are only three ways to split 7 into two positive whole numbers: 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4. Try each. How many close up, and what does that tell you?

That one picture holds two statements at once, depending on which way you read it. Multiplying and factorising are not two skills but one, run in opposite directions.

What Fig. 4.7 shows
  1. Read as a product: the two linear expressions $x + 3$ and $x + 4$ multiply to give $(x + 3)(x + 4) = x^2 + 7x + 12$.
  2. Read as a factorisation: the expression $x^2 + 7x + 12$ breaks into the linear factors $x + 3$ and $x + 4$, giving $x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)$.
