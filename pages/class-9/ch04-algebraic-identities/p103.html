$$a^2 + b^2 \geq 2ab$$

The two sides are equal only when $a = b$ (Fig. 4B.3). Every identity in the chapter was an equation. This one fact turns it into an inequality.

The same fact answers the second question. Let the sides be $x$ and $y$. Then $x + y = 17$. By Pythagoras, $x^2 + y^2 = 144$. So $2xy = 289 - 144 = 145$. Then $(x - y)^2 = x^2 + y^2 - 2xy = 144 - 145 = -1$. A square cannot be $-1$. So there is no such rectangle. The algebra found this without finding a side.

That last answer is worth looking at a second time, because the inequality explains it. Among all rectangles with $x + y = 17$, which has the shortest diagonal? Since $x^2 + y^2 = 289 - 2xy$, the diagonal is shortest when $xy$ is largest, and $a^2 + b^2 \geq 2ab$ says that happens when the two sides are equal.

$$x = y = 8.5 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x^2 + y^2 = 144.5$$

So no rectangle of perimeter $34$ has a diagonal shorter than $\sqrt{144.5}$, a little over $12.02$. A diagonal of $12$ was never available. The algebra found this by arriving at $(x - y)^2 = -1$; the geometry finds it by noticing that the square is the extreme case and that the question asked for something past it. The two arguments are the same argument.

The inequality has a use of its own, too. Put $b = 1$ and divide through: for any positive $a$, $a + \dfrac{1}{a} \geq 2$. A positive number and its reciprocal can never average less than $1$, and they average exactly $1$ only when the number is $1$ itself.
