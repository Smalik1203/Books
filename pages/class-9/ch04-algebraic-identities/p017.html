That is a genuine narrowing. A quadratic has infinitely many pairs of numbers adding to 7, and infinitely many multiplying to 12, but very few do both — and once the constant is a whole number, its factor pairs can simply be listed. The search is short because two conditions have to hold at the same time.

Of the two, start with the product. Sums are cheap — every whole number is the sum of a pair in a hundred ways — while a product pins the two numbers down to a handful of candidates you can write out on one line. For a constant of 12 they are $1$ and $12$, $2$ and $6$, $3$ and $4$, and that is the entire field. Then read the sums off and see which one is wanted.

A word on what happens when none of them works. It does not mean you have made a mistake; it means the quadratic does not factorise over the whole numbers, and plenty do not. $x^2 + x + 1$ is one. Knowing that a search can honestly come up empty is part of knowing how to conduct it.

Example 10

Factorise $x^2 + 7x + 12$.

Solution.

Step 1 $a + b = 7$ the sum, matching $x^2 + (a + b)x + ab$
Step 2 $ab = 12$ the product, at the same time
Step 3 only 3 and 4 satisfy both, in either order
Answer $x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)$
Example 11

Factorise $x^2 + 11x + 30$.

Solution.

Step 1 $a + b = 11$ and $ab = 30$ both must hold at once
Step 2 2 and 15 multiply to 30 but add to 17; 3 and 10 add to 13 ruling candidates out
Step 3 the factor pairs of 30 leave 5 and 6
Step 4 $x^2 + 11x + 30 = x^2 + 5x + 6x + 30$ splitting the middle term
Answer $x^2 + 11x + 30 = (x + 5)(x + 6)$
