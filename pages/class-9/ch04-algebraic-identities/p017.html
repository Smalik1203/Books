Example 10

Factorise $x^2 + 7x + 12$.

Compare it with $x^2 + (a + b)x + ab$. Matching terms gives two conditions at once:

sum $a + b = 7$
product $ab = 12$

Only 3 and 4 satisfy both, in either order, so $x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)$.

Example 11

Factorise $x^2 + 11x + 30$.

Here $a + b = 11$ and $ab = 30$, and both must hold at once — which is what rules most candidates out: 2 and 15 multiply to 30 but add to 17, 3 and 10 add to 13. The factor pairs of 30 leave 5 and 6.

$x^2 + 11x + 30 = x^2 + 5x + 6x + 30 = (x + 5)(x + 6)$

So far both numbers have been positive. What changes when a sign does?

Example 12

Factorise $x^2 - 5x + 6$.

The coefficient of $x$ is negative, so comparing with $x^2 + (a + b)x + ab$ gives

sum $a + b = -5$
product $ab = 6$

A positive product with a negative sum means both are negative: $-2$ and $-3$, in either order.

That last step deserves a second look, because it did real work before any candidates were tried. The product 6 is positive, which means the two numbers share a sign — a positive times a negative could never reach it. The sum $-5$ then says which sign they share. Between them the two conditions settled the signs before a single pair was tested.

The saving is larger than it looks. Without that reasoning the factor pairs of 6 are $1$ and $6$, $2$ and $3$, and each may carry either sign — eight candidates. With it there are two.
