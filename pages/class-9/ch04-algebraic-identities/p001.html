Algebraic Identities

4.1Introduction

In earlier chapters you worked with linear polynomials and linear equations, using them to describe and solve real problems.

This chapter takes the next step, into algebraic identities. An identity holds for every value you put into it — unlike an equation such as $x + 5 = 12$, true only for $x = 7$. That is what makes them useful: they turn awkward arithmetic into mental arithmetic, and let you rewrite an expression in whatever form a problem needs.

Example 1

Take any three consecutive square numbers. Add the first and the last, then subtract twice the middle one.

from $\{1, 4, 9\}$: $(1 + 9) - (2 \times 4) = 10 - 8 =$ 2
from $\{9, 16, 25\}$: $(9 + 25) - (2 \times 16) = 34 - 32 =$ 2
from $\{25, 36, 49\}$: $(25 + 49) - (2 \times 36) = 74 - 72 =$ 2

Three different starting points, the same answer each time. Choose your own triple and you will get 2 again.

Arithmetic can keep confirming this for as long as you have patience, but it can never tell you why, nor rule out some enormous triple that breaks the pattern. For that you need algebra — and by the end of this section you will settle it in one line.

An identity is a tool for working smarter: recognise the pattern, and the arithmetic gets shorter.
