The picture explains the arithmetic. The $x^2$ tile and the fifteen unit squares sit in opposite corners, and the eight strips fill the two rectangles left between them — five standing along one side of the square, three lying along the other. Those two counts are the numbers you were hunting for: they multiply to 15 because they frame the block of unit squares, and they add to 8 because they use up every strip.

That is why factorising $x^2 + bx + c$ always comes down to the same question — which two numbers multiply to $c$ and add to $b$?

That question handed you the pieces and asked what they made. The next one goes the other way: it hands you the finished rectangle — its area, and how its two sides are related — and asks what those sides measure.

Example 18

A rectangular pool is 4 metres narrower than it is long, and its surface covers 96 square metres. How long and how wide is it?

Let the length be $x$ metres. The breadth is then $x - 4$ metres, and the area gives an equation:

$x(x - 4) = 96, \quad\text{so}\quad x^2 - 4x - 96 = 0$

Now split the middle term. Two numbers with product $-96$ and sum $-4$: since $(-12) \times 8 = -96$ and $(-12) + 8 = -4$, write $-4x$ as $-12x + 8x$.

$x^2 - 12x + 8x - 96 = x(x - 12) + 8(x - 12) = (x - 12)(x + 8)$

So $(x - 12)(x + 8) = 0$, which means $x - 12 = 0$ or $x + 8 = 0$; that is, $x = 12$ or $x = -8$. But $x$ is the length of a pool, so it cannot be negative. The length is 12 metres and the breadth is $12 - 4 = 8$ metres — and $12 \times 8 = 96$, as required.

Something new happened there. Every earlier use of factorising rewrote an expression; this one solved an equation. The step that allowed it is easy to walk past:

$$\text{if } AB = 0, \text{ then } A = 0 \text{ or } B = 0$$

Nothing like this holds for any other number. That $(x - 12)(x + 8) = 5$ tells you nothing about either bracket, because 5 splits as a product in endlessly many ways — but zero cannot. So the equation had to be rearranged with 0 on one side before factorising.

The last step is worth a habit too: the algebra offered two answers, the situation kept one.
