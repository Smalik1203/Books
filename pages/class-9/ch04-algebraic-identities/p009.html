It earns its keep as the others did: an awkward number often splits neatly into hundreds, tens and units.

Example 9

Find $119^2$.

Split 119 into $100 + 10 + 9$, so $a = 100$, $b = 10$ and $c = 9$:

$119^2 = 100^2 + 10^2 + 9^2 + 2(100)(10) + 2(100)(9) + 2(10)(9)$

$= 10000 + 100 + 81 + 2000 + 1800 + 180$

$= 14161$

Every one of those six numbers is easy on its own. The identity does not make the arithmetic disappear; it breaks one hard multiplication into six easy ones, and easy ones you can do in your head.

Where we have got to

Three identities so far, each verified, each proved, and each usable in both directions — to expand a bracket, to factorise an expression, or to square a number without writing anything down.

The three identities
  1. $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$
  2. $(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$
  3. $(a + b + c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca$

The second came from the first by replacing $b$ with $-b$; the third by grouping two terms into one. Neither needed a fresh argument — and the identities still to come are built the same way, from the ones you already have.
