The chapter is finished; the identities are not. Ten pages of harder work on the same eleven statements follow — no new syllabus, only questions of the kind asked outside school.

Three numbers that add to nothing

The last identity of the chapter looks the most forbidding of the eleven, and is the most useful. Put $x + y + z = 0$ into

$$x^3 + y^3 + z^3 - 3xyz = (x + y + z)\left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - xy - yz - zx\right)$$

and the whole right-hand side collapses, whatever the second bracket may be. So three numbers that add to zero have cubes that add to three times their product. Check it on $1, 1, -2$: the cubes give $-6$, and so does $3(1)(1)(-2)$. Nothing was computed — the answer was read off the condition.

A square is never negative

Every identity in the chapter was an equation. Combine one with the single fact that no square is negative, and out comes an inequality — a statement of a different kind. From $(a - b)^2 \geq 0$,

$$a^2 + b^2 \geq 2ab \quad\text{for every } a \text{ and } b$$

with equality only when $a = b$. Take $a = t$, $b = 1$ and divide by a positive $t$: then $t + \frac{1}{t} \geq 2$, always — which is why the chapter's number-and-reciprocal question offered $\frac{10}{3}$ and not $\frac{1}{3}$.

What three numbers know about three numbers

In Example 15 the three unknowns were never found, and the answer came out anyway. Write $p = x + y + z$, $q = xy + yz + zx$ and $r = xyz$: the chapter's identities turn every symmetric quantity into those three, and into nothing else.
