Now the trade makes sense. Choose $b$ so that $a - b$ and $a + b$ are both round numbers, and the product on the right is one you can do in your head, with $b^2$ small. An awkward square becomes an easy multiplication plus a correction.

Think and Reflect

Check the rearranged rectangle for yourself. How tall is it, and how wide? Multiply those out, add the $b^2$ you set aside, and see whether you get back the area of the original square.

A method from 750 CE

Śhrīdharāchārya set this out as a way to square numbers quickly. To find $55^2$, take $b = 5$, so that the two factors become 60 and 50:

$55^2 = (55 + 5)(55 - 5) + 5^2$

$= 60 \times 50 + 25$

$= 3000 + 25 = 3025$

The whole calculation is $6 \times 5$ and a small addition. What made $b = 5$ the right choice? It is the distance from 55 to the nearest multiple of ten — and that is the rule in general: take $b$ to be whatever carries $a$ to a round number.

Try it on $98^2$. Taking $b = 2$ gives $100 \times 96 + 4$, which is $9604$ — with no two-digit multiplication at all.

Notice how little separates these identities in practice. Each one swaps an expression you cannot handle directly for one you can: a bracket for a sum of terms, a hard multiplication for an easy one. That is the whole business of this chapter.
