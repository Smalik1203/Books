Every rectangle so far has had sides of the form $x + \text{something}$, so exactly one $x^2$-tile appeared each time. Change that and the picture grows. Fig. 4.8 shows a rectangle with sides $2x + 3$ and $3x + 1$: two $x$-tiles along one edge, three along the other, so the $x^2$-tiles now fill a block rather than sit alone.

Count what the picture contains and you have the product without multiplying anything: six large squares of area $x^2$, eleven strips of area $x$, and three unit tiles. That is $6x^2 + 11x + 3$, and the two edge labels are the factors it came from.

Keep the tiles in mind for the section that follows, because factorising is this picture read backwards: you are handed the loose tiles and asked what rectangle they make. Most collections make none.
