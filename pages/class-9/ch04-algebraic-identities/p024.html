4.9Putting Factorisation to Work

Every question in the chapter so far has arrived already stripped down to an expression, with the word factorise attached. Outside a textbook they do not come that way. A question turns up as an area, a volume, a length someone has measured — and factorising is what turns that one measured quantity back into the dimensions that produced it.

Example 17

Saira has one square tile of side $x$ units, eight rectangular strips $x$ units long and 1 unit wide, and fifteen small squares of side 1 unit. She fits all of them together, with no gaps and no overlaps, into a single larger rectangle. What are its sides?

Nothing is said about how the pieces are arranged, and nothing needs to be. Whatever the arrangement, the area of the rectangle is the total area of the tiles:

square $1 \times x^2 = x^2$ sq. units
strips $8 \times x \times 1 = 8x$ sq. units
units $15 \times 1 = 15$ sq. units

So the rectangle has area $x^2 + 8x + 15$ square units. Its sides are two expressions that multiply to give this — which is to say, its factors. Two numbers with sum 8 and product 15: the pair is 3 and 5.

$x^2 + 8x + 15 = (x + 3)(x + 5)$

The rectangle is $x + 5$ units long and $x + 3$ units wide. Fig. 4.11 shows the pieces in place; count them and check that all twenty-four are used.
