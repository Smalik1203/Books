What that means is argued over. It may be a tally, a lunar record, or the earliest surviving trace of someone noticing a pattern among numbers rather than merely recording a count. What is not in doubt is the notches themselves: twenty thousand years ago, a person sat down and wrote a number.

Whether the carver knew what a prime number was, nobody can say. What the bones do settle is that the abstract idea of a number — a thing detachable from the cows or the days it counts — is tens of thousands of years old.

Trade, and very large numbers

As settlements grew into cities, the numbers had to grow with them. In the Indus Valley — at Lothal, at Harappa — standardised weights and measures made trade possible, and a merchant shipping cotton or lapis lazuli to Mesopotamia needed an accounting system he could trust.

Indian thinkers went further than trade required. The Vedas name every power of ten up to $10^{12}$, called parārdha. By the fourth century BCE the Lalitavistara has the Buddha reciting names as far as $10^{53}$ — tallakṣaṇa. Nobody was counting anything that large. They were exploring how far the naming could go, and the habit of writing quantities as powers of ten is exactly what made a place-value system possible later.
