The words in lowest terms are doing real work there. Written as $\tfrac{6}{40}$ the same number looks no different, but $40$ has the same prime factors as $20$, so the test survives cancelling — try it on a fraction that is not in lowest terms and you may get the wrong answer for the right reason.

Going back: from a decimal to a fraction

Every terminating or repeating decimal is a rational number, so each one must be $\tfrac{p}{q}$ for some integers. Finding them takes three different techniques, according to where the repetition starts.

A decimal that terminates needs no technique at all — it can simply be read. One that repeats from the very first digit needs a short piece of algebra. And one that settles into its cycle only after a few stray digits needs the same algebra with one extra step. The three examples that follow take them in that order.

Example 9

Write $0.35$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$.

A terminating decimal needs no algebra. Read it as what it says: 35 hundredths.

two decimal places so the denominator is $100$ $\dfrac{35}{100}$
lowest terms divide both by 5 $\dfrac{7}{20}$

Check by dividing back: $7 \div 20 = 0.35$.

That was the easy direction, and it needed no algebra at all — only a reading of what the decimal already said. The moment the digits stop stopping, that reading is unavailable, and something else has to take its place.
