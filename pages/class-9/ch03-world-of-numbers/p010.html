Example 3

Find $\dfrac{3}{4} + \dfrac{5}{6}$.

Thirds of a thing and quarters of it are not the same size, so the numerators cannot simply be added. Both have to be rewritten in parts of one common size first, and the smallest size that works is the LCM of the denominators.

common denominator LCM of 4 and 6 12
rewrite the first $\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{3 \times 3}{4 \times 3}$ $\dfrac{9}{12}$
rewrite the second $\dfrac{5}{6} = \dfrac{5 \times 2}{6 \times 2}$ $\dfrac{10}{12}$

Now the numerators may be added, because the parts match:

$\dfrac{9}{12} + \dfrac{10}{12} = \dfrac{19}{12}$

Is it in lowest terms? The only factor of 19 is 19 itself, and 19 does not divide 12, so yes. As a check on size: $\tfrac{3}{4}$ and $\tfrac{5}{6}$ are each a little under 1, so the sum should be a little over $1\tfrac{1}{2}$ — and $\tfrac{19}{12} = 1\tfrac{7}{12}$.

The rationals are closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication: combine any two that way and the result is another rational. They are closed under division too, with the one exception you would expect — you may not divide by zero.
