Example 7

Expand $\dfrac{3}{8}$ and $\dfrac{5}{11}$ as decimals.

The first stops. Dividing 3 by 8 gives remainders 6, then 4, then 0 — and once the remainder is 0 there is nothing left to bring down.

$\dfrac{3}{8} = 0.375$

The second never does. Dividing 5 by 11 gives remainder 5, then 6, then 5 again — and the moment a remainder comes back, every step after it must repeat what happened before.

$\dfrac{5}{11} = 0.454545\ldots = 0.\overline{45}$

The bar marks the block that repeats for ever.

The second case is not bad luck; it is forced. Dividing by 11, the only possible remainders are $1, 2, \ldots, 10$ — ten of them, and zero would have ended the division. Keep going and a remainder must eventually recur, because there are only finitely many to choose from. Once one does, the whole cycle starts again.

That argument does not care which number you divide by. It proves that every rational number has a decimal that either terminates or repeats — there is no third possibility.

Telling which, without dividing

You can predict which of the two will happen by looking at the denominator alone — no long division needed. The reason is that a decimal is a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten.

Write out what that means. $0.375$ is $\dfrac{375}{1000}$, and $0.15$ is $\dfrac{15}{100}$. So a decimal that terminates is exactly a number that can be written over some $10^n$ — and $10^n = 2^n \times 5^n$ carries no prime factor except 2 and 5.

Now run that backwards. Suppose $\dfrac{p}{q}$ is in lowest terms and can be rewritten over a power of ten. Then $q$ can only have been built out of 2s and 5s in the first place: a factor of 3, or 7, or 11 sitting in $q$ would still be sitting there afterwards, and $10^n$ has no matching factor to cancel it against. The test on the next page is that one observation, stated once and used ever after.
