Example 6

Take the first few terms of Mādhava's series in turn and watch the running total.

Solution.

Step 1 $4(1) = 4.000$ 1 term
Step 2 $4\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = 4 \times \tfrac{2}{3} = 2.667$ 2 terms
Step 3 $4 \times \tfrac{13}{15} = 3.467$ 3 terms
Step 4 $4 \times \tfrac{76}{105} = 2.895$ 4 terms
Step 5 $4 \times \tfrac{263}{315} = 3.340$ 5 terms
Answer $4.000$, $2.667$, $3.467$, $2.895$, $3.340$ — closing in on $3.14159\ldots$ from alternate sides

The totals swing above and below, and the swings get smaller. Every partial sum is rational, being a finite sum of fractions. Only the value they approach is not.

Do not mistake this for a fast method. After 500 terms the total is $3.1396$ — barely two decimal places. Mādhava's series is a statement about what $\pi$ is, not a good way to work it out.

3.6Real Numbers and Their Decimals

Put the dense web of rationals together with the irrationals that fill its gaps and you get the real numbers, $\mathbb{R}$ — a line with nothing missing from it anywhere.

Which raises a practical question. Handed a number, how do you tell which kind it is? The quickest test is to look at its decimal expansion, because the two kinds behave quite differently there.

One caution before the test itself. Nobody has ever seen a whole decimal expansion, and nobody ever will: the digits of a repeating decimal go on for ever, and so do the digits of an irrational one. Reading digits off a calculator settles nothing: a hundred of them with no cycle are perfectly consistent with a cycle beginning at the hundred and first.

So the test is a criterion rather than a procedure. It says what would be true of the expansion if you could see all of it, and it is applied by knowing where the number came from rather than by inspecting a screen.

Terminating and repeating

Divide the numerator of a rational number by its denominator and exactly one of two things happens.

Either the division runs out — a remainder of 0 appears, there is nothing further to bring down, and the decimal simply stops. Or it never runs out, in which case the digits settle into a block that repeats for ever.
