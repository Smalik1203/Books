In an assertion–reason question, answer from this list every time: (a) both true, R explains A; (b) both true, R does not explain A; (c) A true, R false; (d) A false, R true.

B6 · Competitive-Style Thinking
  1. Assertion. Every terminating decimal is a rational number.  Reason. A decimal with $n$ places can be written over $10^n$.
  2. Assertion. $\sqrt{9}$ is irrational.  Reason. The square root of a natural number is irrational unless that number is a perfect square.
  3. Which of the following hold for $\dfrac{13}{125}$?
    1. Its decimal terminates.
    2. It terminates after exactly two places.
    3. $125 = 5^3$, and that is what decides the matter.
    Choose: (a) (i) only   (b) (i) and (iii)   (c) (ii) and (iii)   (d) all three.
  4. Write $0.4\overline{27}$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms. Name the job each of your two multipliers does, and say what the denominator you end with records about the decimal you started from.
  5. A student tabulates four fractions and their decimals. One row is wrong. Find it, name the mistake, and give the right value.
    Fraction$\dfrac{3}{8}$$\dfrac{5}{16}$$\dfrac{7}{25}$$\dfrac{9}{40}$
    Decimal$0.375$$0.3125$$0.028$$0.225$
  6. A student measures a square of side $1.000$ m and finds its diagonal to be $1.414$ m. He concludes that the diagonal is $\frac{1414}{1000}$, and therefore rational. Say what his measurement does establish, what it cannot establish, and what kind of argument settles the question instead.
  7. Let $a = 0.\overline{6}$, $b = 0.1\overline{6}$ and $c = 0.\overline{16}$. Write each as a fraction in lowest terms, then put the three in increasing order. Two of them are close enough that the decimals alone should not satisfy you.
