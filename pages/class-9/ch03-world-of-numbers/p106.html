Solution 4

Option (b) is a difference of squares, the identity from Chapter 4, and the roots disappear when it is multiplied out:

$\left(\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{3}\right)\left(\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{3}\right) = 5 - 3 = 2$

So (b) is rational, and it is the answer. Option (a) was settled in the second question of this section: it is irrational. Option (c) is $\pi$ with a rational taken away, and a rational subtracted from an irrational leaves an irrational, for the same reason a rational added to one does.

Option (d) is $\sqrt{6}$, which is irrational. It is worth being clear about why it has to be checked rather than assumed: two irrational numbers multiplied can perfectly well give a rational. $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{8} = 4$. What makes a product of surds rational is not the surds but what is under them, and $6$ is not a perfect square.

Problem 5 · Try it first

How many numbers of the form $\dfrac{k}{24}$, with $k$ a whole number, lie strictly between $\dfrac{1}{4}$ and $\dfrac{1}{3}$?

  1. $0$
  2. $1$
  3. $2$
  4. infinitely many
Solution 5

Put the two ends over $24$ as well, and the counting is done for you:

$\dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{6}{24}$,   $\dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{8}{24}$

Strictly between $6$ and $8$ there is one whole number, so $k = 7$ and the answer is (b). Option (c) counts $6$ and $8$ themselves, which the word strictly rules out.

Option (d) is the one to think about. Infinitely many rationals do lie between $\dfrac{1}{4}$ and $\dfrac{1}{3}$ — that is what density says, and the previous question used it. But this question fixes the denominator at $24$, and a fixed denominator leaves only whole numbers to choose. Density is a statement about all denominators at once; take that freedom away and the count becomes finite, and usually small.
