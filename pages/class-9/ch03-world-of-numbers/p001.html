3.1The Dawn of Counting

Long before anyone built a city, wrote a law or charted a star, there was a plainer need: to keep count. Mathematics did not begin in a classroom with equations on a board. It began in the dirt, on bark, and on bone.

Imagine living some thousands of years ago in a settlement on the banks of the Saraswati. You keep cattle. Every morning they go out to graze and every evening they come back. How do you know that none has wandered off? You have no words for numbers and no symbols to write them with — and you still solve it.

For every cow that leaves, drop one pebble into a pot. For every cow that returns, take one out. If the pot is empty at dusk, the herd is whole; if pebbles remain, some are missing. Nothing here is counted in our sense. One thing is simply matched against another, which is what one-to-one correspondence means — and it is where the natural numbers $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, \dots\}$ come from.

The herder never needs to know how many cows there are. Matching answers the question without counting — which is why it came first.

A history written in bone

The decimal place-value system we use today was perfected in the Indian subcontinent. But the oldest surviving evidence of a human being recording a number takes us to Africa, and the first mathematicians wrote not on paper but in notches cut into bone.

The Lebombo bone, found in the Lebombo mountains between South Africa and Eswatini, is about 35,000 years old. It carries 29 notches, deliberately cut and evenly sized. Twenty-nine is close enough to the length of a lunar month that most scholars read it as a tally of days — a calendar, kept in natural numbers.

The Ishango bone, from near the headwaters of the Nile in the Democratic Republic of Congo, is younger — about 20,000 BCE — and stranger. It carries three columns of notches, and the groupings are not random. One column runs 11, 13, 17, 19: every prime between 10 and 20, and nothing else. Another column doubles.

What that means is argued over. It may be a tally, a lunar record, or the earliest surviving trace of someone noticing a pattern among numbers rather than merely recording a count. What is not in doubt is the notches themselves: twenty thousand years ago, a person sat down and wrote a number.
