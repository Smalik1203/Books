Why must the definition insist that $q \neq 0$? Try to say what would go wrong, rather than only that it is not allowed.

A quotient $\tfrac{p}{q}$ is meant to be the number which, times $q$, gives back $p$. Ask whether any number does that when $q = 0$ — first when $p$ is not zero, then when it is. The two cases fail differently, and both failures matter.

How rationals are combined

Brahmagupta gave rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions too, and noticed that they hold for the negative ones exactly as they do for the positive. Four laws cover everything.

Equality. $\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}$ exactly when $ad = bc$.

Addition. Put both over the same denominator, then $\dfrac{a}{b} + \dfrac{c}{b} = \dfrac{a+c}{b}$, and likewise for subtraction.

Multiplication and division. $\dfrac{a}{b} \times \dfrac{c}{d} = \dfrac{ac}{bd}$, and $\dfrac{a}{b} \div \dfrac{c}{d} = \dfrac{a}{b} \times \dfrac{d}{c} = \dfrac{ad}{bc}$, provided no denominator is zero.

Order and grouping. Addition and multiplication are commutative, and multiplication distributes over addition: $p(q + r) = pq + pr$.
