$\pi$, and what an infinite sum is for

The other famous irrational is $\pi$, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. For centuries the effort went into better and better fractions for it. Āryabhaṭa (499 CE) gave $\dfrac{3927}{1250} = 3.1416$, which is very good — and he was careful to call it an āsanna, an approximation, suspecting that no fraction would ever be exact.

He was right, though it took until 1761 for Lambert to prove it. And that leaves a real problem: if no fraction can name $\pi$, and no finite list of fractions either, what could possibly give it exactly?

Mādhava of Sangamagrāma, who founded the Kerala school in the fourteenth century, saw the answer. If a finite expression will not do, use one that never finishes:

$$\pi = 4\left(1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \cdots\right)$$

We know what adding a hundred numbers means. What does adding infinitely many mean? It means watching the running total as more terms are taken, and asking what value it settles towards.

Example 6

Take the first few terms of Mādhava's series in turn and watch the running total.

1 term $4(1)$ $4.000$
2 terms $4\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = 4 \times \tfrac{2}{3}$ $2.667$
3 terms $4 \times \tfrac{13}{15}$ $3.467$
4 terms $4 \times \tfrac{76}{105}$ $2.895$
5 terms $4 \times \tfrac{263}{315}$ $3.340$

The totals swing above and below, and the swings get smaller: $4.000$, $2.667$, $3.467$, $2.895$, $3.340$ — closing in on $3.14159\ldots$ from alternate sides. Every partial sum is rational, being a finite sum of fractions. Only the value they approach is not.

Do not mistake this for a fast method. After 500 terms the total is $3.1396$ — barely two decimal places. Mādhava's series is a statement about what $\pi$ is, not a good way to compute it.
