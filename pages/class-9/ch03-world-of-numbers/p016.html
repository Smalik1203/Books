Example 6

Take the first few terms of Mādhava's series in turn and watch the running total.

1 term $4(1)$ $4.000$
2 terms $4\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = 4 \times \tfrac{2}{3}$ $2.667$
3 terms $4 \times \tfrac{13}{15}$ $3.467$
4 terms $4 \times \tfrac{76}{105}$ $2.895$
5 terms $4 \times \tfrac{263}{315}$ $3.340$

The totals swing above and below, and the swings get smaller: $4.000$, $2.667$, $3.467$, $2.895$, $3.340$ — closing in on $3.14159\ldots$ from alternate sides. Every partial sum is rational, being a finite sum of fractions. Only the value they approach is not.

Do not mistake this for a fast method. After 500 terms the total is $3.1396$ — barely two decimal places. Mādhava's series is a statement about what $\pi$ is, not a good way to compute it.

3.6Real Numbers and Their Decimals

Put the dense web of rationals together with the irrationals that fill its gaps and you get the real numbers, $\mathbb{R}$ — a line with nothing missing from it anywhere.

Which raises a practical question. Handed a number, how do you tell which kind it is? The quickest test is to look at its decimal expansion, because the two kinds behave quite differently there.
