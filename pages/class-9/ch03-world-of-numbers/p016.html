Putting $\sqrt{2}$ on the line

We have a number that is not a ratio. Does it still have a place on the number line? It does, and a ruler and compass will find it exactly — not approximately, and without a single decimal.

Step 1 Mark $OA = 1$ unit on the line, and raise a perpendicular at $A$.
Step 2 On it mark $B$ with $AB = 1$ unit, and join $OB$. Then $OB = \sqrt{2}$, by the theorem again.
Step 3 With centre $O$ and radius $OB$, swing an arc down to the line. It cuts it at $P$, and $OP = \sqrt{2}$.
Think and Reflect
?

Extend the method to $\sqrt{3}$: what should the two legs of the right triangle be? Then $\sqrt{5}$.

Now generalise. Given a segment of length $\sqrt{n}$ already built, how would you get $\sqrt{n+1}$ from it — and what does repeating that for ever draw on the page?

The answer to that last question is worth drawing. Build the triangle for $\sqrt{2}$; then stand a new unit perpendicular on its hypotenuse and join back to the origin, which gives $\sqrt{3}$; then do it again. Each new hypotenuse is $\sqrt{n+1}$, because its legs are $\sqrt{n}$ and 1, and the theorem does the rest.

Two things about that are worth noticing. The construction never approximates: at no point is $\sqrt{2}$ replaced by $1.414$. The arc puts the length down exactly, and the compass is entirely untroubled by the fact that the number has no finite decimal. And it never runs out — every whole number, however large, has its square root placed on the line by a finite number of steps.
