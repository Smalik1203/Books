Why $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational

Suppose it were rational. Then it could be written as a fraction in lowest terms — and everything that follows is squeezed out of those two words, lowest terms.

Step 1 Assume $\sqrt{2} = \dfrac{p}{q}$, with $p$, $q$ integers, $q \neq 0$, and $p$, $q$ co-prime.
Step 2 Square both sides: $2 = \dfrac{p^2}{q^2}$.
Step 3 Multiply by $q^2$: $\;2q^2 = p^2$.
Step 4 $p^2$ is twice an integer, so $p^2$ is even; and if a square is even the number itself must be. So $p$ is even — write $p = 2k$.
Step 5 Substitute: $2q^2 = (2k)^2 = 4k^2$.
Step 6 Divide by 2: $\;q^2 = 2k^2$.
Step 7 By the same reasoning as Step 4, $q^2$ is even, so $q$ is even.

And there is the collapse. Step 4 makes $p$ even, Step 7 makes $q$ even — so both are divisible by 2, and share a common factor. But Step 1 said they share none. Both cannot be true.

Every step from 2 to 7 is forced; none of them can be the mistake. So the mistake is the only thing not forced — the assumption itself. $\sqrt{2}$ is not a ratio of integers.

Notice what the proof never does: it never computes a single decimal place of $\sqrt{2}$. No amount of computing could have settled the question anyway.

Think and Reflect
?

Run the same argument for $\sqrt{3}$. Then try it on $\sqrt{5}$ and $\sqrt{7}$.

Now try it on $\sqrt{9}$ — where it must fail, since $\sqrt{9} = 3$. Find the exact step that breaks, and you will have found what the argument was really using about 2, 3, 5 and 7.

Putting $\sqrt{2}$ on the line

We have a number that is not a ratio. Does it still have a place on the number line? It does, and a ruler and compass will find it exactly — not approximately, and without a single decimal.
