3.2When Nothing Became Something

For most of history the number line began at 1. If you had five apples and gave all five away, there was no number for what you had left. You simply had none — a state, not a quantity.

Several civilisations got part of the way. The Babylonians and the Mayans used a placeholder: a mark meaning this column is empty, so that 205 could be told from 25. But a placeholder is punctuation — you cannot add it to seven or multiply it by three. It marks an absence; it is not yet a number.

From emptiness to a number

The step that was missing was conceptual, and India had been taking it for a thousand years in another discipline entirely. In the Upanishads and in Buddhist literature from the seventh century BCE onward, śūnyatā — emptiness — is not a lack but a state to be reached: the stilling of the vṛttis, the fluctuations of the mind.

A culture that treats emptiness as a state worth naming and working towards already has the habit of mind that lets nothing be a thing in its own right. The word śūnya means zero. The idea travelled from philosophy into architecture, into linguistics, and at last into mathematics.

You can watch the symbol arrive. The Bakhshālī manuscript, dated to the early centuries CE, marks zero with a bold dot — a bindu. But a mark on birch bark is still only a mark until somebody says what may be done with it.
