3.5Numbers That Are Not Ratios

The rationals are dense. Between any two of them lies another, and between those, another again. It is very hard to look at that and not conclude that they must fill the line completely, leaving no gap anywhere. For a long time that is exactly what was believed.

It is false, and the counter-example is embarrassingly easy to draw.

When Baudhāyana composed his Śulbasūtra around 800 BCE — a manual for laying out fire altars — he met lengths that no ratio would name. The Greeks ran into the same wall a few centuries later. The offending shape is a square of side 1.

By the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem the diagonal $d$ satisfies $1^2 + 1^2 = d^2$, so $d^2 = 2$ and $d = \sqrt{2}$. The length is perfectly definite — you can draw it with a ruler, and it is shorter than $1.5$ and longer than $1.4$. The question is whether any ratio of whole numbers names it.

It does not, and the argument that shows so is one of the oldest and cleanest in mathematics. It is credited to Hippasus of the Pythagorean school, around 400 BCE, and it works by contradiction: assume the opposite of what you want, and follow it until it collapses.

Irrational number

A number on the number line that cannot be written as a ratio of two integers is called an irrational number.
