Example 10

Write $0.\overline{6}$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$.

Now the digits never stop, so there is nothing to read off. The trick is to make two numbers whose infinite tails are identical, and then subtract, so that the tails cancel and leave something finite behind.

Solution.

Step 1 Let $x = 0.6666\ldots$
Step 2 $\;10x = 6.6666\ldots$ one digit repeats: multiply by $10^1$
Step 3 $\;10x - x = 6.6666\ldots - 0.6666\ldots$; both tails are $.6666\ldots$ subtract: the tails vanish
Step 4 $9x = 6$
Answer $x = \dfrac{6}{9} = \dfrac{2}{3}$
Check $2 \div 3 = 0.6666\ldots$

Notice why the multiplier was 10 and not 100 — one repeating digit needs a shift of exactly one place, or the tails would not have lined up.

The multiplier there was chosen to match the cycle: one repeating digit, one shift. That is not a rule to memorise but the reason the two tails lined up, and a longer cycle will need a longer shift.

It is worth saying plainly what has just been done, because the same move settles every repeating decimal there is. An infinite tail cannot be subtracted away on its own — but two numbers carrying the identical tail can be subtracted from each other, and the tails go together. Choosing the multiplier is nothing more than arranging for those two tails to match.

It is worth seeing the move fail once, because that is what shows which part of it was doing the work. Suppose the same shift of one place were tried on $0.\overline{45}$, whose cycle is two digits long.

$10x = 4.545454\ldots$  and  $x = 0.454545\ldots$

$9x = 4.090909\ldots$

Nothing is wrong with that line — it is perfectly true — but it has not got us anywhere, because the right-hand side is still an infinite decimal. The tails did not match, so they did not cancel, and we are left with the same kind of object we began with. The multiplier has to move the number by a whole cycle, and that is the only thing being asked of it.
