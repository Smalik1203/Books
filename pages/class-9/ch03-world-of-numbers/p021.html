In both of those the repetition began immediately after the decimal point. Very often it does not — a digit or two occurs once and only then does the cycle start, and a single shift can no longer bring the tails into agreement.

Example 12

Write $0.1\overline{6}$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$.

This one does not repeat immediately: the 1 occurs once and only the 6 recurs. One shift will not do, because the two numbers must agree in their tails and the tails only match once the non-repeating part is out of the way. So shift twice, for two different reasons.

Step 1 Let $x = 0.16666\ldots$
Step 2 One digit does not repeat. Multiply by $10^1$ to push it past the point: $\;10x = 1.6666\ldots$
Step 3 One digit repeats. Multiply by $10^1$ again to move a whole cycle: $\;100x = 16.6666\ldots$
Step 4 Subtract the Step 2 line from the Step 3 line — both end in $.6666\ldots$: $\;90x = 15$.
Step 5 $x = \dfrac{15}{90} = \dfrac{1}{6}$.

Check: $1 \div 6 = 0.16666\ldots$ The two multipliers had different jobs — the first cleared the non-repeating digit, the second moved one full cycle — and it is worth naming which is which as you go.

The two multipliers in that example were doing quite different jobs. The general case does both at once, with a whole-number part along for the ride, and needs no new idea.
