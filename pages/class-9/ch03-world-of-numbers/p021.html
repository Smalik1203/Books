The two multipliers in that example were doing quite different jobs. The general case does both at once, with a whole-number part along for the ride, and needs no new idea.

Example 13

Write $2.35\overline{7}$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$.

The general case, with everything at once: a whole part, two non-repeating digits and a one-digit cycle. The method of Example 12 handles it unchanged — count the two kinds of digit, and use one multiplier for each.

Step 1 Let $x = 2.35777\ldots$; the block $35$ does not repeat (2 digits), the $7$ does (1 digit).
Step 2 Clear the 2 non-repeating digits with $10^2$: $\;100x = 235.777\ldots$
Step 3 Move 1 full cycle with a further $10^1$: $\;1000x = 2357.777\ldots$
Step 4 Subtract: $\;900x = 2357.777\ldots - 235.777\ldots = 2122$.
Step 5 $x = \dfrac{2122}{900} = \dfrac{1061}{450}$, dividing both by 2.

Check: $1061 \div 450 = 2.35777\ldots$ And notice the denominator that emerged — $900 = 9 \times 100$. The nine comes from the single repeating digit, the hundred from the two that do not repeat. The shape of the answer records the shape of the decimal.
