One chapter, five kinds of number, and a single pattern running through all of them: each was invented because a perfectly sensible question had no answer among the numbers that already existed.

What this chapter established
  1. The natural numbers came from matching, not counting — one pebble to one cow — and the oldest surviving record of them is cut into bone tens of thousands of years old.
  2. Śūnya became a number when Brahmagupta gave it rules in 628 CE. A placeholder marks an absence; a number can be added to, subtracted from and multiplied.
  3. The integers extend the line left of zero. Brahmagupta's laws of signs were justified by debts and fortunes, and we still use them unchanged.
  4. The rational numbers are the ratios $\dfrac{p}{q}$ with $q \neq 0$. They are closed under all four operations, division by zero excepted, and they are dense: between any two lies another, and therefore infinitely many.
  5. Density is not enough to fill the line. $\sqrt{2}$ is the length of a diagonal you can draw, yet no ratio names it — proved by contradiction, without computing a single decimal place.
  6. The real numbers are the rationals and the irrationals together, and they leave no gaps.
  7. A decimal expansion is a signature. Terminating or repeating means rational; non-terminating and non-repeating means irrational. Which of the first two happens is decided by the prime factors of the denominator: only 2s and 5s terminate.
  8. Any terminating or repeating decimal can be turned back into a fraction by shifting and subtracting, so that the infinite tails cancel and leave something finite behind.
