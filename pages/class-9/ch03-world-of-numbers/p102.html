Now $50 = 2 \times 5^2$ has no prime factor but $2$ and $5$, so the decimal terminates, and it stops after two places. The $3$ in $150$ was never part of the number. It belonged to one way of writing it, and cancelling removed it. Cancel first, then read the denominator.

A number $x$ has $x^2 = 1 + \sqrt{2}$. Given that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, show that $x$ is irrational too.

The first question was settled by supposing the opposite, and the same opening works here, even though $x$ itself is never found. Suppose $x$ were rational. The rationals are closed under multiplication, so $x \times x = x^2$ would be rational as well, and

$$\sqrt{2} = x^2 - 1$$

The right side is a difference of two rationals, so $\sqrt{2}$ would be rational, and it is not. The supposition fails, and $x$ is irrational.

What the argument used is worth stating on its own: the square of a rational number is rational. So a number whose square is irrational must be irrational itself. The statement does not turn round. $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, and its square is $2$.

Write $0.4\overline{27}$ as $\dfrac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms. Say what each multiplier you use is for.

One digit does not repeat and two do, so two multipliers are needed and they do different jobs. Put $x = 0.4272727\ldots$ The first multiplier pushes the part that does not repeat past the point. The second moves the repeating block on by exactly one cycle, so that the two tails match and cancel:

$10x = 4.272727\ldots$

$1000x = 427.272727\ldots$

$990x = 423$,  so  $x = \dfrac{423}{990} = \dfrac{47}{110}$

Look at the denominator before the cancelling: $990$ is two $9$s and one $0$, one $9$ for each repeating digit and one $0$ for the digit that does not. The denominator is a record of the decimal's shape. And $110 = 2 \times 5 \times 11$, whose factor of $11$ is what keeps the decimal from terminating — the same test as in the second question, read in the other direction.

A student says there is no rational number between $\dfrac{2}{7}$ and $\dfrac{3}{7}$, since no whole number lies between the numerators $2$ and $3$. Is the student right?

No, and one example settles it. The average of the two, $\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{2}{7} + \tfrac{3}{7}\right) = \tfrac{5}{14}$, lies strictly between them, since $\tfrac{2}{7} = \tfrac{4}{14}$ and $\tfrac{3}{7} = \tfrac{6}{14}$. So the claim was false.
