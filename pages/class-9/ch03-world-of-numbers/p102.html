The fraction is in lowest terms, and $80 = 2^4 \times 5$. The only prime factors are $2$ and $5$, so the decimal terminates. To see where it stops, do what Example 8 did and turn the denominator into a power of ten. There are four $2$s and one $5$, so three more $5$s are needed.

$$\dfrac{7}{80} = \dfrac{7 \times 125}{2^4 \times 5^4} = \dfrac{875}{10000} = 0.0875$$

It stops after four places. Notice that the four came from $2^4$, the larger of the two powers. The single $5$ was matched by the $2$s without any help. A denominator of $2^3 \times 5$ would give three places, and so would $5^3$ on its own. We knew the length of the decimal before we knew any of its digits.

Try it first

Given that $\sqrt{6}$ is irrational, show that $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is irrational too.

The first question was settled by supposing the opposite, and the same opening works here. Suppose $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ were a rational $r$. Squaring both sides of an equation keeps it true, and squaring a sum of two roots is the one move that reduces the number of roots:

$$\left(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\right)^2 = 2 + 2\sqrt{6} + 3 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$$

So $5 + 2\sqrt{6} = r^2$, and rearranging gives $\sqrt{6} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(r^2 - 5\right)$. The right side is built from a rational by subtracting and halving, and the rationals are closed under both. So $\sqrt{6}$ would be rational, and it is not. The supposition fails.

The identity that did the work is $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ from Chapter 4, and what makes it useful here is that $a^2$ and $b^2$ come out whole and only the middle term keeps a root. Squaring is worth trying on any sum of two surds for exactly that reason.
