Absolute value

The absolute value of a rational number $x$, written $|x|$, is its distance from 0 on the number line. Distance has no direction, so the sign is discarded.

Example 4

Find $\left|\dfrac{5}{3}\right|$, $\left|-\dfrac{5}{3}\right|$ and $|0|$. Then find the distance between $-4$ and $3$ on the number line.

Take each as a question about distance, not about signs.

$\left|\dfrac{5}{3}\right|$ already to the right of 0 $\dfrac{5}{3}$
$\left|-\dfrac{5}{3}\right|$ the same distance, other side $\dfrac{5}{3}$
$|0|$ no distance at all $0$

For the distance between two numbers, subtract and take the absolute value of what you get — $|a - b|$. Here $a = 3$ and $b = -4$:

$|a - b| = |3 - (-4)| = |3 + 4| = |7| = 7$

Seven units, and the order does not matter: $|b - a| = |-7| = 7$ gives the same answer, as a distance must. Note that $|x| \geq 0$ always — the one thing an absolute value can never be is negative.

How crowded the rationals are

Between 1 and 2 there is $\tfrac{3}{2}$. Between 1 and $\tfrac{3}{2}$ there is $\tfrac{5}{4}$. Between 1 and $\tfrac{5}{4}$ there is $\tfrac{9}{8}$ — and the process has no reason to stop.

Take any two rationals $a$ and $b$ with $a < b$. Their average $\dfrac{a+b}{2}$ is a rational number, since the rationals are closed under addition and division; and it lies strictly between them. So between any two rationals there is another, and therefore infinitely many. We say the rationals are dense.
