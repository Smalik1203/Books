Example 8

Will $\dfrac{3}{20}$ terminate? Decide without dividing, then check.

A terminating decimal is one that can be written over $10$, $100$, $1000$ and so on. So the question is whether $20$ can be turned into a power of ten by multiplying it by something.

factorise 20 $20 = 2^2 \times 5$
a power of ten is $10^n = 2^n \times 5^n$
what is missing one more 5, to match the two 2s $\times \dfrac{5}{5}$

So multiply top and bottom by 5, which changes nothing:

$\dfrac{3}{20} = \dfrac{3 \times 5}{2^2 \times 5 \times 5} = \dfrac{15}{100} = 0.15$

It terminates, in two places. Had the denominator contained any prime other than 2 or 5 — a 3, say, or an 11 — no multiplier could ever have cleared it, because a power of ten contains no such factor to cancel against.

Which decimals terminate

Let $\dfrac{p}{q}$ be a rational number in lowest terms. Its decimal expansion terminates exactly when the only prime factors of $q$ are 2 and 5. Otherwise it repeats.

The words in lowest terms are doing real work there. Written as $\tfrac{6}{40}$ the same number looks no different, but $40$ has the same prime factors as $20$, so the test survives cancelling — try it on a fraction that is not in lowest terms and you may get the wrong answer for the right reason.

Going back: from a decimal to a fraction

Every terminating or repeating decimal is a rational number, so each one must be $\tfrac{p}{q}$ for some integers. Finding them takes three different techniques, according to where the repetition starts.
