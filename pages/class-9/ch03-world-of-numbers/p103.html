$10x = 4.272727\ldots$

$1000x = 427.272727\ldots$

$990x = 423$,  so  $x = \dfrac{423}{990} = \dfrac{47}{110}$

Look at the denominator before the cancelling: $990$ is two $9$s and one $0$, one $9$ for each repeating digit and one $0$ for the digit that does not. The denominator is a record of the decimal's shape. And $110 = 2 \times 5 \times 11$, whose factor of $11$ is what keeps the decimal from terminating — the same test as in the second question, read in the other direction.

Try it first

A student says there are only two rationals between $\dfrac{1}{3}$ and $\dfrac{1}{2}$, namely $\dfrac{2}{6}$ and $\dfrac{3}{6}$, since over $6$ there is nothing in between. Is the student right?

No, and one example settles it. The average of the two, $\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2}\right) = \tfrac{5}{12}$, lies strictly between them and is neither of the two named. So the list was not complete.

What went wrong is worth naming. The student checked one denominator and reported the answer for all of them. Over $6$ there is indeed nothing between $\tfrac{2}{6}$ and $\tfrac{3}{6}$; over $12$ there is $\tfrac{5}{12}$, and over $120$ there are nineteen. Averaging can be done again on the new pair, and again, without ever running out, which is what it means to say the rationals are dense. A check on one case never settles a claim about every case — and here the claim was false, so one case was enough to bring it down.

Look back at the five questions. Three were settled by supposing the opposite and letting closure do the rest; one was settled by reading the denominator instead of dividing; and one was brought down by a single example. Not one of them needed a decimal place to be written out. That is the shape of most questions about number: decide what kind of number you are dealing with, and the arithmetic either becomes easy or turns out to be unnecessary.
