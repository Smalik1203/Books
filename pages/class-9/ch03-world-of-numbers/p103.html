Connect the Concepts
Two results of the chapter, one after the other.

A question about number is rarely only about number. Each of these reaches into a chapter you have met, or out of the book altogether. Name both results as you go.

  1. Chapter 1 measured the distance between two points. Take $A(0, 0)$ and $B(1, 1)$.
    1. Find $AB$, and say which section of this chapter proves that the answer is not a fraction.
    2. Give a pair of points with whole-number coordinates whose distance apart is a whole number, and say what has to be true of the two shifts.
  2. Chapter 4 proved $(x + y)(x - y) = x^2 - y^2$. Use it on $\left(\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{3}\right)\left(\sqrt{5} - \sqrt{3}\right)$, and hence write $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{5} + \sqrt{3}}$ with no root in its denominator. Which of the two numbers you have written is easier to place on a number line, and why?
  3. Chapter 2 drew the line $y = ax$. Let $a = \sqrt{2}$.
    1. Does the line pass through any point other than the origin whose coordinates are both whole numbers?
    2. Answer the same question for $a = \frac{3}{4}$, and say what the difference between the two cases really is.
  4. Chapter 5 defines $\pi$ as the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. A ratio of two lengths — and yet $\pi$ is irrational. Explain why there is no contradiction, and say what the word ratio means in each of the two sentences.
  5. A scientist reports a measurement as $2.30$ m rather than $2.3$ m.
    1. What is being claimed by the extra zero?
    2. No measurement can ever show that a length is irrational. Say why, and what kind of argument can.
  6. Mādhava's series gives $\pi$ exactly and computes it terribly: five hundred terms reach only $3.1396$. Every partial sum is rational and the limit is not. Say how a list of rationals can approach something that is not one, and name the everyday object in this chapter that does the same thing digit by digit.
