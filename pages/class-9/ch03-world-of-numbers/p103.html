What went wrong is worth naming. The student checked one denominator and reported the answer for all of them. Over $7$ there is indeed nothing between $\tfrac{2}{7}$ and $\tfrac{3}{7}$; over $14$ there is $\tfrac{5}{14}$, and over $70$ there are nine. Averaging can be done again on the new pair, and again, without ever running out, which is what it means to say the rationals are dense. A check on one case never settles a claim about every case — and here the claim was false, so one case was enough to bring it down.

That averaging has a consequence worth stating on its own. Between any two rationals there is a third, so no rational has a next one. Ask for the rational immediately after $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and there is no answer: whatever you name, the average of it and $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is nearer.

The integers are not like this at all. After $7$ comes $8$, with nothing between them, and every integer has a definite neighbour on each side. Extending the naturals to the rationals bought the missing subtractions and divisions, and it cost this — the idea of the next number — which had seemed too basic to be worth naming until it was gone.

Look back at the five questions. Two were settled by supposing the opposite and letting closure do the rest, and one of those never found the number it was about. Two were settled by reading a denominator, once to see whether a decimal stops and once to see why it does not. And one was brought down by a single example. Not one of them needed a long division to be done. That is the shape of most questions about number: decide what kind of number you are dealing with, and the arithmetic either becomes easy or turns out to be unnecessary.

Example 1

A submarine is at a depth of $120$ m, which is written $-120$ m. It rises $45$ m, and then dives three times as far as it rose. At what depth does it end?

Solution.

Step 1 a rise is $+45$; the dive is $3 \times (-45) = -135$ a fortune times a debt
Step 2 $-120 + 45 = -75$ after the rise
Step 3 $-75 + (-135) = -210$ a debt plus a debt
Answer It ends at $-210$ m, a depth of $210$ m
