How crowded the rationals are

Between 1 and 2 there is $\tfrac{3}{2}$. Between 1 and $\tfrac{3}{2}$ there is $\tfrac{5}{4}$. Between 1 and $\tfrac{5}{4}$ there is $\tfrac{9}{8}$ — and the process has no reason to stop.

Take any two rationals $a$ and $b$ with $a < b$. Their average $\dfrac{a+b}{2}$ is a rational number, since the rationals are closed under addition and division; and it lies strictly between them. So between any two rationals there is another, and therefore infinitely many. We say the rationals are dense.

Example 5

Find three rational numbers lying strictly between $-\dfrac{1}{2}$ and $\dfrac{1}{4}$.

Averaging would give them one at a time. It is quicker to put both over one denominator and read off the numerators in between — but the first attempt shows why the denominator has to be chosen with care.

common denominator $-\dfrac{1}{2} = -\dfrac{2}{4}$ and $\dfrac{1}{4}$ only $0$ between

Between the numerators $-2$ and $1$ there is just one whole number to spare, and we need three. So widen the denominator — multiply top and bottom of both by 4, which changes neither number:

widened $-\dfrac{8}{16}$ and $\dfrac{4}{16}$ $11$ numerators between

Now take any three of them, say $-7$, $-4$ and $1$:

$-\dfrac{7}{16}, \qquad -\dfrac{4}{16} = -\dfrac{1}{4}, \qquad \dfrac{1}{16}$

Check the ends rather than trusting the method: $-0.5 < -0.4375 < -0.25 < 0.0625 < 0.25$. All three are strictly inside. The lesson generalises — to fit $n$ numbers between two rationals, widen the denominator until at least $n$ whole numbers separate the numerators.
