It did work again. Rather than stretch the line, mathematicians stepped off it entirely and built a second dimension of numbers, written with the letter $i$. They are called imaginary numbers, which is an unfortunate name for something so useful: they are indispensable in electrical engineering, in quantum mechanics, and in the signal processing inside the phone in your pocket.

That is a journey for a later year. For now the real numbers are enough, and there is a great deal written in their language.

Every extension in this chapter began the same way: with a perfectly reasonable question that the numbers of the day could not answer.

End-of-Chapter Exercises
  1. By long division, write each as a terminating or a repeating decimal, whichever it turns out to be: $\dfrac{3}{50}$ and $\dfrac{2}{9}$.
  1. Prove that $\sqrt{5}$ is irrational.
  1. Write each in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$, in lowest terms:
    1. $12.\overline{6}$
    2. $0.\overline{012}$
    3. $2.\overline{05}$
    4. $1.2\overline{35}$
  1. Locate $0.532$ and $1.15$ on the number line, magnifying the interval at each step to place the next digit.
  1. Find six rational numbers between 3 and 4.
  1. Find five rational numbers between $\dfrac{2}{5}$ and $\dfrac{3}{5}$.
  1. Find five rational numbers between $\dfrac{1}{6}$ and $\dfrac{2}{5}$.
  1. Find the rational number $x$ for which $\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{x}{5} = \dfrac{16}{15}$.
  1. Let $a$ and $b$ be non-zero rationals with $a + b = 0$. Without choosing any numbers, decide whether $ab$ is positive or negative, and justify it.
  1. Without dividing, decide whether $\dfrac{18}{125}$ terminates. If it does, say in how many decimal places, and why that many.
