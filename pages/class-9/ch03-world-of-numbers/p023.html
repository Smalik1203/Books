Example 11

Write $0.\overline{45}$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$.

Same idea, but the repeating block is two digits long, so the shift has to be two places to bring the tails into line.

Step 1 Let $x = 0.454545\ldots$
Step 2 Two digits repeat, so multiply by $10^2$: $\;100x = 45.454545\ldots$
Step 3 Subtract: $\;100x - x = 45.4545\ldots - 0.4545\ldots$
Step 4 $99x = 45$, so $x = \dfrac{45}{99} = \dfrac{5}{11}$.

Which agrees with Example 7, where dividing 5 by 11 gave $0.\overline{45}$ in the first place — the two directions meet.

Two examples in, a pattern in the denominators is already showing. One repeating digit left $9x = 6$; two repeating digits left $99x = 45$. The subtraction always removes a power of ten and leaves one behind, so what multiplies $x$ is $10^{n} - 1$ — a string of $n$ nines, and nothing else is possible.

Read backwards that is a small gift. A cycle of $n$ digits, starting immediately, is simply that block of digits over $n$ nines: so $0.\overline{7} = \tfrac{7}{9}$, $0.\overline{45} = \tfrac{45}{99}$ and $0.\overline{123} = \tfrac{123}{999}$, each waiting only to be reduced.

Turn it round once more and it says something about the nines themselves. $\tfrac{1}{9} = 0.\overline{1}$, $\tfrac{1}{99} = 0.\overline{01}$, $\tfrac{1}{999} = 0.\overline{001}$ — and the reason $9$ and $99$ behave this way is the reason $10$ and $100$ do not: they are the numbers a power of ten falls just short of.

Reducing the fraction hides all this, of course. Once $\tfrac{45}{99}$ has become $\tfrac{5}{11}$ there is no $99$ left to see, and coming the other way there is no obvious reason why $11$ should give a cycle of two digits. But the $99$ has not gone anywhere: $11$ divides $99$, which is $10^{2} - 1$, and two is the length of the cycle.

That works generally, and it can be checked on a case worth checking. Seven divides $999999$, which is $10^{6} - 1$, and no shorter string of nines — so $\tfrac{1}{7}$ ought to repeat with a cycle of exactly six digits. Divide it out and it does.

In both of those the repetition began immediately after the decimal point. Very often it does not — a digit or two occurs once and only then does the cycle start, and a single shift can no longer bring the tails into agreement.
