Example 11

Write $0.\overline{45}$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$.

Same idea, but the repeating block is two digits long, so the shift has to be two places to bring the tails into line.

Step 1 Let $x = 0.454545\ldots$
Step 2 Two digits repeat, so multiply by $10^2$: $\;100x = 45.454545\ldots$
Step 3 Subtract: $\;100x - x = 45.4545\ldots - 0.4545\ldots$
Step 4 $99x = 45$, so $x = \dfrac{45}{99} = \dfrac{5}{11}$.

Which agrees with Example 7, where dividing 5 by 11 gave $0.\overline{45}$ in the first place — the two directions meet.

In both of those the repetition began immediately after the decimal point. Very often it does not — a digit or two occurs once and only then does the cycle start, and a single shift can no longer bring the tails into agreement.
