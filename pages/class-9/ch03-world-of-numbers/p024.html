Example 13

Write $2.35\overline{7}$ in the form $\dfrac{p}{q}$.

The general case, with everything at once: a whole part, two non-repeating digits and a one-digit cycle. The method of Example 12 handles it unchanged — count the two kinds of digit, and use one multiplier for each.

Solution.

Step 1 Let $x = 2.35777\ldots$; the block $35$ does not repeat (2 digits), the $7$ does (1 digit)
Step 2 $\;100x = 235.777\ldots$ clear the 2 non-repeating digits with $10^2$
Step 3 $\;1000x = 2357.777\ldots$ move 1 full cycle with a further $10^1$
Step 4 $\;900x = 2357.777\ldots - 235.777\ldots = 2122$ subtract
Answer $x = \dfrac{2122}{900} = \dfrac{1061}{450}$, dividing both by 2
Check $1061 \div 450 = 2.35777\ldots$

And notice the denominator that emerged — $900 = 9 \times 100$. The nine comes from the single repeating digit, the hundred from the two that do not repeat. The shape of the answer records the shape of the decimal.

Converting a repeating decimal to a fraction
If the decimal isThen
terminating read it over $10$, $100$, … and cancel
purely repeating,
$n$ digits in the cycle		 let $x$ be the number, multiply by $10^n$, subtract, solve
repeating after $m$ other digits,
$n$ digits in the cycle		 multiply by $10^m$, then by a further $10^n$, subtract the first from the second, solve

Because the shape of the answer records the shape of the decimal, the table can be read backwards and used at sight. Take all the digits up to the end of one cycle, subtract the digits that do not repeat, and put the result over as many nines as there are repeating digits followed by as many zeros as there are non-repeating ones.

$0.2\overline{7} = \dfrac{27 - 2}{90} = \dfrac{25}{90} = \dfrac{5}{18}$

That is not a new method. It is the algebra of the last three examples with the working left out, and it is worth checking against one of them before trusting it.
