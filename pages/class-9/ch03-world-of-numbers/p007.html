$$-\frac{1}{5} \;=\; \frac{-1}{5} \;=\; \frac{1}{-5}$$

Put all the integers together with all the fractions, positive and negative, and you have the rational numbers, written $\mathbb{Q}$ — for quotient.

Rational number

A rational number is any number that can be written as $\dfrac{p}{q}$, where $p$ and $q$ are integers and $q \neq 0$.

Three things follow from that definition, and each is worth stating before we use it.

Every integer is already rational. Write $5$ as $\tfrac{5}{1}$ and $-10$ as $\tfrac{-10}{1}$. So the rationals contain the naturals, the whole numbers and the integers — nothing has been left behind by the extension.

The writing is not unique. The same rational number has endlessly many forms: $-\tfrac{1}{3} = -\tfrac{2}{6} = -\tfrac{3}{9} = -\tfrac{10}{30}$, and so on. These are equivalent fractions, and it is this freedom that lets us cancel a common factor from top and bottom whenever it helps.

So we agree on one form. When we say the rational number $\tfrac{p}{q}$, or mark it on the number line, we take $q \neq 0$ and take $p$ and $q$ to share no factor but 1 — to be co-prime. Among all the fractions equal to a half we choose $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to stand for them.

Think and Reflect
?

Why must the definition insist that $q \neq 0$? Try to say what would go wrong, rather than only that it is not allowed.

A quotient $\tfrac{p}{q}$ is meant to be the number which, times $q$, gives back $p$. Ask whether any number does that when $q = 0$ — first when $p$ is not zero, then when it is. The two cases fail differently, and both failures matter.
