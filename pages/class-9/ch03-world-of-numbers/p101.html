Using What You Know
The chapter's results, in questions that do not say which one to use.

The chapter ended with a line full of numbers and a few facts about them. The rationals are closed under the four operations. A number is rational exactly when its decimal terminates or repeats. $|a - b|$ is a distance. And $\sqrt{2}$ is not a ratio. Every question below can be answered with those facts alone. What is new is that the question no longer says which fact to use. Try each before reading on.

Try it first

Is $\sqrt{2} + 1$ rational or irrational? Then decide: is the sum of two irrational numbers always irrational?

Suppose $\sqrt{2} + 1$ were rational, and call it $r$. Then

$$\sqrt{2} = r - 1$$

The right side is a difference of two rationals, and the rationals are closed under subtraction. So $\sqrt{2}$ would be rational, and Section 3.5 proved that it is not. The supposition fails: $\sqrt{2} + 1$ is irrational. Notice that we did not write down a single decimal place of $\sqrt{2}$. Nothing in the argument depended on the $1$ either. A rational number added to any irrational number gives an irrational number, for the same reason.

The second question has a different answer. $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, and so is $-\sqrt{2}$, since if $-\sqrt{2}$ were a rational $s$ then $\sqrt{2} = -s$ would be rational too. Their sum is $0$. One example is enough: the sum of two irrational numbers is not always irrational. The argument above does not apply here, because it needed one of the two numbers to be rational.

Try it first

Does $\dfrac{7}{80}$ have a terminating decimal? If it does, after how many places does it stop? Answer without dividing.

The fraction is in lowest terms, and $80 = 2^4 \times 5$. The only prime factors are $2$ and $5$, so the decimal terminates. To see where it stops, do what Example 8 did and turn the denominator into a power of ten. There are four $2$s and one $5$, so three more $5$s are needed.

$$\dfrac{7}{80} = \dfrac{7 \times 125}{2^4 \times 5^4} = \dfrac{875}{10000} = 0.0875$$

It stops after four places. Notice that the four came from $2^4$, the larger of the two powers. The single $5$ was matched by the $2$s without any help. A denominator of $2^3 \times 5$ would give three places, and so would $5^3$ on its own. We knew the length of the decimal before we knew any of its digits.
