Using What You Know

The chapter ended with a line full of numbers and a few facts about them. The rationals are closed under the four operations. A number is rational exactly when its decimal terminates or repeats. $|a - b|$ is a distance. And $\sqrt{2}$ is not a ratio. Every question below can be answered with those facts alone. What is new is that the question no longer says which fact to use. Try each before reading on.

Is $3\sqrt{2}$ rational or irrational? Then decide: is the sum of two irrational numbers always irrational?

Suppose $3\sqrt{2}$ were rational, and call it $r$. Then

$$\sqrt{2} = \frac{r}{3}$$

The right side is a quotient of two rationals, with a divisor that is not zero, and the rationals are closed under division. So $\sqrt{2}$ would be rational, and Section 3.5 proved that it is not. The supposition fails: $3\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. Notice that we did not write down a single decimal place of $\sqrt{2}$. Nothing in the argument depended on the $3$ either, except that it is not zero: a non-zero rational times an irrational is irrational, for the same reason. The zero matters, because $0 \times \sqrt{2} = 0$.

The second question has a different answer. $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, and so is $-\sqrt{2}$, since if $-\sqrt{2}$ were a rational $s$ then $\sqrt{2} = -s$ would be rational too. Their sum is $0$. One example is enough: the sum of two irrational numbers is not always irrational. The argument above does not apply here, because it needed one of the two numbers to be rational.

Does $\dfrac{21}{150}$ have a terminating decimal? A student says it cannot, because $150 = 2 \times 3 \times 5^2$ has the prime factor $3$. Answer without dividing.

The student has used the right test on the wrong fraction. The test is about a denominator in lowest terms, and $\tfrac{21}{150}$ is not in lowest terms: $21 = 3 \times 7$, so the $3$ cancels.

$$\frac{21}{150} = \frac{7}{50} = \frac{7 \times 2}{2^2 \times 5^2} = \frac{14}{100} = 0.14$$
