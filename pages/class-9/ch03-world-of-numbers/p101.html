The Foundation Bridge
Take what you know. Go further.

Five kinds of number, one line, and a decimal expansion that tells you which kind you are holding. Ten pages of harder work on exactly those follow — no new syllabus, only questions of the kind asked outside school.

What arithmetic does to the two kinds

The rationals are closed: combine two of them and you get another. The irrationals are not, and the mixed cases are where examinations live. One line settles the most useful of them. If $r$ is rational and $i$ is irrational, could $r + i$ be some rational $s$? Then $i = s - r$, a difference of two rationals — and so rational, which it is not.

Four cases, and only two are settled
  1. rational $+$ irrational is always irrational; so is (non-zero) rational $\times$ irrational;
  2. irrational $+$ irrational, and irrational $\times$ irrational, may be either — $\sqrt{2} + \left(-\sqrt{2}\right) = 0$, but $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is not rational.

How long a cycle can be

Dividing by $7$, the only possible remainders are $1$ to $6$, so the cycle of $\frac{1}{7}$ cannot be longer than six digits — and it is exactly six. Dividing by $11$ there are ten possible remainders, and yet $\frac{1}{11} = 0.\overline{09}$ has a cycle of only two.

So $q - 1$ is a ceiling, not a promise. Which primes reach it is a real question, unanswered to this day: $7$, $17$, $19$, $23$ do; $11$, $13$, $31$ do not. A number whose cycle reaches the ceiling gives a cyclic block, which is why $142857$ behaves as it does and $090909$ does not.

The irrationals are crowded too

The chapter showed the rationals are dense: between any two lies another. The same is true of the irrationals, and the proof is a single trick. Given $a \lt b$, the interval between them contains a rational $r$, and then $r + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{n}$ is irrational for every $n$ — choose $n$ large enough and it still lies below $b$.

So the two kinds are interleaved everywhere on the line, no matter how far you magnify. Neither has an interval to itself.
