Exercise Set 3.5
  1. Without dividing, say which of $\dfrac{7}{20}$, $\dfrac{4}{15}$ and $\dfrac{13}{250}$ have terminating decimals and which repeat. Then do the divisions and check yourself.
  1. Work out $\dfrac{3}{13}$ by long division and identify the repeating block. Now do $\dfrac{1}{13}$ and $\dfrac{2}{13}$. What do the three blocks have to do with one another?
  1. Classify each as rational or irrational, and give the fraction where there is one:
    1. $\sqrt{81}$
    2. $\sqrt{12}$
    3. $0.3333\ldots$
    4. $0.123451234512345\ldots$
    5. $1.01001000100001\ldots$ — is any single block repeating?
    6. $23.560185612239874790120$
  1. The number $0.\overline{9}$ is rational. Using the method of Example 10 — let $x = 0.\overline{9}$, multiply by 10, subtract — show that it is exactly equal to 1. Then say why that is less surprising than it looks.
  1. The repeating block of $\dfrac{1}{7}$ is cyclic. Find other numbers $n$ whose reciprocal $\dfrac{1}{n}$ has a cyclic block. What do the values of $n$ that work have in common?

One last oddity, which question 4 walks into. Terminating decimals are not unique. Just as $1 = \tfrac{10}{10} = \tfrac{100}{100}$, every terminating decimal has a second form ending in repeating nines: $1.000\ldots = 0.999\ldots$ and $2.47000\ldots = 2.46999\ldots$

Most people meet $0.\overline{9} = 1$ with resistance, feeling it must fall a little short. It does not. There is no room between them — any number you propose as lying in the gap can be shown to be larger than $0.\overline{9}$, and the algebra of question 4 settles it outright.
