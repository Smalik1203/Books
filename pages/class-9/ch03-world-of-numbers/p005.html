3.3Integers: Going Left of Zero

Brahmagupta did not stop at zero. He had defined it as $5 - 5 = 0$, and that definition invites an obvious next question: what is $3 - 5$? The notation permits it. Does anything answer it?

His answer came from commerce rather than from arithmetic. A merchant deals in two kinds of quantity, and always has: what he owns and what he owes. Brahmagupta named them dhana, fortunes, and ṛṇa, debts — and treated the second as a number in its own right rather than as a fortune with a note attached.

Going left of zero on the line gives the negative numbers. Together with the positive naturals and zero they make the integers, written $\mathbb{Z}$ — from the German Zahlen, numbers.

It is worth being exact about what that bought. Among the naturals, subtraction is a rule with an exception attached: you may take a smaller number from a larger one, and $3 - 5$ has no answer at all. Among the integers there is no exception. Every subtraction can be carried out, and the answer is always waiting somewhere on the line.

And nothing was given up to get it. Every natural number is still present and still behaves as it did — $3$ is the same $3$ it was before the line was extended leftwards, and $5 - 2$ is still $3$. The new numbers were built around the old ones rather than in place of them. That is how the number system grows for the rest of this chapter: each stage keeps everything the stage before it had, and removes an exception.

The arithmetic of integers

Brahmagupta wrote the rules out, and we still use them exactly as he stated them thirteen centuries ago. What is striking is that every one of them is justified by what it would mean for a merchant, not by any appeal to the symbols.

Brahmagupta's laws of signs

A fortune plus a fortune is a fortune: $5 + 4 = 9$.

A debt plus a debt is a debt: $(-5) + (-4) = -9$.

A fortune less zero is a fortune; a debt less zero is a debt.

A debt times a fortune is a debt: $(-3) \times 4 = -12$.

A debt times a debt is a fortune: $(-3) \times (-4) = 12$.
