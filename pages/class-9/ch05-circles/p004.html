Watch a wheel go past. At one moment some point of the rim is touching the road; a moment later some point of the rim is touching the road again. Is it the same point? Nothing you can see decides it. Chalk a mark on the rim and you can follow it, as in Fig. 5.2 — but rub the mark out and the two pictures become one picture.

Turn a circle about its centre, through any angle you like, and you get the circle back — not another circle of the same size, but the very same set of points. The reason is the definition and nothing else. Turning about $O$ leaves every point's distance from $O$ unchanged, so a point at distance $r$ goes to a point at distance $r$; the set of all points at distance $r$ is carried onto itself.

No other shape in this book does that. Turn a square about its centre and it comes back to itself four times in a full turn, at multiples of $90^\circ$, and at no other angle. A circle comes back at every angle. We say it has complete rotational symmetry.

Now the other kind of sameness. Draw a circle, cut it out, and fold it so that the boundary falls exactly onto itself. Open it out: the crease is a line of reflection symmetry. It is Jamuna's fold from the first page of this chapter, and we are at last in a position to say what it does.

Back to the folded paper

Call the crease $\ell$. Folding along $\ell$ carries the circle onto itself, and folding does not change distances. So the point where the centre lands — call it $O'$ — is again a point from which every point of the circle is at distance $r$. Could $O'$ be anywhere other than $O$? If it were, every point of the circle would be equidistant from two different points, and so would lie on the perpendicular bisector of $OO'$ — the whole circle would sit on one straight line. It does not. So $O' = O$. And the only points a fold leaves where they were are the points of the crease itself.
