Now there are two right-angled triangles with their right angle at $M$. In triangle $OMA$ the hypotenuse is the radius and $AM = 16$ cm, so $OM^2 = 20^2 - 16^2 = 400 - 256 = 144$, and $OM = 12$ cm. The point $P$ is $7$ cm from $A$, so $PM = 16 - 7 = 9$ cm. In triangle $OMP$, $OP^2 = 12^2 + 9^2 = 144 + 81 = 225$, and $OP = 15$ cm.

Notice that $M$ was not given, and neither was the right angle there. We drew the one line that every result about a chord is built on, and the question became two uses of Pythagoras.

$ABCD$ is a quadrilateral with $\angle B = \angle D = 90^\circ$. Show that a circle passes through all four vertices, and say where its centre is.

Theorem 12 answers the first part in one line: $\angle B + \angle D = 180^\circ$, so the four points are concyclic. But it says nothing about the centre. Look instead at triangle $ABC$. It is right-angled at $B$, so the circle through $A$, $B$ and $C$ has $AC$ as a diameter (Section 5.9). Triangle $ADC$ is right-angled at $D$, so the circle through $A$, $D$ and $C$ also has $AC$ as a diameter. A segment is the diameter of only one circle, the one whose centre is its midpoint. So the two circles are the same circle, it passes through all four points, and its centre is the midpoint of $AC$. Notice that Theorem 12 was not needed in the end. It said a circle exists but not where. The angle in a semicircle said both.

None of these questions needed anything beyond the chapter, except Pythagoras and its converse from earlier classes. What each needed was a question: what can I use from what I already know? The solved examples that follow ask it again.
