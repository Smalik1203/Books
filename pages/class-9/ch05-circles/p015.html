Three things have been in play: that the segment runs from the centre, that it meets the chord at a right angle, and that it hits the midpoint. Theorem 4 takes the first and third and delivers the second; Theorem 5 takes the first and second and delivers the third. Any two of the three force the remaining one — and the last pairing is the first question below.

A word about RHS, which has now done the work twice and will do it again. It says that two right-angled triangles are congruent if they agree in the right angle, in the hypotenuse, and in one other side.

That is worth pausing on, because it is not SAS. In SAS the known angle sits between the two known sides, and here it does not: the right angle at $M$ is not between $CM$ and the hypotenuse $CA$. Two sides and an angle that is not between them will usually not fix a triangle at all — swing the third side about and there are commonly two different triangles fitting the same three measurements.

What removes the second possibility is the right angle itself. Once the angle is $90^\circ$ the hypotenuse is fixed as the longest side, and the remaining side is not free to be chosen: Pythagoras gives it as $\sqrt{CA^{2} - CM^{2}}$, one value and no other. There is nothing left to swing. RHS is really Pythagoras wearing the clothes of a congruence test, which is why it is available for right-angled triangles and for no others.

Example 6

A chord of length $16$ cm is drawn in a circle of radius $10$ cm. How far is the chord from the centre?

Distance from the centre means the perpendicular distance, and Theorem 5 says that perpendicular lands on the midpoint of the chord. That is what makes the calculation possible: it produces a right-angled triangle with a radius as its hypotenuse and half the chord as one leg.

Solution.

Step 1 half the chord: $\tfrac{1}{2}\times 16$, which is $8$ cm Theorem 5
Step 2 hypotenuse: a radius of the circle, $10$ cm
Step 3 $d^{2} = 10^{2} - 8^{2} = 100 - 64 = 36, \qquad d = 6$ the remaining side
Answer The chord lies $6$ cm from the centre.

Step 3 is the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem set out in the Śulbasūtra, centuries before Pythagoras. The distance is less than the radius, as it must be: a line $10$ cm out would only touch the circle.
