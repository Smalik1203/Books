5.7How Far a Chord Lies from the Centre

The distance from a point to a line means the perpendicular distance — the shortest way across. So the distance from the centre to a chord is measured along the perpendicular from the centre, and Theorem 5 has already told us where that perpendicular lands: on the midpoint of the chord.

Take a paper circle and fold a piece of the boundary inwards; the crease is a chord. Fold again so that the two ends of that crease meet, and open both folds. The second crease crosses the first at its midpoint, square on. Theorems 4 and 5, in paper.

Now make a second chord of the same length somewhere else on the circle, and measure how far it lies from the centre. The two distances come out equal. Try another pair and they come out equal again.

Is that convincing? It is the same trap as Jamuna's folded disc on the first page of this chapter. A statement can hold in every case you have patience to try and still be false. And even when it is true, a stack of measurements never says why. So here is why.

Theorem 6

Chords of a circle that have the same length lie at the same distance from the centre.

Let $AB$ and $FG$ be chords of a circle with centre $C$ and radius $r$, with $AB = FG$. Let $E$ and $H$ be their midpoints, so that by Theorem 4 the segments $CE$ and $CH$ are perpendicular to the chords — and are therefore the two distances we are comparing.

$AE = \tfrac{1}{2}AB = \tfrac{1}{2}FG = FH$, since $E$ and $H$ are the midpoints.

$CA = CF = r$, and these are the hypotenuses.

$\angle CEA = \angle CHF = 90^\circ$.

So $\triangle CEA \cong \triangle CHF$, by RHS, and therefore $CE = CH$.
