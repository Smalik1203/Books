Are there others? Notice that the question has quietly turned back into a question about points: which points of the plane are equidistant from $A$ and $B$? You met the answer in an earlier grade. They are the points of the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ — and there are infinitely many of them.

That answer needs stating with some care, because it is really two statements and a locus needs both of them:

Every point of the perpendicular bisector is equidistant from $A$ and $B$; and every point equidistant from $A$ and $B$ lies on the perpendicular bisector. The first says that no point of the line is missed out. The second says that no point outside it sneaks in. Drop either half and "the locus of" becomes the wrong phrase — you would have described some of the centres, or too many.

With both halves in hand the count is settled. The centres of the circles through $A$ and $B$ are exactly the points of the perpendicular bisector of $AB$: one circle for each point of a line, and so infinitely many.

Circles through two points

Infinitely many circles pass through any two given points. Their centres are precisely the points of the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them, and the smallest of those circles has that segment as a diameter.
