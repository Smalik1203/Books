Why is this true? The argument is the one just used, run forwards instead of backwards. Suppose some circle passes through $A$, $B$ and $C$, and call its centre $O$.

Since $OA = OB$, the point $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$.

Since $OA = OC$, the point $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$.

Now $A$, $B$ and $C$ are not collinear, so $AB$ and $AC$ point in different directions, and the two bisectors are therefore not parallel. Two non-parallel lines of a plane meet at exactly one point. So there is only one candidate for $O$ — which settles the exactly one before we have even shown that a circle exists.

And it does exist. Take that meeting point as centre and draw the circle of radius $OA$. Because $O$ is on the bisector of $AB$ we have $OB = OA$, and because it is on the bisector of $AC$ we have $OC = OA$. So $B$ and $C$ lie on that circle as well, and all three points are on it.
