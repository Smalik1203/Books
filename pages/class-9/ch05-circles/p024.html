Example 10

An arc $AXB$ subtends $110^\circ$ at the centre of a circle. What angle does it subtend at a point of the major arc? And what angle does the other arc subtend at a point of the minor arc?

The first is Theorem 9 read straight off. The second asks about a different arc, so the angle to halve is the one that arc sweeps at the centre — the reflex one.

Solution.

Step 1 arc $AXB$: $110^\circ$ at the centre, halved, is $55^\circ$ Theorem 9
Step 2 the other arc: $360^\circ - 110^\circ = 250^\circ$, halved, is $125^\circ$ the reflex angle
Answer A point on the major arc sees the chord at $55^\circ$; a point on the minor arc sees it at $125^\circ$.

Notice that the two add to $180^\circ$. That is not a coincidence of these numbers — the two central angles always make a full turn, so their halves always make a straight angle. Section 5.9 turns the observation into a theorem.

One case of Theorem 9 is used so often that it has a name of its own. A corollary is a fact that drops out of a result already proved, with no fresh argument needed.

Corollary

The angle subtended by a diameter at any point of the circle is a right angle.

Let $AB$ be a diameter and $D$ any other point of the circle. The arc from $A$ to $B$ that avoids $D$ is a semicircle, and the angle it sweeps at the centre is a straight angle, $180^\circ$. By Theorem 9 the angle at $D$ is half of that: $\angle ADB = 90^\circ$, wherever on the circle $D$ happens to be.

Section 5.4 left a debt here. It claimed that the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle lands on the midpoint of the hypotenuse, and gave no reason. The corollary is half of the reason. The other half is that a point seeing $AB$ under a right angle has no choice but to lie on the circle with $AB$ as diameter. That is exactly the sort of statement Section 5.9 is about, and you will be able to finish the argument there.

The corollary is old enough to carry a name: the angle in a semicircle is a right angle, and it is usually credited to Thales, who is said to have proved it some two and a half thousand years ago. It is useful, too. To find the centre of a circular disc, slide a set square about until both of its short edges touch the rim. The corner is then on the circle, and the two touching points are the ends of a diameter. Two such diameters cross at the centre.
