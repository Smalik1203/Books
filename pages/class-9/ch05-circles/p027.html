Suppose instead $D$ lies inside. Extend $AD$ until it meets the circle at $E$. This time $\angle ADB$ is the exterior angle of $\triangle BED$, so $\angle ADB > \angle AEB = \angle ACB$, and the same impossibility arrives from the other direction.

Both positions are ruled out, so $D$ lies on the circle through $A$, $B$ and $C$. The four points are concyclic.

A quadrilateral whose four vertices are concyclic is called a cyclic quadrilateral. Theorem 10 tells us when four points form one; the next result tells us what we get in return.

Theorem 11

The two opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to $180^\circ$.

Let $ABCD$ be cyclic, with centre $O$, and look at the two opposite angles at $A$ and at $C$. Each is an angle subtended by an arc at a point of the circle outside it, so Theorem 9 applies to both — and the trick is to be careful about which arc.

$\angle BAD$ is subtended at $A$ by the arc from $B$ to $D$ that passes through $C$. So it is half the angle that arc sweeps at $O$.

$\angle BCD$ is subtended at $C$ by the arc from $B$ to $D$ that passes through $A$. So it is half the angle that arc sweeps at $O$.

Between them the two arcs make up the whole circle, so the two angles they sweep at $O$ add to a complete turn of $360^\circ$. Halving,

$$\angle BAD + \angle BCD = \tfrac{1}{2}\times 360^\circ = 180^\circ$$

The same holds for the other pair, $\angle ABC$ and $\angle ADC$, by the identical argument on the arcs through $D$ and through $B$. And the converse is true as well.
