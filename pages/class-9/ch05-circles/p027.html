Between them the two arcs make up the whole circle, so the two angles they sweep at $O$ add to a complete turn of $360^\circ$. Halving,

$$\angle BAD + \angle BCD = \tfrac{1}{2}\times 360^\circ = 180^\circ$$

The same holds for the other pair, $\angle ABC$ and $\angle ADC$, by the identical argument on the arcs through $D$ and through $B$. And the converse is true as well.

Example 12

A cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ has $\angle A = 95^\circ$ and $\angle B = 70^\circ$. Find its other two angles.

Theorem 11 pairs each angle with the one opposite it, never with a neighbour. So $A$ goes with $C$, and $B$ with $D$.

A with C $\angle C = 180^\circ - 95^\circ$ $85^\circ$
B with D $\angle D = 180^\circ - 70^\circ$ $110^\circ$

Check the total: $95 + 70 + 85 + 110 = 360$, which the angles of any quadrilateral must come to. That check is worth doing every time, because it catches the commonest slip here — pairing angles that sit next to each other instead of across from each other.

Theorem 12

If two opposite angles of a quadrilateral add up to $180^\circ$, then its four vertices are concyclic.
