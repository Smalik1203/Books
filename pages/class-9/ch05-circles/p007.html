For the third, ask what the arc is turning into. Nothing in the argument ever lets it become a straight line — but see how close it is willing to get.

Two points, then, are not enough to pin a circle down. Three might be — and the answer turns out to depend on how the three are arranged.

The first thing to notice about three points is that they need not have any circle through them at all.

Suppose $A$, $B$ and $C$ lie on one straight line — that they are collinear. A circle through all three would need a centre equidistant from $A$ and $B$, so lying on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$; and equidistant from $B$ and $C$, so lying on the perpendicular bisector of $BC$. But both of those bisectors are perpendicular to the same line, which makes them parallel, and they are not the same line, because $AB$ and $BC$ have different midpoints. Parallel and distinct: they never meet. There is no such centre, and so no such circle.

Three points in a row, then, are hopeless. Rule that case out and everything changes.

Theorem 1

Through any three points that are not collinear there passes exactly one circle.

Why is this true? The argument is the one just used, run forwards instead of backwards. Suppose some circle passes through $A$, $B$ and $C$, and call its centre $O$.

Since $OA = OB$, the point $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$.

Since $OA = OC$, the point $O$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AC$.

Now $A$, $B$ and $C$ are not collinear, so $AB$ and $AC$ point in different directions, and the two bisectors are therefore not parallel. Two non-parallel lines of a plane meet at exactly one point. So there is only one candidate for $O$ — which settles the exactly one before we have even shown that a circle exists.
