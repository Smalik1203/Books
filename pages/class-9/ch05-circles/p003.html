Example 2

A circle has centre $O$ and radius $10$ cm. Three points are marked, with $OP = 10$ cm, $OQ = 7$ cm and $OR = 13$ cm. Which of them lie on the circle?

The definition puts one question to a point, and only one: is its distance from the centre equal to the radius? So set each distance beside $r = 10$ cm.

P $OP = 10$, and $r = 10$ on the circle
Q $OQ = 7$, which is $\lt r$ inside
R $OR = 13$, which is $\gt r$ outside

Only $P$ lies on the circle. Notice that being inside or outside is not a near miss that might be argued about: the definition allows no degrees. A point either stands at distance $r$ from the centre or it is no part of the circle at all.

A diameter is a chord of a particular kind, and its length follows from the definition alone: both ends lie at distance $r$ from the centre, and the centre lies between them. Every diameter of a given circle is $2r$ long.

No chord is longer. A chord that misses the centre still has both ends at distance $r$ from it, so going from one end to the other by way of the centre measures $2r$ — and going straight across is shorter. Nothing here was measured: the argument used the definition, and that a straight path is the shortest one.
