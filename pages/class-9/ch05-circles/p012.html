Now run the argument backwards. Suppose instead that the two angles at the centre are equal — must the chords be?

Theorem 3

Chords of a circle that subtend equal angles at the centre are equal in length.

$CA = CD = r$ and $CB = CE = r$.

$\angle ACB = \angle DCE$ is now what is given, and it is the angle between those two pairs of sides.

So $\triangle CAB \cong \triangle CDE$, by SAS, and therefore $AB = DE$.

Notice how little separates the two proofs: the same figure, the same two triangles, the same four radii. All that changes is which fact is given and which is wanted — and with it, the congruence rule that does the work.

Example 5

A chord of a circle of radius $9$ cm subtends $60^\circ$ at the centre. How long is the chord? And if a second chord of the same circle is also $9$ cm long, what angle does it subtend?

Join the ends of the chord to the centre. The two radii and the chord make a triangle, and here it is better than isosceles.
