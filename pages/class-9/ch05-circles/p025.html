5.9Points on One Circle

Section 5.4 counted the circles through two points — infinitely many — and through three, where there is exactly one so long as they are not in a row. Four points are a different matter altogether. Any three of them already fix a circle, so the fourth has no freedom left: either it happens to land on that circle or it does not. Points that all lie on one circle are called concyclic.

So when does the fourth point land? Theorem 9 supplies the clue. If $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ are concyclic, with $C$ and $D$ on the same side of $AB$, then $C$ and $D$ are on the same arc, and angles in the same segment are equal: $\angle ACB = \angle ADB$. That much is forced. The question is whether it works in reverse.

Theorem 10

If a segment $AB$ subtends equal angles at two points $C$ and $D$ lying on the same side of it, then $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ are concyclic.

The proof takes a shape not used anywhere else in this chapter: rather than building the circle, we show that $D$ has nowhere else to be.

The points $A$, $B$, $C$ are not collinear — if they were, $\angle ACB$ would be a straight angle or nothing at all — so by Theorem 1 there is exactly one circle through them. Draw it. Now $D$ is either outside that circle, or inside it, or on it. Rule out the first two and only the third is left.
