Example 4

Is there a circle through $P(1,2)$, $Q(3,6)$ and $R(5,10)$?

Theorem 1 promises one for any three points that are not collinear, so the real question is whether these three are in a row. Compare the step from $P$ to $Q$ with the step from $Q$ to $R$.

Solution.

Step 1 $P$ to $Q$: across $2$, up $4$, which is $2$ up per $1$ across
Step 2 $Q$ to $R$: across $2$, up $4$, which is $2$ up per $1$ across
Step 3 the two steps rise at the same rate and share the point $Q$, so all three points lie on one straight line collinear
Answer There is no circle through them.

The reason is the one given above: the perpendicular bisectors of $PQ$ and $QR$ are parallel, so they never meet to give a centre.

For an acute-angled triangle the circumcentre lies inside — Fig. 5.4 is one such. For an obtuse-angled triangle it lies outside the triangle altogether. And for a right-angled triangle it lands exactly on the midpoint of the hypotenuse, which is another way of saying that the hypotenuse is a diameter of the circumcircle.

Read the three cases together and they make one statement. The circumcentre moves outwards as the largest angle of the triangle grows, crossing the triangle's own edge at the moment that angle reaches $90^\circ$. So its position is a report on the triangle. Find the circumcentre inside and the triangle is acute-angled, on an edge and it is right-angled, outside and it is obtuse. No angle has had to be measured to learn it.

That last case is not a coincidence, and it is not proved here. Section 5.8 will hand it to you in a line.
