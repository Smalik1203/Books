Example 12

A cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ has $\angle A = 95^\circ$ and $\angle B = 70^\circ$. Find its other two angles.

Theorem 11 pairs each angle with the one opposite it, never with a neighbour. So $A$ goes with $C$, and $B$ with $D$.

Solution.

Step 1 $A$ with $C$: $\angle C = 180^\circ - 95^\circ$, which is $85^\circ$ opposite angles
Step 2 $B$ with $D$: $\angle D = 180^\circ - 70^\circ$, which is $110^\circ$ opposite angles
Answer $\angle C = 85^\circ$ and $\angle D = 110^\circ$
Check $95 + 70 + 85 + 110 = 360$ any quadrilateral

That check is worth doing every time, because it catches the most common slip here — pairing angles that sit next to each other instead of across from each other.

Theorem 12

If two opposite angles of a quadrilateral add up to $180^\circ$, then its four vertices are concyclic.

The proof runs like Theorem 10's — by showing the fourth vertex has nowhere else to go. Suppose $\angle BAD + \angle BCD = 180^\circ$. Since $A$, $B$, $D$ are not collinear there is a circle through them; suppose $C$ lay outside it, and let $E$ be where $DC$ meets the circle. Then $ABED$ is cyclic, so Theorem 11 gives $\angle BAD + \angle BED = 180^\circ$. Comparing with what we were given, $\angle BED = \angle BCD$. But $\angle BED$ is an exterior angle of $\triangle BEC$ and so exceeds $\angle BCE$, which is $\angle BCD$. Once again a quantity is greater than itself. The case with $C$ inside is the same argument with the roles exchanged, and is worth writing out.

Closing an old debt

Twice now this chapter has promised that the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle sits at the midpoint of the hypotenuse. Theorem 10 pays up.

Let $\triangle ABD$ have its right angle at $D$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of the hypotenuse $AB$. Draw the circle with centre $M$ and radius $MA$, so that $AB$ is a diameter of it, and mark any point $D'$ of that circle on the same side of $AB$ as $D$. By the corollary in Section 5.8, $\angle AD'B = 90^\circ$ — which is also $\angle ADB$. Two points, equal angles, same side: by Theorem 10 the four points $A$, $B$, $D'$ and $D$ are concyclic, so $D$ is on that circle too. Its centre $M$ is therefore equidistant from $A$, $B$ and $D$ — which is exactly what it means to be the circumcentre.
