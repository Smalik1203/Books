Using What You Know

The chapter ended with twelve results, and every question below can be answered with those and with what you already know about triangles. What is new is that the question no longer says which result to use. Often it does not even mention the thing the result is about. Try each one before reading on.

There is a reason the centre keeps turning up. It is the one point the whole circle agrees about — every point of the rim is the same distance from it, and no other point in the plane can say as much. So anything stated at the centre is stated about the circle as a whole, while anything stated at a single point of the rim is, until it is converted, a remark about that point only.

A chord $AB$ of a circle is $7$ cm long, and it subtends an angle of $150^\circ$ at a point $Q$ of the minor arc. Find the radius of the circle.

The angle given is at $Q$, and no result in the chapter finds a radius from an angle at a point of the circle. But Theorem 9 connects the angle at $Q$ with an angle at the centre $O$. The point $Q$ lies on the minor arc, so the arc it looks at is the major arc, and that arc sweeps $2 \times 150^\circ = 300^\circ$ at the centre. The angle $AOB$ on the other side is therefore

$$\angle AOB = 360^\circ - 300^\circ = 60^\circ$$

Now the picture is the one in Example 5 of Section 5.5. $OA$ and $OB$ are radii, so triangle $OAB$ is isosceles with its apex angle $60^\circ$, and the two base angles are $60^\circ$ each. All three angles are equal, so all three sides are equal, and the radius is $OA = AB = 7$ cm. Notice that the question never mentioned the centre. We put it in ourselves, because the results of the chapter are all about the centre. An angle on the circle became an angle at the centre, and there the chapter could work.

A triangle has sides $9$ cm, $12$ cm and $15$ cm. Find the radius of the circle that passes through its three vertices.

Theorem 1 says there is exactly one such circle, and Section 5.4 says where its centre is: where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet. Finding that point would take a drawing. But look at the sides first. $9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225 = 15^2$. You met the converse of the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem in an earlier class: when the squares of two sides add up to the square of the third, the angle opposite the third side is a right angle. So this triangle is right-angled, with the $15$ cm side as its hypotenuse.
