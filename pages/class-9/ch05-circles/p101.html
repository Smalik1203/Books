The Foundation Bridge
Take what you know. Go further.

Twelve theorems, and one sentence underneath them all. Ten pages of harder work on exactly those follow — no new syllabus, only questions of the kind asked outside school.

One relation, doing the work of four theorems

Section 5.7 proved that a chord at distance $d$ from the centre of a circle of radius $r$ has length $2\sqrt{r^2 - d^2}$. Four separate theorems collapse into it, because in a fixed circle the formula ties $c$ and $d$ together with nothing else allowed in.

$$c = 2\sqrt{r^2 - d^2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad d = \sqrt{r^2 - \left(\tfrac{c}{2}\right)^2}$$

Read it as a locus and it says something the theorems do not: all the chords of one length have their midpoints at the same distance from the centre, so those midpoints trace a circle, concentric with the first.

A hidden circle, wherever two equal angles are

Theorems 9 and 10 together say more than either says alone. Fix a segment $AB$ and an angle $\theta$, and ask which points of the plane see $AB$ under exactly $\theta$. Theorem 9 says every point of one arc does; Theorem 10 says no point off that arc does. So the answer is an arc — a pair of them, one each side of $AB$.

Equal angles mean a circle is present

When a figure gives you two equal angles standing on the same segment from the same side, four points are concyclic whether or not anybody has drawn the circle. Drawing it is usually the whole solution.

Why the right angle is the famous case

Take $\theta = 90^\circ$ and the two arcs become the two halves of one circle on $AB$ as diameter. That is the corollary of Section 5.8 read backwards, and it is what finally settled the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle. A right angle is not a special theorem; it is the one value of $\theta$ for which the two arcs close up into a single circle.
