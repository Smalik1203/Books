A reasoning question has no working to show. It wants the sentence that would convince someone who disagreed — so answer in words, name the theorem you are leaning on, and kill a false claim with one counter-example rather than an argument.

B2 · Think and Reason
  1. Jamuna's folds meet at the centre every time she tries it, and the chapter still calls that no proof.
    1. Say what folding establishes and what it cannot.
    2. The proof turns on the sentence a fold does not change distances. Where exactly is it used?
  2. Theorems 2 and 3 have the same figure, the same two triangles and the same four radii, and different proofs. Say what changes between them, and why the congruence rule has to change with it.
  3. From the centre, through the midpoint, at a right angle: any two of these three force the third. Take the pairing the chapter left to an exercise, state the theorem it gives, and say which congruence would prove it.
  4. Three points fix at most one circle, and sometimes none at all.
    1. Explain, without algebra, why three points in a row have no circle through them.
    2. Say what happens to the centre, and to the circle, as the third point is nudged back towards the line.
  5. A chord slides from the centre outwards. Using $c = 2\sqrt{r^2 - d^2}$, describe what happens to its length, and say why the chapter can claim there is no shortest chord when there is plainly a longest.
  6. The angle at the centre does not depend on where $D$ sits, because the centre does not know where $D$ is. Explain how that one remark proves that angles in the same segment are equal, with no further drawing — and say what stops the same argument from working for a point $D$ on the other arc.
  7. Two chords of one circle lie $6$ cm and $8$ cm from the centre.
    1. Which is longer? Answer before any radius is given, and say which theorem lets you.
    2. Now you are told the radius is $10$ cm. By how much is it longer — and could you have predicted that too?
