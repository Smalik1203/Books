Theorem 1 says there is exactly one such circle, and Section 5.4 says where its centre is: where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet. Finding that point would take a drawing. But look at the sides first. $6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2$, so the angle opposite the $10$ cm side is a right angle. Section 5.9 proved that the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle is the midpoint of the hypotenuse. So the centre is the midpoint of the $10$ cm side, and the radius is $5$ cm. Notice that no bisector was drawn. The numbers held a right angle, the right angle picked out a result, and that result did all the work.

Try it first

In Fig. 5B.1, $O$ is the centre and $\angle OAB = 25^\circ$. The point $P$ lies on the major arc and $Q$ on the minor arc. Find $\angle APB$ and $\angle AQB$.

The angle given is at $A$, at the end of a chord, and no theorem of the chapter says anything about an angle there. But $OA$ and $OB$ are radii, so triangle $OAB$ is isosceles and $\angle OBA = \angle OAB = 25^\circ$. Then $\angle AOB = 180^\circ - 25^\circ - 25^\circ = 130^\circ$. Now the chapter can take over. By Theorem 9, $\angle APB = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 130^\circ = 65^\circ$. The point $Q$ stands on the other arc, and the arc it looks at is the major arc, which sweeps $360^\circ - 130^\circ = 230^\circ$ at the centre. So $\angle AQB = 115^\circ$. As a check, $65^\circ + 115^\circ = 180^\circ$, which Example 10 said must happen. Notice that the first step was not a circle theorem at all. It was an isosceles triangle. The radii make it isosceles, and that step is often what turns a question about a chord into one the chapter has already answered.
