Solution 2

Two facts are given, and each belongs to a different result. $\angle ADC$ is opposite $\angle ABC$, so by Theorem 11

$\angle ABC = 180^\circ - 130^\circ = 50^\circ$

$AB$ is a diameter and $C$ is a point of the circle, so the angle in a semicircle gives $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$. Triangle $ABC$ now has two known angles, and $\angle CAB = 180^\circ - 90^\circ - 50^\circ = 40^\circ$, option (a).

Option (b) stops at $\angle ABC$. Option (c) halves $130^\circ$, as if it were an angle at the centre, but $D$ is a point of the circle and not the centre. Option (d) is $130^\circ - 50^\circ$, a difference of two angles that share no triangle.

Problem 3 · Try it first

One chord of a circle of radius $r$ subtends $60^\circ$ at the centre, and another subtends $120^\circ$. The second chord is

  1. twice the first
  2. $\sqrt{2}$ times the first
  3. $\sqrt{3}$ times the first
  4. $1\tfrac{1}{2}$ times the first
Solution 3

The first chord is Example 5: the two radii and the chord make an equilateral triangle, so the chord is $r$. Call the second chord $AB$, with centre $O$ and midpoint $M$. By Theorem 4, $OM$ is perpendicular to $AB$, and the two congruent triangles in its proof also give $\angle AOM = \angle BOM = 60^\circ$. So triangle $OMA$ has angles $90^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $30^\circ$, with hypotenuse $OA = r$.

Reflect triangle $OMA$ in the line $AB$. The centre $O$ goes to a point $O'$ with $AO' = AO = r$, and $\angle OAO' = 30^\circ + 30^\circ = 60^\circ$. Two equal sides with $60^\circ$ between them make an equilateral triangle, so $OO' = r$, and $OM$ is half of it: $OM = \tfrac{1}{2} r$. Now

$AM^2 = r^2 - \tfrac{1}{4} r^2 = \tfrac{3}{4} r^2$,  so  $AB = 2\,AM = \sqrt{3}\, r$

The second chord is $\sqrt{3}$ times the first, option (c). Option (a) is the natural guess: double the angle, double the chord. Exercise Set 5.3 asked why that fails, and here is the answer in numbers. $\sqrt{3}$ is about $1.73$, and no chord reaches $2r$ except a diameter, which subtends $180^\circ$. A chord does not grow in proportion to its angle.
