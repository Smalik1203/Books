Selected solutions

B5.4 $PQ$ is a chord of both circles, so $d_1 + d_2 = 21$ and $10^2 - d_1^2 = 17^2 - d_2^2$. The second gives $d_2^2 - d_1^2 = 189$, and dividing by the first gives $d_2 - d_1 = 9$. So $d_1 = 6$, the half-chord is $8$, and $PQ = 16$ cm.
key idea One length read as a chord of two circles gives two equations.
B6.3 Opposite sides would need $d_1 + d_2 = 7$ with $d_1^2 - d_2^2 = 119$, forcing $d_1 - d_2 = 17$ — impossible. So they share a side: $d_1 - d_2 = 7$ gives $d_1 + d_2 = 17$, hence $d_1 = 12$, $d_2 = 5$ and $r = 13$ cm.
key idea The arrangement a question leaves open is not always free. Test both: here one is impossible.
B2.4 Both statements are true, so (c) and (d) are out. But equal radii give only an isosceles triangle; the length $r\sqrt{2}$ needs the right angle and Pythagoras, which R never mentions. Hence (b).
What to take away
  1. $r^2 = d^2 + (c/2)^2$ carries Sections 5.6 and 5.7 between them.
  2. Equal angles on one side of a segment mean a circle. Draw it.
  3. Read what a question has not said, then test both. Bound before you compute: most options die to $2r$.
