Note what the argument gave us along the way. All three perpendicular bisectors pass through $O$, though only two were used to find it; the third has no choice in the matter, since $O$ is equidistant from $B$ and $C$ too.

Theorem 1 says the circumcircle exists and that there is only one. It says nothing about where the centre lands — and the answer is not the one you might guess, because the circumcentre need not lie inside the triangle at all.

Example 3

A triangle has vertices $A(0,0)$, $B(6,0)$ and $C(0,8)$. Find its circumcentre, and the radius of its circumcircle.

The circumcentre is where the perpendicular bisectors meet, so two of them are enough. Which two? The ones that are least work — and here two sides lie along the axes, so their bisectors can be written down without any calculation.

Solution.

Step 1 $AB$ runs along the $x$-axis, $0$ to $6$, so its bisector is $x = 3$ bisector of $AB$
Step 2 $AC$ runs along the $y$-axis, $0$ to $8$, so its bisector is $y = 4$ bisector of $AC$
Step 3 those two lines cross at $O(3,4)$ the circumcentre
Step 4 $OA = \sqrt{3^{2} + 4^{2}} = \sqrt{25} = 5$ distance to a vertex
Answer The circumcentre is $O(3,4)$, and the radius is $5$.
Check $OB$ and $OC$ come to $5$ as well as Theorem 1 says

Check the other two rather than assume them. All three agree, as Theorem 1 says they must.

Two bisectors settled it, and the third was never needed. That is not a shortcut but the content of Theorem 1: all three pass through one point, so once two of them have been crossed the third has nothing left to say.

Which two you take is therefore free, and should be decided on the arithmetic alone. Here two sides lay along the axes and their bisectors could be written down at once. A different labelling of the same triangle would have made a different pair the easy one, and given the same centre.

The check at the end is worth keeping all the same. It was made on the radii rather than the bisectors, and if $OB$ or $OC$ had come out anything but $5$ the fault would have been in the working, since Theorem 1 leaves no room for it to be anywhere else.
