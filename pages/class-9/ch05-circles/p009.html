Three non-collinear points also determine a triangle, and what we have just built is the circle through its vertices. Both the circle and its centre have names.

The circle through the vertices of a triangle is its circumcircle, and its centre is the circumcentre — the point where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet. The circle is said to circumscribe the triangle, and the triangle to be inscribed in the circle.

Note what the argument gave us along the way. All three perpendicular bisectors pass through $O$, though only two were used to find it; the third has no choice in the matter, since $O$ is equidistant from $B$ and $C$ too.

Theorem 1 says the circumcircle exists and that there is only one. It says nothing about where the centre lands — and the answer is not the one you might guess, because the circumcentre need not lie inside the triangle at all.

Example 3

A triangle has vertices $A(0,0)$, $B(6,0)$ and $C(0,8)$. Find its circumcentre, and the radius of its circumcircle.

The circumcentre is where the perpendicular bisectors meet, so two of them are enough. Which two? The ones that are least work — and here two sides lie along the axes, so their bisectors can be written down without any calculation.

bisector of AB $AB$ runs along the $x$-axis, $0$ to $6$ $x = 3$
bisector of AC $AC$ runs along the $y$-axis, $0$ to $8$ $y = 4$

Those two lines cross at $O(3,4)$, so that is the circumcentre. The radius is its distance from any one vertex:

$OA = \sqrt{3^{2} + 4^{2}} = \sqrt{25} = 5$

Check the other two rather than assume them: $OB$ and $OC$ come to $5$ as well. All three agree, as Theorem 1 says they must.
