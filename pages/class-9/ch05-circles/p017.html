Notice how little of that proof was about circles. Three of its four lines are congruence; the circle enters only to say that $CA$ and $CF$ are radii, and — through Theorem 4 — that $CE$ and $CH$ meet the chords at right angles.

The first line is doing quiet work too. Writing $AE = \tfrac{1}{2}AB$ is Theorem 5 being spent: it is only because the perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord that $E$ is the midpoint at all. A proof that leans on an earlier theorem in a single symbol is easy to read past.

There is a second route. Triangles $CAB$ and $CFG$ are congruent by SSS — two pairs of radii and the given equal chords — and $CE$ and $CH$ are corresponding altitudes of congruent triangles. Either argument will do; the first has the advantage of never leaving the right-angled halves.

Turn it round and the converse follows from the same two triangles, with the given fact and the wanted one exchanged once more.

Theorem 7

Chords of a circle that lie at the same distance from the centre are equal in length.

$CE = CH$ is now given, and $CA = CF = r$.

$\angle CEA = \angle CHF = 90^\circ$, so RHS gives $\triangle CEA \cong \triangle CHF$ again.

So $AE = FH$, and doubling gives $AB = FG$.
