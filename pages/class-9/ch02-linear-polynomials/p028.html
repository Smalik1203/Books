What this chapter established
  1. An algebraic expression is built from numbers, variables and operation symbols. Its terms are the parts added together, its coefficients the numbers multiplying the variables.
  2. A univariate polynomial is an expression in one variable and its whole-number powers. Its degree is the highest power appearing in it, and the degree is what sorts polynomials into families: cubic, quadratic, linear, constant.
  3. A polynomial of degree $1$ is a linear polynomial. Evaluated at $1$, $2$, $3$, … it produces a linear pattern — a sequence whose consecutive terms differ by the same amount throughout.
  4. Linear growth is a pattern increasing by a constant amount over equal intervals; linear decay is one decreasing by a constant amount. They differ only in the sign of that constant.
  5. A linear relationship between $x$ and $y$ is written $y = ax + b$. Two observations are enough to recover $a$ and $b$, since each supplies one equation.
  6. Its graph is a straight line. The slope is $a$, and it is the constant difference of the pattern: positive for growth, negative for decay. The $y$-intercept is $b$, and the line cuts the $y$-axis at $(0, b)$.
  7. The two numbers act independently. Changing $a$ tilts the line about its intercept; changing $b$ slides it without tilting — so lines of equal slope and unequal intercept are parallel and never meet.
  8. A table, an expression and a graph are three views of one relationship. Which to reach for is a matter of what you want to see: a table gives particular values, an expression gives all of them at once, and a graph shows you the shape of the whole thing.
