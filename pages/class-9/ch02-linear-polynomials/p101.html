The Foundation Bridge
Take what you know. Go further.

One expression, $ax + b$, and everything in the chapter followed from it. Ten pages of harder work on that same expression follow — no new syllabus, only questions of the kind asked outside school.

Two points are enough. Three are often too many

Two observations fix $a$ and $b$, because there are two unknowns and each observation gives one equation. Take a third and something new can happen: it may disagree. The bills $(10, 350)$, $(20, 550)$ and $(30, 780)$ cannot all come from one linear rule, since the first two force $y = 20x + 150$ and that predicts $750$, not $780$.

A scientist measuring a spring takes a dozen readings, not two, knowing they will not lie on one line exactly. Then the question stops being what line passes through these points and becomes what line comes closest to all of them — a different question, and the beginning of statistics.

Differences, and differences of differences

A linear polynomial has constant first differences. That is a test you can run on any table, and it goes further than degree $1$. Evaluate $x^2$ at $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$: the values $1, 4, 9, 16, 25$ have differences $3, 5, 7, 9$, which are not constant — but their differences are all $2$.

Reading the degree off a table

Constant first differences mean degree $1$; constant second differences mean degree $2$; and the constant never appears at all for a pattern that doubles. Differencing a table is the fastest way to find out what kind of rule made it.

Where a formula stops being true

The auto-rickshaw fare was $15n - 5$, and the tank's depth was $3 - 0.5t$. Both formulas go on answering long after they have stopped describing anything: the fare rule is wrong for the first two kilometres, and the depth rule reports $-1$ metres of water in the eighth month.
