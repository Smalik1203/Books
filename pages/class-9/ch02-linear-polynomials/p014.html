2.5Linear Relationships

Every pattern in this chapter has tied one quantity to another: a stage number to a tile count, a distance to a fare, a month to a depth. Written with the two quantities called $x$ and $y$, all of them take the same form.

$$y = ax + b$$

This is a linear relationship, and $a$ and $b$ are what distinguish one from another. The tile pattern of Fig. 2.4 is the case $a = 2$, $b = -1$; Bela's pocket money is $a = -5$, $b = 100$.

The two letters are doing different jobs, and separating them is most of the skill. $a$ is the rate: how much $y$ moves for each step of $x$. $b$ is the starting value: what $y$ is when $x$ is $0$, before anything has happened at all. In the chess club of Example 5, $a$ was the ₹$50$ a match and $b$ the ₹$200$ joining fee — one is charged repeatedly, the other exactly once.

Once a relationship is in this form, a great deal follows without further thought. The sign of $a$ says whether it is growth or decay. Its size is the constant difference of the pattern. And $b$ can be read off by asking a single question: what happens at $x = 0$?

Reading $a$ and $b$ off a rule you already have is easy. The useful skill is the reverse one — given a few measurements, find the rule they came from. Two measurements turn out to be enough, and the reason is worth holding on to: there are exactly two unknowns, so two independent facts pin them both down.

Independent is the word to watch. Two readings taken at the same value of $x$ say the same thing twice and settle nothing, however carefully they were measured. The pair has to differ in $x$ — and the further apart they are, the less a small error in either one disturbs the answer.
