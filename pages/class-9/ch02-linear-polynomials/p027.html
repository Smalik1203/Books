Where this goes next

Three threads have been left deliberately loose, and each is picked up in a later chapter.

The first is the equation. Setting a linear polynomial equal to a number gave a linear equation with one answer, and that was enough for Example 6. But $y = ax + b$ has two letters in it, and a single equation in two unknowns has infinitely many solutions — which is exactly what the straight line of Section 2.6 is a picture of. Every point on it is a solution. Asking for one particular solution means asking a second question, and two lines crossing at a point is what that looks like.

The second is the slope. It was defined here by comparison — $y = 2x$ is steeper than $y = x$ — and read off the equation as the coefficient of $x$. That is enough for lines through the origin, but it does not yet say how to measure the steepness of a line you have been handed as a drawing, or as two points. The chapter on linear equations does.

The third is the pattern. The sequences $1, 3, 5, 7, \ldots$ and $100, 95, 90, \ldots$ were called linear because their consecutive terms differ by a constant. Sequences and Progressions takes that constant difference as the defining property and asks the questions this chapter did not: what the $n$th term is in general, and what the first $n$ terms add up to.

What ties all three together is the thing worth carrying out of this chapter. A relationship you can write as $ax + b$ is completely determined by two numbers, and two observations are enough to find them. That is a strong claim, and it is false the moment the degree rises above one.
