Two things are visible at once. Every line of the form $y = ax$ passes through the origin — unavoidably, since $x = 0$ forces $y = 0$. And the lines fan out in order of $a$: the line $y = x$ leans equally on both axes, $y = 2x$ is steeper than it, and $y = \dfrac{1}{2}x$ is shallower.

Slope

In $y = ax$ the multiplier $a$ is the slope of the line. It is what steepness is, measured: a line with $a \gt 1$ rises more sharply than $y = x$, and one with $a \lt 1$ less sharply. Slope is taken up properly in the chapter on linear equations.

Slope also settles a question left open earlier in this chapter. A linear growth has a positive slope and a linear decay a negative one — and the size of the slope is exactly the constant difference the pattern goes up or down by. The tile pattern of Fig. 2.4 had relationship $y = 2x - 1$ and gained two tiles a stage; its slope is $2$. Bela's pocket money fell by ₹$5$ a day and its relationship was $y = 100 - 5x$; its slope is $-5$.

That is a genuine unification. Until this section the constant difference was a fact about a list of numbers — you found it by subtracting one term from the next — while the steepness of a line was a fact about a picture. They have turned out to be the same number arrived at two ways, and either one can be used to find the other.

It also explains why a linear pattern has to plot as a straight line rather than a curve. Going one step to the right always changes $y$ by the same amount, so the direction never alters; and a path whose direction never alters is straight. A pattern whose difference itself changed would bend.
