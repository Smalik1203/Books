The three lines never meet. They cannot: equal slopes mean equal steepness, and two lines of the same steepness in the same plane either coincide or stay the same distance apart for ever. Changing $b$ has not tilted the line at all — it has slid it up or down.

What does Fig. 2.10 say about $y = ax + b$ when $a$ is held fixed and $b$ is allowed to vary? Here $a = 2$ throughout, and $b$ took the values $-1$, $1$ and $5$.

Check it against the arithmetic rather than the eye. At $x = 1$ the three expressions give $1$, $3$ and $7$; at $x = 2$ they give $3$, $5$ and $9$. The gaps between them — $2$ and $4$ — are the same at both values of $x$, and will stay the same at every other, because the $2x$ cancels when one expression is subtracted from another. What is left is the difference of the constants.

That is the whole of it. Two lines with the same slope differ by a fixed amount everywhere, so the distance between them never closes, so they never meet. The picture and the algebra are saying the same thing.

If $b$ slides the line, it should be readable off the picture somewhere. It is — at the one place every one of these lines is easy to find.

Draw $y = x + 3$, $y = 2x + 5$ and $y = 3x - 2$ — three different slopes this time — and watch where each one crosses the $y$-axis.
