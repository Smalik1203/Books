Example 14

Draw $y = \dfrac{1}{2}x$, $y = x$ and $y = 2x$ on one pair of axes.

Solution.

Step 1 two points are enough for each two points fix a line
Step 2 for $y = \dfrac{1}{2}x$ take $(0, 0)$ and $(4, 2)$
Step 3 for $y = x$ take $(0, 0)$ and $(3, 3)$
Step 4 for $y = 2x$ take $(0, 0)$ and $(2, 4)$
Answer plot each pair and join it with a ruler: the three lines of Fig. 2.8

Notice how little work each of those three lines cost. Every one of them passes through the origin, so one of the two points needed was known before the equation was even read — and $(0, 0)$ is the easiest point on the paper to mark accurately. Only the second point had to be worked out.

That is a consequence of $b$ being $0$ in each. A line $y = ax + b$ with $b$ not zero passes through $(0, b)$ instead, which is just as easy to mark and just as free.

Two things are visible at once. Every line of the form $y = ax$ passes through the origin — unavoidably, since $x = 0$ forces $y = 0$. And the lines fan out in order of $a$: the line $y = x$ leans equally on both axes, $y = 2x$ is steeper than it, and $y = \dfrac{1}{2}x$ is shallower.
