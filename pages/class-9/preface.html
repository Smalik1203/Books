Preface

Mathematics is not a list of rules to be remembered. It is the habit of asking why something must be so, and then showing that it is — and that habit is what this book sets out to teach.

Each chapter states an idea, works through it beside the theory rather than after it, and ends with practice graded from the straightforward to the genuinely hard. Where a result can be settled in three lines, it is settled. Where it cannot be settled yet, the book says so, and says what would be needed.

The mathematics here was made by people, over a long time and in many places, and this book says whose it is. Brahmagupta gave zero its rules in 628 CE. Baudhāyana met a length that no ratio would name while laying out an altar. Mādhava wrote an infinite series for π in fourteenth-century Kerala. A student who knows that mathematics has a history is less likely to believe it fell from the sky.

Two things are worth knowing about how to read it. Do not skip the worked examples: they carry as much of the argument as the paragraphs around them. And when a question is put to you in a panel — Think and Reflect — stop and try it before reading on. The answer is usually a few lines further down, and it is worth much less once you have seen it.

Every chapter then goes ten pages further, in a section called Beyond the Book, which takes the same ideas somewhere new without adding a line to the syllabus. None of it is examinable. It is there because a subject met only in an examination is a subject that will not be remembered.
