The Linear Pair Property

If two adjacent angles form a linear pair, they are supplementary: their measures add to $180^\circ$.

Why is this true? Because the two outer arms make a straight line, so turning from one of them to the other is a turn of $180^\circ$ — and the shared arm sits inside that turn, cutting it into the two angles. The whole turn is the sum of its two parts. That is all the argument there is, and it is enough.

The property runs the other way too, and the converse is less obvious than it looks. If two adjacent angles add to $180^\circ$, their outer arms must lie in a straight line — otherwise the turn from one to the other would not be $180^\circ$.

Example 1

In Fig. 6.3 (right), $\angle POR = 118^\circ$. Find $\angle ROQ$.

$OP$ and $OQ$ are opposite rays, so $\angle POR$ and $\angle ROQ$ are a linear pair.

$\angle POR + \angle ROQ = 180^\circ$
$118^\circ + \angle ROQ = 180^\circ$
$\angle ROQ = 62^\circ$

Nothing was measured. The single fact that $P$, $O$ and $Q$ lie in a line fixed the answer.

Example 2

Two adjacent angles on a line are in the ratio $4 : 5$. How big is each?

Being adjacent angles on a line, they are a linear pair, so they add to $180^\circ$. Write them as $4x$ and $5x$, which is what the ratio permits.

$4x + 5x = 180^\circ$
$9x = 180^\circ$, so $x = 20^\circ$

The angles are $80^\circ$ and $100^\circ$. As a check, they do add to $180^\circ$, and they are in the ratio $80 : 100 = 4 : 5$.
