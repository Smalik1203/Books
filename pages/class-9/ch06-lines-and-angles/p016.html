Example 9

In triangle $ABC$, side $BC$ is produced to $D$. If $\angle ACD = 128^\circ$ and $\angle B = 47^\circ$, find $\angle A$ and $\angle ACB$.

Solution.

Step 1 $\angle A + \angle B = 128^\circ$ exterior angle at $C$
Step 2 $\angle A = 128^\circ - 47^\circ = 81^\circ$
Step 3 $\angle ACB = 180^\circ - 128^\circ = 52^\circ$ linear pair with $\angle ACD$
Answer $\angle A = 81^\circ$ and $\angle ACB = 52^\circ$
Check $81 + 47 + 52 = 180$

The check is not a formality — it catches a wrong subtraction at once.

Example 10

In triangle $PQR$, the bisectors of $\angle Q$ and $\angle R$ meet at $O$. Show that $\angle QOR = 90^\circ + \tfrac{1}{2}\angle P$.

Solution.

Step 1 triangle $QOR$ has the angle $\angle QOR$ and the half-angles at $Q$ and $R$ the bisectors
Step 2 $\angle QOR = 180^\circ - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle Q - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle R$ angle sum in that triangle
Step 3 $\angle Q + \angle R = 180^\circ - \angle P$ angle sum in the whole triangle
Step 4 $\tfrac{1}{2}\angle Q + \tfrac{1}{2}\angle R = 90^\circ - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle P$ halving Step 3
Step 5 $\angle QOR = 180^\circ - (90^\circ - \tfrac{1}{2}\angle P)$ Step 4 in Step 2
Answer $\angle QOR = 90^\circ + \tfrac{1}{2}\angle P$

The result holds for every triangle, so $\angle QOR$ can never be a right angle or less — the bisectors always meet at an obtuse angle, however flat the triangle is made.

The formula is worth testing at its edges. In an equilateral triangle $\angle P = 60^\circ$, so $\angle QOR = 120^\circ$. Flatten the triangle until $\angle P$ is almost nothing and $\angle QOR$ falls towards $90^\circ$ without ever arriving; open $\angle P$ out towards $180^\circ$ and $\angle QOR$ climbs towards $180^\circ$ with it.

So $\angle QOR$ runs over the obtuse angles and nothing else, which is what the paragraph above claimed. A formula that survives its own extreme cases is usually right, and one that does not is certainly wrong.
