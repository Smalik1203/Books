Corresponding same side of $t$, and matching positions at the two crossings: $(\angle 1, \angle 5)$, $(\angle 2, \angle 6)$, $(\angle 4, \angle 8)$, $(\angle 3, \angle 7)$
Alternate interior inside, on opposite sides of $t$: $(\angle 4, \angle 6)$, $(\angle 3, \angle 5)$
Alternate exterior outside, on opposite sides of $t$: $(\angle 1, \angle 7)$, $(\angle 2, \angle 8)$
Co-interior inside, on the same side of $t$: $(\angle 4, \angle 5)$, $(\angle 3, \angle 6)$

A word of warning about what has and has not been claimed. Not one of these four names says anything about size. They are descriptions of where an angle sits, and they apply just as well to the messy figure you get when $m$ and $n$ are nowhere near parallel. In that case corresponding angles are simply unequal, and there is nothing wrong with the figure.

Four position words, no promises. What happens when $m$ and $n$ are parallel is the next section, and it has to be argued for separately.

Two remarks will save you time later. First, "alternate" always means opposite sides of the transversal — the word is doing the work of "on the other side". Second, co-interior angles are the odd ones out: every other pair on the list turns out to be equal when the lines are parallel, while co-interior angles turn out to be supplementary. If you remember one exception, remember that one.

6.6When the Two Lines Are Parallel

Two lines in a plane that never meet, however far they are produced either way, are called parallel. We write $m \parallel n$. The definition is a statement about the whole infinite length of both lines, which is exactly why it cannot be checked by looking: you can see that two lines have not met yet, but not that they never will.

So we need some property of parallel lines that can be checked locally, near a crossing, and that is what the transversal gives us. Draw two parallel lines and a transversal, and measure a pair of corresponding angles. Do it again with a different pair of parallels, a different transversal. Corresponding angles come out equal every time.

This one we do not prove. It is taken as the starting point — an axiom, a statement assumed rather than deduced — because something has to be assumed about parallel lines before anything can be deduced about them, and this is the cleanest thing to assume.
