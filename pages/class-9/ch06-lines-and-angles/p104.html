The mirrors are parallel and $BC$ crosses both, so $\angle BCP$ is alternate to $\angle CBS$ and is also $\theta$. At $C$ the ray leaves at the angle it arrived at, so $\angle DCQ = \theta$ too, and the straight angle at $C$ gives $\angle BCD = 180^\circ - 2\theta$.

Now $\angle ABC$ and $\angle BCD$ are equal, and they are alternate angles for the lines $AB$ and $CD$ with $BC$ as the transversal. Equal alternate angles make the lines parallel, so $AB \parallel CD$. The mirrors were used twice, once to carry $\theta$ from $B$ to $C$ and once, through the converse, to carry the answer back.

In triangle $ABC$, the bisector of $\angle B$ and the bisector of the exterior angle at $C$ meet at $E$. Find $\angle BEC$ when $\angle A = 64^\circ$. Then find it for any $\angle A$.

Produce $BC$ to $D$, so that the exterior angle at $C$ is $\angle ACD$. The exterior angle theorem, used in triangle $ABC$, says $\angle ACD = \angle A + \angle B$, and $CE$ takes half of it:

$$\angle ECD = \tfrac{1}{2}\angle A + \tfrac{1}{2}\angle B$$

Now use the same theorem again, in triangle $EBC$. Its exterior angle at $C$ is the same $\angle ECD$, and its two interior opposite angles are $\angle EBC = \tfrac{1}{2}\angle B$ and $\angle BEC$. So $\angle ECD = \tfrac{1}{2}\angle B + \angle BEC$. Put the two statements side by side and the half of $\angle B$ appears in both:

$$\angle BEC = \tfrac{1}{2}\angle A$$

With $\angle A = 64^\circ$, $\angle BEC = 32^\circ$. One theorem, used in two triangles that share an exterior angle, did the whole of the work, and $\angle B$ and $\angle C$ were never needed.

Look back at the five questions. Not one of them named a result, and not one of them needed a result the chapter had not already proved. Three of them turned on a total — the angle sum, a linear pair, an exterior angle — and halved it or split it rather than finding every angle it was made of. The other two turned on a line that was not drawn, or on reading an angle from one crossing at another. That is most of what this chapter is for.
