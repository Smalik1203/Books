The same four lines, with the letters shuffled, give $\angle 2 = \angle 4$. And notice what the proof does not depend on: not on how steeply the lines cross, not on which is drawn first, not on the paper. It uses only that both pairs sit on straight lines. So it holds for every crossing of every two lines there could be — which is a great deal more than a protractor could ever have told us.

  2. Can two of the four angles at a crossing be $70^\circ$ and $120^\circ$?
For the second, there are only two ways a pair of the four can be related. Try each.

One more fact belongs here, and it comes from the same idea of turning. Draw any number of rays out from a single point. Going round from the first ray to the next, and the next, and so on all the way back to the first, you have turned through one complete revolution. So the angles round a point, taken in order, add to $360^\circ$.

Example 3

Four rays from a point $O$ make angles of $x$, $2x$, $3x$ and $120^\circ$ in order round the point. Find $x$, and say which of the four angles is obtuse.

The four angles fill one complete turn.

$x + 2x + 3x + 120^\circ = 360^\circ$
$6x = 240^\circ$, so $x = 40^\circ$

The four angles are $40^\circ$, $80^\circ$, $120^\circ$ and $120^\circ$. Two of them are obtuse — the given $120^\circ$ and the $3x$, which turns out to be $120^\circ$ as well. It is worth noticing that the algebra did not know they would come out equal; we found it out only after solving.
