That example is worth one more look, because of how little it needed. Two parallel lines and a transversal make eight angles, and one measurement settled all eight. It could not have been otherwise: every angle in the figure is either equal to the $58^\circ$ or supplementary to it, so the eight take only two values, $58^\circ$ and $122^\circ$, four of each.

That is a useful thing to expect. If a figure of this kind ever yields a third value, either the lines were not parallel or a step has gone astray.

Example 6

A transversal cuts two lines so that a pair of co-interior angles measure $(3x + 10)^\circ$ and $(2x + 40)^\circ$. For what value of $x$ are the two lines parallel?

Solution.

Step 1 the lines are parallel exactly when the pair is supplementary, so set the sum to $180^\circ$ converse for co-interior angles
Step 2 $(3x + 10) + (2x + 40) = 180$
Step 3 $5x + 50 = 180$, so $x = 26$
Answer $x = 26$
Check the angles are $88^\circ$ and $92^\circ$, which do add to $180^\circ$

For any other value of $x$ the lines meet somewhere, and the algebra even tells you on which side, though we will not chase that here.

6.7Parallel to the Same Line

Here is a fact that feels too obvious to need proving, which is usually a sign that it is worth proving. If two lines are each parallel to a third line, are they parallel to each other?

Let $m \parallel k$ and $n \parallel k$. Draw a transversal $t$ cutting all three. Because $m \parallel k$, the corresponding angles at $m$ and $k$ are equal. Because $n \parallel k$, the corresponding angles at $n$ and $k$ are equal. So the corresponding angles at $m$ and $n$ are equal to each other, both being equal to the one at $k$ — and by the converse of the axiom, $m \parallel n$.

Once again the whole argument is "two things equal to a third are equal to each other", dressed in geometry. A surprising amount of this chapter is that sentence wearing different clothes.

Small as it looks, that result is what makes parallelism worth a word of its own. Because a line parallel to $k$ is parallel to every other line parallel to $k$, the lines of the plane fall into families. Pick any line and gather everything parallel to it, and you have a family no member of which is parallel to anything outside it. Every line in the plane belongs to exactly one such family, and two lines are parallel precisely when they belong to the same one.
