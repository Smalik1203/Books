Read the last sentence again, because it is doing something quietly. It says the size of an angle is an amount of turning — not a distance, not an area, not anything to do with how long the arms are drawn. Rub out the arms of an angle and draw them four times as long and you have not changed the angle at all. Beginners are caught by this constantly, and it is worth catching yourself at it once, on purpose, before the chapter goes on.

Angles are named after their vertex when there is no room for doubt — $\angle B$ — and by three letters when there is, the vertex always in the middle. In Fig. 6.2 the vertex $O$ carries three different angles, so $\angle O$ would be useless; we have to write $\angle AOB$, $\angle BOC$ and $\angle AOC$, and each names a different opening.

Angles are sorted by size, and the names are worth having because whole theorems are stated in them.

Acute more than $0^\circ$ and less than $90^\circ$
Right exactly $90^\circ$
Obtuse more than $90^\circ$ and less than $180^\circ$
Straight exactly $180^\circ$ — the two arms make one line
Reflex more than $180^\circ$ and less than $360^\circ$

Two rays out of a point in fact make two angles, not one: the opening you are looking at, and the whole of the rest of the turn. The two add to $360^\circ$, so one of them is reflex unless the rays are opposite and both come to $180^\circ$.

When we write $\angle AOB$ we mean the smaller of the pair, unless something says otherwise. That is why reflex angles are seldom named, although one of them is present at every crossing on every page of this chapter.
