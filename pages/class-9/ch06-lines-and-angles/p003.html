That last equation in the caption is not a theorem. It is a habit of measurement we shall use without comment throughout: if a ray lies inside an angle, it cuts that angle into two whose sizes add up to the whole. Turning from $OA$ to $OC$ is turning from $OA$ to $OB$ and then carrying on to $OC$.

6.3Angles That Come in Pairs

Angles rarely turn up alone. Where two lines cross, four appear at once, and they are not independent of one another — fix one and the other three are decided. Working out exactly how they are tied together is the first real piece of reasoning in this chapter, and everything later leans on it.

Two names first, and they are about sizes only. Two angles are complementary if they add to $90^\circ$, and supplementary if they add to $180^\circ$. The angles need not be anywhere near each other; $30^\circ$ in Delhi and $60^\circ$ in Chennai are complementary. It is a statement about two numbers.

Two more names, and these are about position. Two angles are adjacent when they share a vertex, share an arm, and lie on opposite sides of that shared arm. All three conditions matter: two angles that share a vertex but overlap are not adjacent, and neither are two that merely sit near each other.

The right-hand figure is the case worth its own name. When two adjacent angles have their outer arms in a straight line, they are called a linear pair. And a linear pair, between them, fill a straight angle.
