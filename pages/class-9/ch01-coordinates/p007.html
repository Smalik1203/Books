Now the general statement. The coordinates of a point $P$ in 2-D space are written $(x, y)$, where $x$ is the perpendicular distance of $P$ from the y-axis, measured along the x-axis, and $y$ is the perpendicular distance of $P$ from the x-axis, measured along the y-axis. We call $x$ the x-coordinate and $y$ the y-coordinate of the point $(x, y)$.

Try it on Fig. 1.4. The point $S (3, -5)$ lies in Quadrant IV, with x-coordinate 3 and y-coordinate $-5$; the point $Q (-5, 3)$ lies in Quadrant II, with x-coordinate $-5$ and y-coordinate 3. The same two numbers, swapped — and a different place entirely.

Copy Fig. 1.4 and mark $S$ and $Q$ on your own diagram. Then add a point $P$ anywhere in Quadrant I and a point $R$ anywhere in Quadrant III, and write down their coordinates.

The bracket $(x, y)$ is an ordered pair, and the word ordered is carrying its full weight. A pair of numbers on their own is just a pair. What makes $(3, -5)$ a point is the agreement, settled in advance and never varied, that the first number is read along the x-axis and the second along the y-axis. Change that agreement and every point in the chapter moves.

The two numbers have names of their own, still in use and worth recognising. The x-coordinate is the abscissa — from the Latin for cut off, since it is the length cut off along the horizontal axis — and the y-coordinate is the ordinate. The words are older than the notation: they were in use before anyone thought to write the two numbers side by side in a bracket at all.

Notice what the definition asks you to measure. The abscissa of $P$ is its distance from the y-axis, not from the x-axis, and the ordinate is its distance from the x-axis. It reads backwards the first few times. The reason is that to travel along the x-axis you must first leave the y-axis, so the distance you cover is measured from the line you started on.
