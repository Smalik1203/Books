Where this goes next

Two numbers, and the whole plane is addressable: enough to locate a doorway, to measure a distance without a ruler, and to check whether three points fall in line. In Grade 10 the same pair of numbers begins to carry equations rather than single points, and a line or a curve becomes something you can write down and work with algebraically.

The bridge between geometry and algebra that Descartes built, on foundations laid in Bhārat, is one you will cross in both directions from here on.

What this chapter established
  1. Locating a point in a plane takes two perpendicular lines — one horizontal, one vertical. The horizontal one is the x-axis, the vertical one the y-axis, and together they are the coordinate axes.
  2. The plane they lie in is the Cartesian plane, the coordinate plane or the xy-plane. The axes cut it into four quadrants, and cross at the origin, whose coordinates are $(0, 0)$.
  3. The distance of a point from the y-axis is its x-coordinate, and its distance from the x-axis is its y-coordinate. Written together as $(x, y)$, they are the coordinates of the point.
  4. A point on the x-axis has coordinates of the form $(x, 0)$; a point on the y-axis, of the form $(0, y)$.
  5. The signs alone say which quadrant a point is in: $(+, +)$ in the first, $(-, +)$ in the second, $(-, -)$ in the third and $(+, -)$ in the fourth.
  6. Order matters. If $x = y$ then $(x, y) = (y, x)$; if $x \ne y$ they are two different points.
  7. Distance is a difference of coordinates when a segment is parallel to an axis: $|x_2 - x_1|$ between $(x_1, y)$ and $(x_2, y)$, and $|y_2 - y_1|$ between $(x, y_1)$ and $(x, y_2)$. Every other case is the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem, which contains both as the cases where one of the shifts is zero:

    $\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$
