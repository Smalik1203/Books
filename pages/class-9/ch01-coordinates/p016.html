1. Two main roads cross at the centre of a city, one running North–South and the other East–West. Every other street runs parallel to one of them, 200 m apart, and there are 10 streets in each direction.
    1. Draw a model of the city in your notebook at 1 cm to 200 m, showing each street as a single line.
    2. Name each crossing by the pair of streets that form it: where the 2nd North–South street meets the 5th East–West street, call the crossing $(2, 5)$. Using that rule, how many crossings can be called $(4, 3)$? How many can be called $(3, 4)$?
  1. A graphics program draws on a rectangular screen whose origin is the bottom-left corner. The screen is 800 pixels wide and 600 pixels high. One circular icon of radius 80 pixels is centred at $A (100, 150)$; another, of radius 100 pixels, is centred at $B (250, 230)$. Determine:
    1. whether any part of either circle falls outside the screen;
    2. whether the two circles overlap.
  1. Plot $A (2, 1)$, $B (-1, 2)$, $C (-2, -1)$ and $D (1, -2)$. Is $ABCD$ a square? Explain how you know, and find its area.

Where this goes next

Two numbers, and the whole plane is addressable: enough to locate a doorway, to measure a distance without a ruler, and to check whether three points fall in line. In Grade 10 the same pair of numbers begins to carry equations rather than single points, and a line or a curve becomes something you can write down and work with algebraically.

The bridge between geometry and algebra that Descartes built, on foundations laid in Bhārat, is one you will cross in both directions from here on.
