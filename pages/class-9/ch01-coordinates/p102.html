Think and Reason
Judge a claim — and the reason offered for it.

Questions 1–4 take this key. (a) both true, R explains A; (b) both true, R does not explain A; (c) A true, R false; (d) A false, R true.

A reasoning question has no working to show. It wants the sentence that would convince someone who disagreed — and one counter-example kills a false claim faster than an argument.

  1. A. It never matters which of two points is called the first.  R. The formula squares each shift, and a square does not keep a sign.
  2. A. The distance between $(a, b)$ and $(b, a)$ is $\sqrt{2}\,|a - b|$.  R. Both points lie the same distance from the origin.
  3. A. Multiplying both coordinates of a point by $-1$ leaves its distance from the origin unchanged.  R. The point has been reflected in the $y$-axis.
  4. A. A point equidistant from the two axes lies on one of two lines through the origin.  R. The distance of $(x, y)$ from the $x$-axis is $|y|$ and from the $y$-axis is $|x|$.
  5. For $P(-4, 0)$, which must be true?
    1. $P$ lies in Quadrant II.
    2. $P$ lies on the $x$-axis.
    3. $P$ is $4$ units from the origin.
    1. (i) only
    2. (ii) only
    3. (ii) and (iii)
    4. all three
  6. The distance of $(x, y)$ from the origin equals $x + y$. Which must be true?
    1. It holds for $(3, 0)$.
    2. It holds for $(3, 4)$.
    3. It holds whenever one coordinate is $0$ and the other is not negative.
    1. (i) only
    2. (i) and (iii)
    3. (ii) and (iii)
    4. all three
  7. For a segment parallel to an axis the chapter offers two rules: a difference of coordinates, and the full formula. Show that they never disagree, and say what the formula does with the coordinate that is not changing.
