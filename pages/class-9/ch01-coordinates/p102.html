Try it first

The point $P (a, b)$ lies in Quadrant II. In which quadrants do $(b, a)$, $(-a, b)$ and $(a, -b)$ lie?

In Quadrant II the x-coordinate is negative and the y-coordinate is positive, so $a < 0$ and $b > 0$. Then $(b, a)$ has a positive first coordinate and a negative second one, which is Quadrant IV. The point $(-a, b)$ has both coordinates positive, Quadrant I. And $(a, -b)$ has both negative, Quadrant III. Notice that we did not need the values of $a$ and $b$. The chapter said the quadrant is decided by the two signs alone, and here the signs were all we were given. A point we cannot plot can still be placed.

Try it first

Is the triangle with vertices $A (1, 1)$, $B (4, 5)$ and $C (8, 2)$ right-angled?

The chapter measured sides. Three sides are three distances, and their squares are enough:

$AB^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 25$

$BC^2 = 4^2 + 3^2 = 25$

$CA^2 = 7^2 + 1^2 = 50$

Now $AB^2 + BC^2 = 50 = CA^2$. The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem says that a right-angled triangle has this relation, and the theorem also holds the other way round: if the squares on two sides add to the square on the third, the angle between those two sides is a right angle. So the angle at $B$ is a right angle (Fig. 1B.2). And since $AB = BC$, the triangle is isosceles as well. Notice that we measured no angle, and we did not take a single square root. The formula gives lengths, and the theorem that gave us the formula then reads an angle off the lengths.
