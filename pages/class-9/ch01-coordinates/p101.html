The Foundation Bridge
Take what you know. Go further.

The chapter gave you two numbers and one formula. Ten pages of harder work on exactly those follow — no new syllabus, only questions of the kind asked outside school.

The distance you walk is not the distance you measure

In the gridded city at the end of the chapter, nobody walks along a hypotenuse. To get from the crossing $(1, 2)$ to the crossing $(5, 7)$ you go four blocks east and five north, in whatever order — and the walk is nine blocks however you arrange them, while the straight line is only $\sqrt{41} \approx 6.4$.

$$\text{grid distance} = |x_2 - x_1| + |y_2 - y_1|$$

Both are honest measurements of the same pair of points. Which one is the right distance depends on what may move along it — a pedestrian, or a bird.

Two numbers, then three

Nothing in the argument of Section 1.4 used the fact that the plane is flat. Drop a perpendicular in space instead and the same theorem, applied twice, gives the distance between $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ as

$$\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2}$$

The plane formula is that one with $z_1 = z_2$. A pattern that keeps working when a dimension is added is usually a definition worth trusting, not a coincidence.

Any fraction of the way

The midpoint of $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$ is the average of the two coordinate pairs. That is the halfway case of something more useful: to go a fraction $t$ of the way from $A$ to $B$, take $t$ of each shift and add it to $A$.

A point part of the way along

$\left(x_1 + t(x_2 - x_1),\; y_1 + t(y_2 - y_1)\right)$

$t = \frac{1}{2}$ gives the midpoint, $t = \frac{1}{3}$ and $t = \frac{2}{3}$ the two points of trisection, $t = 0$ gives $A$ and $t = 1$ gives $B$.
