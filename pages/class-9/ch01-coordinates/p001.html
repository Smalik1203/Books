Orienting Yourself:
The Use of Coordinates

1.1Introduction

A system of coordinates is a framework of reference — the ruled lines of a map, the squares of graph paper — that lets numbers do the pointing. Give the numbers and you have named a place exactly: no landmarks, no gestures, no room for argument about which corner you meant.

Grid-based thinking, and the geometry that goes with it, has deep roots in Bhārat. The earliest systematic use of a grid was not drawn on paper at all. It was built, thousands of years ago, at the scale of a whole city. In the Sindhu–Sarasvatī Civilisation the streets ran North–South and East–West with striking precision, set about 10 metres apart — a coordinate system in daily use, since a merchant could find a shop or a warehouse by counting so many units north and so many east of the city centre.

Baudhāyana (c. 800 BCE) later took the same two directions into geometry, using North–South and East–West lines for the constructions that gave us the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem — the result this chapter will lean on when it comes to measuring distance. Between them, the city planners and the geometers had laid the ground for coordinate geometry.

Putting coordinates on the Earth itself mattered next, for navigation. Ujjayinī was described in the ancient world — as early as the 4th century BCE, in the early Siddhāntas — as the central meridian, the line of longitude from which every other place was measured. Ptolemy (c. 150 CE), building on Hipparchus and others, later listed the latitudes and longitudes of thousands of locations, among them ‘Ozine’, which is Ujjayinī.

Āryabhaṭa (c. 499 CE) replaced the Greek ‘chords’ with ‘sines’, which made the coordinates of a star or a city far easier to compute. He mapped the sky with celestial coordinates, measuring from the ecliptic — the path the Sun appears to take.
