$CD = $ x-coordinate of $D - $ x-coordinate of $A = 7 - 3 = 4$

$AC = $ y-coordinate of $A - $ y-coordinate of $D = 4 - 1 = 3$

Now the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem finishes the job:

$$AD = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5 \text{ units}$$

The other two sides go the same way. For $DM$ the shifts are 2 and 5; for $MA$ they are 6 and 2.

$DM = \sqrt{2^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{29}$ units

$MA = \sqrt{6^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{40}$ units

Nothing in that argument depended on the particular numbers. Take any two points, drop the same pair of perpendiculars, and the same right angle appears.

Fig. 1.8 does the same construction with letters in place of numbers. Call the two points $A (x_1, y_1)$ and $D (x_2, y_2)$. Drop a vertical from $A$ and a horizontal from $D$; they meet at $F (x_1, y_2)$, which shares its x-coordinate with $A$ and its y-coordinate with $D$. That is what makes the two legs easy to measure: $FD$ runs horizontally, so its length is $x_2 - x_1$, and $AF$ runs vertically, so its length is $y_2 - y_1$.

In general, then, the distance between the points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ is

$$\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$
