1.3The 2-D Cartesian Coordinate System

In the chapters on integers, rational numbers and decimals you worked with the number line, which is one-dimensional: one number fixes one point. Shalini’s floor needs two.

So the two-dimensional system uses two lines at right angles to each other. One is drawn horizontally, by convention, and is called the x-axis; the other is vertical, and is called the y-axis. Where they cross is the origin, written $O$, whose coordinates are $(0, 0)$. Together the two lines are the coordinate axes — ‘axes’ being the plural of ‘axis’ — and between them they let us locate any point of two-dimensional space, short form 2-D space.

Distances from $O$ are marked off in equal units along both axes. To the right of $O$, or upwards from it, counts as positive; to the left, or downwards, counts as negative. That single convention — Brahmagupta’s negatives, put to work — is what lets two numbers reach every point of the plane rather than a quarter of it.

Read the points of Fig. 1.2 off the axes one at a time. $B$ sits on the x-axis, 4.5 units to the right of $O$, so its coordinates are $(4.5, 0)$; we write $B = (4.5, 0)$. $G$ sits on the y-axis, 4.5 units below $O$, so $G = (0, -4.5)$. $H$ is on the same axis but 4 units above $O$, giving $H = (0, 4)$. And $E$, 2.9 units to the left of $O$ on the x-axis, is $E = (-2.9, 0)$.
