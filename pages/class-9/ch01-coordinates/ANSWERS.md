## 1.3 The 2-D Cartesian Coordinate System

### Exercise Set 1.1

1. - (i) $D_1$ is $8$ ft from the left wall (the y-axis) and $0$ ft from the

   - (ii) $D_1(8, 0)$.
   - (iii) The doorway is $11.5 - 8 = 3.5$ ft wide, about $107$ cm. That is

2. $B_1$ and $B_2$ lie on the y-axis, so the doorway is $4 - 1.5 = 2.5$ ft
   wide. It is **narrower** than the bedroom doorway, by $3.5 - 2.5 = 1$ ft.

### Think and Reflect (after Exercise Set 1.1)

   (about $2.5$ to $3$ ft).
2. Answers will vary. A wheelchair needs a clear opening of about $90$ cm and

### Running text (Section 1.3, Fig. 1.4)

### Think and Reflect (end of Section 1.3)

1. A point on the y-axis has x-coordinate $0$. The matching statement: a
   point on the x-axis has y-coordinate $0$.
2. Yes, but only when $x$ and $y$ are equal. $(y, x)$ and $(x, y)$ are the
   same point exactly when their first coordinates agree, $y = x$, and then
   their second coordinates agree too.
3. **True.** If $x = y$ the two pairs are the same pair. If $x \ne y$, the
   first coordinates differ, so the points differ.

## 1.4 Distance Between Two Points

### Running text (Fig. 1.6)

$AD = 5$, $DM = \sqrt{29}$ and $MA = \sqrt{40}$ units, worked in the text.

### Think and Reflect (Fig. 1.6)

1. $7 - 3 = 4$ units in the x-direction and $4 - 1 = 3$ units in the
   y-direction.
2. **Yes.** They are the legs of a right-angled triangle whose hypotenuse is
   $AD$, so $AD = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$.

### Running text (Fig. 1.9)

The images are $A'(-3, 4)$, $D'(-7, 1)$ and $M'(-9, 6)$, worked in the text.

### Think and Reflect (after Fig. 1.9)

1. The same: every side length ($5$, $\sqrt{29}$, $\sqrt{40}$), every angle,
   the shape and size of the triangle, and every y-coordinate. Changed: the
   sign of every x-coordinate, so the triangle's position, and it is turned
   over, as in a mirror.
2. **Yes.** Reflection in the x-axis changes the sign of every y-coordinate
   instead: the images are $(3, -4)$, $(7, -1)$ and $(9, -6)$. The shifts
   keep their sizes, so the side lengths are again $5$, $\sqrt{29}$ and
   $\sqrt{40}$.

## End-of-Chapter Exercises

1. The axes cross at the origin, $(0, 0)$: x-coordinate $0$ and y-coordinate
   $0$.

2. Every point on that line has x-coordinate $-5$, so $H = (-5, y)$ for some
   $y$. $H$ is in Quadrant II when $y > 0$, in Quadrant III when $y < 0$, and
   on the x-axis when $y = 0$.

3. - (i) $AM$ and $MP$: $A$ and $M$ share the y-coordinate $-2$, so $AM$ is
     horizontal; $M$ and $P$ share the x-coordinate $-5$, so $MP$ is
     vertical.
   - (ii) $AM$ is parallel to the x-axis ($MP$ is parallel to the y-axis).
   - (iii) $M(-5, -2)$ and $P(-5, 2)$ are mirror images in the x-axis.

4. Answers will vary. One instance: $I(5, -2)$ and $N(8, -2)$. Then
   $ZI = 4$, $IN = 3$ and $ZN = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, with the right angle
   at $I$.

5. Without negative numbers, a coordinate could only be measured to the right
   of or above the origin, so the system would reach only Quadrant I and the
   two positive half-axes. **No**, it could not reach every point of the
   plane.

6. **Yes.** $MA = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, $AG = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$ and
   $MG = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = 15$. Since $5 + 10 = 15$, the two shorter
   distances add to the longest, so $A$ lies on the segment $MG$. The method:
   if the two shorter distances between three points add to the longest,
   the points lie on one line; if they add to more, they make a triangle.

7. **No.** $RB = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, $BC = \sqrt{6^2 + 7^2} = \sqrt{85}$ and
   $RC = \sqrt{9^2 + 11^2} = \sqrt{202}$. Now
   $(5 + \sqrt{85})^2 = 110 + 10\sqrt{85}$, and $10\sqrt{85} = \sqrt{8500}$ is
   more than $\sqrt{8464} = 92$, so $(5 + \sqrt{85})^2$ is more than
   $202$. So $RB + BC$ is more than $RC$, and the points make a triangle.
   **Flagged**: in decimals the sums are $14.2195\ldots$ and $14.2127\ldots$,
   too close for the method to settle at two decimal places. The plot shows
   the points almost, but not quite, in line.

8. Answers will vary. One instance of each:
   - (i) $O(0, 0)$, $(4, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$: two sides of $4$, a right angle
     at $O$, and a third side of $\sqrt{32}$.
   - (ii) $O(0, 0)$, $(-3, -4)$ in Quadrant III and $(3, -4)$ in Quadrant IV:
     both are $5$ from $O$, so the triangle is isosceles.

9. $M$ is the midpoint when each of its coordinates is half way between those
   of $S$ and $T$ (the average of the two).
   - Row 1: **yes**; $0$ is half way between $-3$ and $3$, and $0$ between
     $0$ and $0$.
   - Row 2: **yes**; $3$ is half way between $2$ and $4$, and $4$ between
     $3$ and $5$.
   - Row 3: **no**; the midpoint is $(0, -5)$. $(0, 5)$ is $5$ from $S$ but
     $15$ from $T$.
   - Row 4: **no**; the midpoint is $(-1, 2)$.

10. The shift from $A$ to $M$ is $-7 - 3 = -10$ and $1 - (-4) = 5$. The same
    shift again carries $M$ to $B$: $-7 - 10 = -17$ and $1 + 5 = 6$, so
    $B(-17, 6)$.

11. The whole shift from $A$ to $B$ is $16 - 4 = 12$ and $-2 - 7 = -9$; a
    third of it is $4$ and $-3$. So $P(8, 4)$ and $Q(12, 1)$. (Check: $P$ is
    the midpoint of $A$ and $Q$, and $Q$ of $P$ and $B$.)

12. - (i) $OA^2 = 1 + 64 = 65$, $OB^2 = 16 + 49 = 65$ and
      $OC^2 = 49 + 16 = 65$. All three are $\sqrt{65}$ from $O$, so they lie
      on $K$, whose radius is $\sqrt{65}$.
    - (ii) $OD^2 = 25 + 36 = 61$, less than $65$: $D$ is **inside** $K$.
      $OE^2 = 0 + 81 = 81$, more than $65$: $E$ is **outside** $K$.

13. Taking $D$ as the midpoint of $BC$, $E$ of $CA$ and $F$ of $AB$:
    $A = E + F - D = (1, 7)$, $B = D + F - E = (-1, -1)$ and
    $C = D + E - F = (11, 3)$, adding and subtracting coordinate by
    coordinate. Check: the midpoint of $B$ and $C$ is $(5, 1)$, of $C$ and
    $A$ is $(6, 5)$, and of $A$ and $B$ is $(0, 3)$. **Flagged**: the question
    does not say which midpoint is on which side; this is the usual
    lettering.

14. - (i) A drawing: two perpendicular lines through the centre, with the
      other streets parallel to them $1$ cm apart. Answers will vary.
    - (ii) With the streets numbered from $1$ to $10$ in each direction,
      **one** crossing is $(4, 3)$ and **one** is $(3, 4)$, and they are two
      different crossings: the order of the pair matters. **Flagged**: if the
      streets are counted outward from the main roads on each side, $(4, 3)$
      could name a crossing in each of four quarters of the city; the
      question does not say which way the streets are numbered.

15. - (i) **No part falls outside.** The circle at $A$ reaches from
      $100 - 80 = 20$ to $100 + 80 = 180$ across and from $150 - 80 = 70$ to
      $150 + 80 = 230$ up. The circle at $B$ reaches from
      $250 - 100 = 150$ to $250 + 100 = 350$ across and from
      $230 - 100 = 130$ to $230 + 100 = 330$ up. All of these lie within
      $0$ to $800$ across and $0$ to $600$ up.
    - (ii) **Yes, they overlap.**
      $AB = \sqrt{150^2 + 80^2} = \sqrt{28900} = 170$, less than the sum of
      the radii, $80 + 100 = 180$.

16. **Yes.** $AB^2 = 9 + 1 = 10$, $BC^2 = 1 + 9 = 10$, $CD^2 = 9 + 1 = 10$
    and $DA^2 = 1 + 9 = 10$, so the four sides are equal. The diagonals are
    equal too: $AC^2 = 16 + 4 = 20$ and $BD^2 = 4 + 16 = 20$. And
    $AB^2 + BC^2 = 20 = AC^2$, so the angle at $B$ is a right angle. A
    rhombus with a right angle is a square. Its area is $AB^2 = 10$ square
    units.

17. - (i) $(0, 0)$, $(6, 0)$, $(6, 4)$ and $(0, 4)$.
    - (ii) Every coordinate changed. The lengths of the sides ($6$ and $4$),
      the diagonal, the area ($24$) and the shape did not, because a
      distance depends only on the differences of coordinates, and moving
      the origin changes both coordinates of a difference by the same amount.
    - (iii) The origin must be inside the field, not on its edge. One
      instance: the point that was $(5, 3)$ in the first description. The
      corners become $(-3, -2)$, $(3, -2)$, $(3, 2)$
      and $(-3, 2)$.
