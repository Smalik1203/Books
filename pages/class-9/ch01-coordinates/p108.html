B8 · The Trap Room
  1. Kavya reports the distance between $(2, 5)$ and $(7, 9)$ as $(7 - 2) + (9 - 5) = 9$, "because you move 5 across and 4 up". Her arithmetic is right. Say what she has actually measured, and when that number is the one you want.
  2. "The point $(-3, -3)$ is in the fourth quadrant, because both its coordinates are negative." Correct the statement, and say which single fact about the numbering was misremembered.
  3. "$(0, 5)$ is in the first quadrant, since neither coordinate is negative." Decide, then give the rule that settles every such case in one line, and say how many points of the plane lie in no quadrant at all.
  4. Asked for the midpoint of $(2, 6)$ and $(8, 10)$, a student halves the differences and reports $(3, 2)$. Show what that point actually is, and name the operation that should have been used instead.
  5. A student argues: since $\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2} = x_2 - x_1$, the distance from $(5, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$ is $1 - 5 = -4$. Both steps look like algebra you have seen. Find the false one.
  6. "$P(3, 4)$ and $Q(4, 3)$ are the same point — they are built from the same two numbers." Refute it with a calculation rather than an assertion, and then describe every pair of numbers for which the claim would in fact be true.
  7. "$A(1, 2)$, $B(3, 6)$ and $C(4, 8)$ are not in a straight line, because $AB \neq BC$." Test the claim, and say what has been confused with what.
Three shapes of mistake
  1. Measuring the wrong journey (traps 1, 5). Adding the shifts, or keeping their sign, both answer a question that was not asked. A distance is one number and it is never negative.
  2. Trusting a half-remembered convention (traps 2, 3). The quadrant order and the meaning of a zero coordinate are agreements, not deductions: check them, do not reconstruct them.
  3. Confusing a pair with a set (traps 4, 6, 7). $(x, y)$ is an ordered pair, a midpoint is an average and not a difference, and three points in a row need not be evenly spaced.
