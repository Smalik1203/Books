Do the points $P (-2, -1)$, $Q (1, 1)$ and $R (7, 5)$ lie on one straight line?

If $Q$ lies on the segment $PR$, the two parts add up to the whole: $PQ + QR = PR$. If $Q$ is off the line, the three points make a triangle, and two sides of a triangle always add to more than the third. So three distances decide it.

$PQ = \sqrt{3^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{13}$

$QR = \sqrt{6^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13}$

$PR = \sqrt{9^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{117} = 3\sqrt{13}$

So $PQ + QR = 3\sqrt{13} = PR$, and the three points are in line, with $Q$ one third of the way from $P$ to $R$. Notice that a question about a straight line was answered by three distances, and the line was never drawn. This is the method that Question 6 of the chapter's exercises asked you to find. Notice also the shifts. From $P$ to $Q$ they are $3$ and $2$. From $Q$ to $R$ they are $6$ and $4$, twice as much in each direction. That is what being in line looks like in the coordinates.

A point is $5$ units from the origin and also $5$ units from $(6, 0)$. Find it.

Call the point $(x, y)$. Both distances are given, so both can be squared:

$x^2 + y^2 = 25$

$(x - 6)^2 + y^2 = 25$

Two equations, two unknowns, and both contain $x^2$ and $y^2$. Subtract the second from the first. The $y^2$ goes at once, and $x^2 - (x - 6)^2 = 12x - 36$, so $12x - 36 = 0$ and $x = 3$. Then $y^2 = 25 - 9 = 16$, so $y = 4$ or $y = -4$. There are two such points, $(3, 4)$ and $(3, -4)$. The first is the point $P$ of the first question, and that is no accident: $P$ is $5$ from both $O$ and $(6, 0)$, so both of them are $5$ from $P$. Notice that the squares cancelled. When two distances from a point are given, subtracting the squared equations removes $x^2$ and $y^2$ together, and what is left is an equation of the kind you solved in Grade 8.
