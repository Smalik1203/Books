Coordinates were invented to let one subject speak to another, so a question about them is rarely only about them. Each of these reaches into a chapter you have met, or out of the book altogether.

B3 · Connect the Concepts
  1. Chapter 3 divided the numbers into those that are ratios and those that are not. The distances in Fig. 1.6 were $5$, $\sqrt{29}$ and $\sqrt{40}$ — one rational, two not.
    1. State the exact condition on the two shifts under which the distance between two grid points is a whole number.
    2. Find three more pairs of shifts that satisfy it, none of them a multiple of $(3, 4)$.
  2. All points with whole-number coordinates at distance $13$ from the origin satisfy $x^2 + y^2 = 169$.
    1. Find every one of them.
    2. Say how the symmetry of the plane hands you twelve of them once you have checked three.
  3. Chapter 2 called a sequence with a constant difference a linear pattern. Plot $(1, 5)$, $(2, 8)$, $(3, 11)$ and $(4, 14)$.
    1. Use distances alone to show that all four lie on one straight line.
    2. Explain what the constant difference of the pattern is doing on the picture.
  4. A circle (Chapter 5) is the set of points at one fixed distance from a centre. A circle is drawn with centre $O$ through $(9, 12)$. Decide, with reasons, whether $(-15, 0)$, $(6, 13)$ and $(12, 10)$ lie inside it, on it, or outside it.
  5. A drawing program keeps each icon as a centre and a radius. Two, of radii $80$ and $100$ pixels, sit at $(100, 150)$ and $(250, 230)$.
    1. Give the one test the program should apply to decide whether they overlap, and apply it.
    2. Say why the test needs no picture — and what it would have to become if the icons were squares.
  6. On a globe, two places one degree of longitude apart lie about $111$ km apart at the equator and about $28$ km apart at $75^\circ$ north. Say what that shows about using coordinate differences as distances.
