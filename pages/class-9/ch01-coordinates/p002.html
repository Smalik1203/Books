Āryabhaṭa (c. 499 CE) replaced the Greek ‘chords’ with ‘sines’, which made the coordinates of a star or a city far easier to compute. He mapped the sky with celestial coordinates, measuring from the ecliptic — the path the Sun appears to take.

Then came the piece without which none of this chapter would work. Brahmagupta (c. 628 CE) formalised zero and the negative numbers as algebraic objects in their own right. In a modern coordinate system the origin is zero, and the negative axes are exactly the values below it. Without Brahmagupta there is no four-quadrant plane — only a quarter of one.

His work travelled. Translated into Arabic as the Sindhind, it carried the Ujjayinī meridian with it, which entered Arabic geography as ‘Arin’ and served as the zero of longitude for early Arabic maps — negative numbers and all. Al-Bīrūnī (c. 1000 CE) came to India, studied the Siddhāntas, and used Indian trigonometric methods to fix the coordinates of cities across Asia; he went on to perfect the astrolabe, a handheld instrument with which a sailor could read his own position off the stars. Ömar Khayyām (c. 1100 CE), expert in the Indian decimal system and its algebra, was the first to solve algebraic problems geometrically, by reading them as coordinates in a plane.

These ideas reached Europe in the 12th century. The last step was taken by René Descartes (1637 CE), following related work of Fermat (1636 CE): any point of a flat surface, he saw, is fixed by just two numbers — its distances from two perpendicular lines. Shapes could now be written as equations, and equations drawn as shapes. Geometry and algebra had been introduced to each other properly.

Two numbers, two directions, one agreed starting point. Everything in this chapter is built from those three things.

Across Grades 9 and 10 you will work with this system: locating things with pinpoint accuracy, and seeing an algebraic equation turn into a geometric shape — and back again. We begin with a story, because the new words are easier to keep hold of when they belong to somebody.
