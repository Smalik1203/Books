His work travelled. Translated into Arabic as the Sindhind, it carried the Ujjayinī meridian with it, which entered Arabic geography as ‘Arin’ and served as the zero of longitude for early Arabic maps — negative numbers and all. Al-Bīrūnī (c. 1000 CE) came to India, studied the Siddhāntas, and used Indian trigonometric methods to fix the coordinates of cities across Asia. He went on to perfect the astrolabe, a handheld instrument with which a sailor could read his own position off the stars. Ömar Khayyām (c. 1100 CE), expert in the Indian decimal system and its algebra, was the first to solve algebraic problems geometrically, by reading them as coordinates in a plane.

These ideas reached Europe in the 12th century. The last step was taken by René Descartes (1637 CE), following related work of Fermat (1636 CE): any point of a flat surface, he saw, is fixed by just two numbers — its distances from two perpendicular lines. Shapes could now be written as equations, and equations drawn as shapes. Geometry and algebra had been introduced to each other properly.

The adjective we use is his name in disguise. Descartes wrote and published in Latin as Renatus Cartesius, and it is Cartesius that survives in Cartesian — so every time the plane is named, it is named after him twice over.

What he left, though, was not quite the diagram in this chapter. He drew one axis and measured from it; he did not insist that the second be perpendicular, and he had no use for negative coordinates at all. The tidy cross of two axes, the four quadrants and the signs that go with them were assembled by others over the century that followed. They used, at last, the negative numbers Brahmagupta had set out a thousand years before.

Two numbers, two directions, one agreed starting point. Everything in this chapter is built from those three things.

Across Classes 9 and 10 you will work with this system: locating things with pinpoint accuracy, and seeing an algebraic equation turn into a geometric shape — and back again. We begin with a story, because the new words are easier to keep hold of when they belong to somebody.

1.2Settling In

It is the first week of the academic year, and Reiaan is excited and nervous in roughly equal parts. The family has just moved to a new city, and he and his sister Shalini will both be starting at a new school. Today Shalini is helping him settle in. Reiaan cannot see, which makes a new set of rooms a genuine challenge — but with their mother’s transferable job the siblings have done this often, and each move has been a little easier than the last.

Shalini has just finished Class 9, and this time she decided to put her coordinate geometry to work.

She wanted Reiaan to feel the directions, so she built the room rather than describing it. On a rectangular grid she fixed pins and threads, at a scale of 1 cm to 1 foot. Each pin marked a key point of the floor. The corners of the furniture were joined with thick wool, so that Reiaan’s fingers could follow an edge from one corner to the next.
