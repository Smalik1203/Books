It makes no difference whether $x_2 - x_1$ and $y_2 - y_1$ come out positive or negative. We are recording shifts along the two axes, and the squaring throws the sign away before it can matter. This is worth holding on to: you never need to decide which point to call ‘first’.

What happens with negative coordinates

The drawing in Fig. 1.7 sat in the first quadrant, where every coordinate is positive. Does the formula still hold when the numbers are negative? Fig. 1.9 puts it to the test by reflecting triangle $ADM$ in the y-axis. What are the coordinates of the images of $A$, $D$ and $M$?

The images are $A' (-3, 4)$, $D' (-7, 1)$ and $M' (-9, 6)$. Work the legs of the right-angled triangle exactly as before, and let the negatives fall where they may:

$C'D' = $ x-coordinate of $A' - $ x-coordinate of $D' = -3 - (-7) = 4$

$A'C' = $ y-coordinate of $A' - $ y-coordinate of $D' = 4 - 1 = 3$

The same 4 and the same 3, so the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem returns the same answer: $A'D' = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$ units. The other two sides behave the same way, with the negative shifts squared away:

$D'M' = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{29}$ units

$M'A' = \sqrt{(-6)^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{40}$ units

Reflection has moved the triangle without changing the length of a single side.
