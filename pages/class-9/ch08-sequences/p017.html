The tile pattern of Fig. 8.3 reaches $81$ tiles somewhere around stage $21$. Where does the doubling pattern of Fig. 8.6 reach $81$?

Now ask the same question for a thousand tiles, and say what that comparison tells you.

The answer to that comparison is worth stating plainly, because it is the most useful thing in this section. The tile pattern needs about twenty stages to reach eighty-one tiles and about two hundred and fifty to reach a thousand. The doubling pattern reaches eighty-one at its sixth stage and a thousand at its tenth. Any GP with a ratio above $1$ will overtake any AP eventually, whatever head start the AP is given, because the AP adds a fixed amount while the GP adds a share of a total that is itself growing.

8.6.2 A shape that is a progression

Start with a paper triangle. Join the midpoints of its three sides, which cuts it into four smaller triangles, and remove the middle one. Now do the same to each of the three that remain. Then again, and again.

The number of shaded triangles goes $1, 3, 9, 27, \ldots$ — a GP with $a = 1$ and $r = 3$, because each one is replaced by three. At stage $n$ there are $3^n$ of them, and by stage $10$ that is $59\,049$.

Now ask about the area instead. Take the whole triangle at stage $0$ to be one square unit. Cutting it into four and throwing one away leaves $\tfrac34$ of it. Do the same again and $\tfrac34$ of that is left, and so on:

$1, \quad \tfrac34, \quad \tfrac{9}{16}, \quad \tfrac{27}{64}, \ \ldots$

another GP, this one with $r = \tfrac34$, and its $n$th stage has area $\left(\tfrac34\right)^n$.

So the same figure carries two progressions at once, running opposite ways. The number of triangles multiplies by $3$ and races away; the area they cover multiplies by $\tfrac34$ and dwindles towards nothing. More and more pieces, less and less paper.

A shape like this, which contains smaller copies of itself at every scale, is called a fractal. Zoom in on any corner of it and you find the whole thing again.
