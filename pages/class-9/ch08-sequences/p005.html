Example 1

A sequence is given by $u_n = 2n - 1$. Write its first three terms.

Substitute $n = 1$, then $2$, then $3$:

$u_1$ $= 2 \times 1 - 1 = 1$
$u_2$ $= 2 \times 2 - 1 = 3$
$u_3$ $= 2 \times 3 - 1 = 5$

So the sequence begins $1, 3, 5$ — the odd numbers, as expected.

8.2.1 Reading the rule backwards

An explicit rule is an equation, and an equation can be read in either direction. Forwards it turns a position into a term. Backwards it answers a different and more interesting question: is this number in the sequence at all, and if so, where?

Take $137$. Is it an odd number — and if it is, which one? Solve $t_n = 137$:

set $2n - 1 = 137$
so $2n = 138$
and $n = 69$

So $137$ is the $69$th odd number. The answer came out a whole number, and that is what settled it.

That last remark carries the whole method. Solving $t_n = 137$ would have given an answer whatever number was put on the right, and the arithmetic would have looked no different. What decided the question was the kind of answer that came out. A position is a counting number, so a whole number means the number is in the sequence and a fraction means it is not — and the equation cannot tell you which until you look.

The same reading answers a question that sounds harder than it is: how many terms of a sequence fall below some limit. Ask where the odd numbers pass $200$ and you are solving $2n - 1 = 200$, which gives $n = 100.5$. No term sits at that position, so the hundredth term is the last one under $200$, and there are exactly a hundred of them. The fraction was not a failure of the method; it was the answer.
