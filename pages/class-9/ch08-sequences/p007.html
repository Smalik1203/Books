Both describe the same sequence, and each is better at something. To find the $500$th term, the explicit rule answers at once and the recursive one asks you to take $499$ steps. But a recursive rule is often the easier of the two to discover, because it only asks what one step does — and for some sequences it is the only rule anybody has.

Example 3

Find the first four terms of the sequence given by $u_1 = 1$, $u_n = 2u_{n-1} + 3$ for $n \geq 2$.

Each term needs the one before it, so they come out in order.

Solution.

Step 1 $u_2 = 2 \times 1 + 3 = 5$ from $u_1$
Step 2 $u_3 = 2 \times 5 + 3 = 13$ from $u_2$
Step 3 $u_4 = 2 \times 13 + 3 = 29$ from $u_3$
Answer The sequence begins $1, 5, 13, 29$
Example 4

Find the first four terms of the sequence given by $s_1 = 3$, $s_n = s_{n-1}(s_{n-1} - 1)$ for $n \geq 2$.

Solution.

Step 1 $s_2 = 3 \times (3 - 1) = 3 \times 2 = 6$ from $s_1$
Step 2 $s_3 = 6 \times (6 - 1) = 6 \times 5 = 30$ from $s_2$
Step 3 $s_4 = 30 \times (30 - 1) = 30 \times 29 = 870$ from $s_3$
Answer The sequence is $3, 6, 30, 870, \ldots$

The fifth term is already $756\,030$.

That last remark is not an aside. A recursive rule can hide how fast a sequence moves: nothing in $s_n = s_{n-1}(s_{n-1} - 1)$ looks violent, and four steps take you from $3$ to nearly nine hundred. Walking the sequence is the only way to find out.

8.3.1 A rule that looks further back

A recursive rule need not stop at the previous term. It may reach back two, or more. The most famous sequence in mathematics is built exactly that way:

start $s_1 = 1$,   $s_2 = 2$
then $s_n = s_{n-1} + s_{n-2}$   for $n \geq 3$

Each term is the sum of the two before it. Two starting values are needed now rather than one, because the rule reaches back two places and has to have something to reach for.
