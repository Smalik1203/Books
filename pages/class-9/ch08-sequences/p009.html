Each term is the sum of the two before it. Two starting values are needed now rather than one, because the rule reaches back two places and has to have something to reach for.

$s_3$ $= 2 + 1 = 3$
$s_4$ $= 3 + 2 = 5$
$s_5$ $= 5 + 3 = 8$

giving $1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, \ldots$

This is the Virahānka–Fibonacci sequence, and it is the oldest sequence in this chapter by some centuries.

It was written down and studied by Virahānka in the seventh century, in his Vṛttajātisamuchaya — and not as a curiosity about numbers. He was working on Prakrit metre. A line of verse is built from syllables that are short or long, a long one lasting exactly as long as two short ones, and he asked how many different rhythms fill a line of a given length. The answer is this sequence, and the reason is the rule itself: a rhythm of length $n$ ends either in a short syllable, leaving a rhythm of length $n-1$ before it, or in a long one, leaving $n-2$.

The linguist-mathematicians Gopāla (c. 1135) and Hemachandra (c. 1150) took it further. It reached Europe with Fibonacci around 1200, whose name it has carried since — though he was some five hundred years behind.

This sequence has no simple explicit rule of the kind $t_n = 3n - 2$. It is a case where the recursive rule is not the convenient description but the only one within reach.
