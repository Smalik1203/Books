Example 8

Is $5,\ \tfrac{15}{4},\ \tfrac{45}{16},\ \tfrac{135}{64}, \ldots$ a geometric progression? If so, find its $n$th term.

Solution.

Step 1 $\dfrac{t_2}{t_1} = \dfrac{15}{4} \div 5 = \dfrac{15}{20} = \dfrac34$ ratio to the term before
Step 2 $\dfrac{t_3}{t_2} = \dfrac{45}{16} \div \dfrac{15}{4} = \dfrac{45}{16} \times \dfrac{4}{15} = \dfrac34$
Step 3 $\dfrac{t_4}{t_3} = \dfrac{135}{64} \times \dfrac{16}{45} = \dfrac34$
Step 4 the ratio is $\tfrac34$ throughout, so this is a GP with $a = 5$ and $r = \tfrac34$ the same ratio each time
Answer Yes. Its $n$th term is $t_n = 5 \left(\frac34\right)^{n-1}$

Here the ratio is less than $1$, so the terms shrink. They never reach zero — multiplying by $\tfrac34$ can make a positive number small but not nothing — and that is a GP as much as a doubling one is.

Notice how the test was carried out. Three ratios were taken, not one. A single ratio proves nothing at all — any two numbers have a ratio — and it is only when the same one keeps coming back that the sequence has been shown to be geometric. The same warning applies to an AP and its differences.

8.6.1 What a GP looks like

Do for the doubling pattern what was done for the tiles: set the stage number against the count and plot the pairs $(1, 3)$, $(2, 6)$, $(3, 12)$, $(4, 24)$, $(5, 48)$.

They do not lie on a line. They start off close to one and then peel away upwards, and the gap widens at every stage.

That is the whole difference between the two kinds, drawn. An AP adds the same amount to a total that is getting larger, so the amount matters less and less in proportion, and the graph is straight. A GP adds a proportion of the total, so what it adds grows as the total grows — and the more there is, the faster it goes.
