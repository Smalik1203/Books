Every counting question of this shape comes apart the same way: find the first, find the last, count the steps between them, add one. It does not matter whether the question is about multiples of $9$, two-digit numbers divisible by $3$, or the terms of an AP between two limits. Those are the same question in different clothes, and the last of the four operations is the one that gets left out.

A sequence is given by $t_1 = 1$ and $t_n = t_{n-1} + 2n - 1$ for $n \geq 2$. Find its first five terms, and then say what sequence it is.

The rule is $t_1 = 1$, $t_n = t_{n-1} + 2n - 1$. Walk it.

$t_2$ $= 1 + 3 = 4$
$t_3$ $= 4 + 5 = 9$
$t_4$ $= 9 + 7 = 16$
$t_5$ $= 16 + 9 = 25$

The square numbers — and the recursion says why, if you read what is being added. The amount added at step $n$ is $2n - 1$, which is the $n$th odd number. So this rule is the picture from the first section of the chapter written as algebra: each square is the one before it plus an L of odd many dots.

It is worth noticing that the explicit rule, $t_n = n^2$, is far the simpler of the two here — and that the recursive one is what you would have found first from the picture. Neither is the better rule; they are answers to different questions.

Two sequences begin $3, 6, \ldots$ and nothing more is said about either. Give a third term for the first that makes it an AP, and a third term for the second that makes it a GP. What does this show about predicting from two terms?

For $3, 6, \ldots$ to be an AP the common difference is $3$, so the third term is $9$. For it to be a GP the common ratio is $2$, so the third term is $12$. Both continuations are perfectly legitimate.

And they are not the only two. The third term could be $7$, or $-40$, or anything at all: some rule can be written to produce it. Two terms fix a difference and a ratio, and that is all they fix.

Which is why a question that shows you a few terms and asks what comes next is strictly speaking unanswerable, and why it is asked anyway. What is really wanted is the simplest rule that fits — and simplest is a judgement, not a calculation. That judgement is usually right, and it is worth knowing that it is a judgement.

Three terms are no better in principle, but they are far better in practice, because now the two tests can disagree. Given $3, 6, 9$ the differences are equal and the ratios are not; given $3, 6, 12$ it is the other way round. With only two terms both tests pass, which is precisely why two terms settle nothing.
