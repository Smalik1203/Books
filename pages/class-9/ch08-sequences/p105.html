The same trick is worth remembering for three terms of an AP: write them $a - d$, $a$, $a + d$ and their sum is $3a$, with the $d$ gone. Whenever a question gives you a symmetric fact about an odd number of terms — their sum, their product — centring the notation on the middle one is what makes it fall out.

A word about the third and the sixth. Both look like questions about particular numbers, and both are really questions about what a rule is entitled to claim. A GP with a positive first term and a positive ratio can shrink for ever without changing sign. Two terms of a list settle nothing at all about the third. Those two habits — asking what a rule forbids, and asking how much of a list you actually need — catch most of the mistakes made in this subject.

None of the eight yields to a remembered formula on its own. Each turns on a decision made before the formula: which kind of sequence this is, what is fixed and what is being asked for, and whether the question has one answer or several. Settle that and the arithmetic is a line or two.

Two of them are worth attempting more than once. The seventh asks for all the ways, which is a different demand from finding one — you have to know when to stop, and knowing when to stop means understanding why the list is finite at all. The eighth gives you a sum and a product rather than terms, and the whole difficulty is choosing how to write the three unknowns down before any algebra begins.

If a question resists, the useful move is to write out five or six terms by hand. A pattern that is invisible in the rule is often obvious in the list, and the list costs a minute. Every solution above begins that way, even the ones that end in algebra.

And one habit is worth more than any of these. Before answering, say out loud what kind of sequence you are looking at — arithmetic, geometric, or neither — and how you know. Half the wrong answers in this chapter are right answers to the other kind of question.
