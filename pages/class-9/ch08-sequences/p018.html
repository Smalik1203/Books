The figure is named for Wacław Sierpiński (1882–1969), the Polish mathematician who described it in 1915. Fractals are not only a curiosity of paper and scissors. The branching of a tree, the florets of a cauliflower, the arms of a snowflake and the ragged edge of a coastline all repeat themselves at different scales in much the same way.

The two sequences in the figure are worth keeping together in mind, because between them they show what a common ratio does. A ratio greater than $1$ runs away: three, nine, twenty-seven, and by stage twenty there are more than three thousand million triangles. A ratio less than $1$ closes down: the paper left is three quarters, then a little over a half, and it goes on shrinking without ever quite reaching nothing. Same rule, same figure, opposite directions.

Example 9

A ball is dropped from a height of $24$ feet. Each bounce carries it back to $\tfrac34$ of the height it fell from. Write the heights of the first five bounces, and find how many bounces it takes before the ball stays below $\tfrac16$ of the height it started at.

Solution.

Step 1 each height is $0.75$ times the one before, so the heights form a GP
Step 2 $24 \times 0.75$, which is 18 1st bounce
Step 3 $18 \times 0.75$, which is 13.5 2nd bounce
Step 4 $13.5 \times 0.75$, which is 10.125 3rd bounce
Step 5 $10.125 \times 0.75$, which is 7.594 4th bounce
Step 6 $7.594 \times 0.75$, which is 5.695 5th bounce
Step 7 a sixth of $24$ feet is $4$ feet; the fifth bounce is still above it, and so is the sixth at $4.271$ feet — but the seventh reaches only $3.203$ feet
Answer The first five bounces reach 18, 13.5, 10.125, 7.594 and 5.695 feet. The ball first stays below a sixth of its starting height at the seventh bounce
