8.6.1 What a GP looks like

Do for the doubling pattern what was done for the tiles: set the stage number against the count and plot the pairs $(1, 3)$, $(2, 6)$, $(3, 12)$, $(4, 24)$, $(5, 48)$.

They do not lie on a line. They start off close to one and then peel away upwards, and the gap widens at every stage.

That is the whole difference between the two kinds, drawn. An AP adds the same amount to a total that is getting larger, so the amount matters less and less in proportion, and the graph is straight. A GP adds a proportion of the total, so what it adds grows as the total grows — and the more there is, the faster it goes.

Think and Reflect

The tile pattern of Fig. 8.3 reaches $81$ tiles somewhere around stage $21$. Where does the doubling pattern of Fig. 8.6 reach $81$?

Now ask the same question for a thousand tiles, and say what that comparison tells you.

The answer to that comparison is worth stating plainly, because it is the most useful thing in this section. The tile pattern needs about twenty stages to reach eighty-one tiles and about two hundred and fifty to reach a thousand. The doubling pattern reaches eighty-one before its sixth stage and a thousand before its ninth. Any GP with a ratio above $1$ will overtake any AP eventually, whatever head start the AP is given, because the AP adds a fixed amount while the GP adds a share of a total that is itself growing.
