What this chapter established
  1. A sequence is an ordered list of numbers; each number in it is a term, and $t_n$ names the term in position $n$.
  2. An explicit rule gives $t_n$ from $n$ alone, so any term can be reached directly. Read backwards, it also says whether a given number is in the sequence, and where.
  3. A recursive rule gives $t_n$ from the terms before it, together with a starting value. It is often easier to find, and for some sequences it is the only rule there is.
  4. Not every sequence has an explicit rule. The primes are perfectly well defined and have none known.
  5. An arithmetic progression adds a fixed common difference $d$ at every step: $t_n = a + (n-1)d$. Plotted against position, its terms lie on a straight line.
  6. A geometric progression multiplies by a fixed common ratio $r$ at every step: $t_n = ar^{\,n-1}$. Its terms do not lie on a line; they curve away.
  7. $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + n = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$, which is also the $n$th triangular number.
  8. The $n - 1$ in both progression formulas is the same $n-1$: the first term has taken no steps at all.
  9. To tell which kind a sequence is, take differences and take ratios. One of them is constant, or neither is — and one example of either proves nothing.
  10. A GP with $r > 1$ overtakes any AP eventually, whatever head start the AP is given: an AP adds a fixed amount, a GP adds a share of a total that is itself growing.
  11. The same figure can carry two progressions running opposite ways. In the Sierpiński triangle the count multiplies by $3$ and the area by $\tfrac34$ — more and more pieces covering less and less paper.
