An Arithmetic Progression

An AP with first term $a$ and common difference $d$ runs

$a, \ a + d, \ a + 2d, \ a + 3d, \ \ldots$

and its $n$th term is $t_n = a + (n - 1)d$.

Look at where the $(n-1)$ comes from. The first term has had no $d$ added to it at all, the second has had one, the third two. By the $n$th you have taken $n - 1$ steps, not $n$. That single offset is where most mistakes with APs are made.

The common difference may be negative. In $11, 7, 3, -1, -5, \ldots$ the terms fall by $4$ each time, so $a = 11$ and $d = -4$, and the sequence is an AP as much as any rising one is.

Think and Reflect

Go back through every sequence in this chapter so far. Which are arithmetic progressions and which are not?

For each one that is not, say what goes wrong — and be precise: it is not enough to say the numbers grow differently.

The recursive rule for an AP is even shorter than the explicit one, and it is the rule the definition itself gives you:

start $t_1 = a$
then $t_n = t_{n-1} + d$   for $n \geq 2$

Written this way an AP says almost nothing: start here, and keep adding the same amount. That is the whole of it. Everything else in this section — the formula for the $n$th term, the straight line it draws, the way its terms can be summed in one step — follows from those two lines and adds no new assumption.

It is worth noticing how little is being asked of the numbers. They need not be whole, or positive, or increasing. A common difference of $-4$ gives a falling AP, one of $0.5$ gives a sequence of halves, and one of $0$ gives the same number over and over — which is a perfectly good, if dull, arithmetic progression.
