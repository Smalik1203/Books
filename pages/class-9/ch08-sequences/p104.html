What a ratio of a half can and cannot do

The classmate is wrong, and no arithmetic is needed to say so.

Every term of this GP is $\tfrac12$ times the one before it. Half of a positive number is positive. So if the first term is positive every term after it is positive too, however far the sequence runs — the terms fall towards zero and never arrive, and they certainly never cross it.

What the classmate has done is confuse shrinking with decreasing past zero. Only a negative ratio changes signs, and it changes them at every single step: $r = -\tfrac12$ would give $5, -\tfrac52, \tfrac54, -\tfrac58, \ldots$, alternating rather than descending.

Counting three-digit multiples of seven

The multiples of $7$ form an AP with $d = 7$, so the whole question is: where does it start, where does it stop, and how many steps between?

first $7 \times 15 = 105$
last $7 \times 142 = 994$
count $142 - 15 + 1$ 128

The $+1$ is the whole difficulty. From the fifteenth multiple to the hundred-and-forty-second there are $127$ steps but $128$ multiples, because both ends are being counted. It is the same off-by-one as the $n-1$ in $t_n = a + (n-1)d$, met from the other side.

The same could be had from the formula: solve $105 + (n-1)7 = 994$ to get $n = 128$. The arithmetic is identical; the fence-post is just easier to see when the multiples are numbered.

Finding the two ends is the part worth practising. The smallest three-digit multiple of $7$ is not $100$ rounded somehow — it is the first multiple at or above $100$, and $100 \div 7$ is a little over $14$, so it is the fifteenth. The largest is found the same way from the top: $999 \div 7$ is a little over $142$, so the hundred-and-forty-second, $994$, is the last one that fits.

Every counting question of this shape comes apart the same way: find the first, find the last, count the steps between them, add one. It does not matter whether the question is about multiples of $7$, two-digit numbers divisible by $3$, or the terms of an AP between two limits — those are the same question in different clothes, and the last of the four operations is the one that gets left out.
