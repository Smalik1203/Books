One hundred, as a run of consecutives

Suppose the run has $k$ terms and starts at $a$. It is an AP with $d = 1$, so by the rule from the chapter — first plus last, halved, times the count —

sum $= \dfrac{k\,\bigl(2a + k - 1\bigr)}{2} = 100$
so $k\,(2a + k - 1) = 200$

Now look at the two brackets. If $k$ is even then $2a + k - 1$ is odd, and if $k$ is odd then $2a + k - 1$ is even — one of the two factors is always odd. But $200 = 2^3 \times 5^2$, whose only odd factors are $1$, $5$ and $25$.

$k = 5$ $2a + 4 = 40$, so $a = 18$ 18 + 19 + 20 + 21 + 22
$k = 8$ $2a + 7 = 25$, so $a = 9$ 9 + 10 + … + 16

The other cases give no whole $a$, or the single term $100$ itself. So there are exactly two ways, and the reason there are so few is that $200$ is nearly all twos.

The GP with product $-1$

Write the three terms as $\dfrac{a}{r}$, $a$, $ar$ rather than $a$, $ar$, $ar^2$. It is the same GP, centred on its middle term, and the product collapses at once:

product $\dfrac{a}{r} \times a \times ar = a^3 = -1$, so $a = -1$
sum $-\dfrac1r - 1 - r = \dfrac{13}{12}$

That gives $12r^2 + 25r + 12 = 0$, whose roots are $r = -\tfrac34$ and $r = -\tfrac43$. Either gives the same three numbers in opposite orders: $\tfrac43,\ -1,\ \tfrac34$.

The choice of notation did the work. Written $a, ar, ar^2$ the product is $a^3r^3$ and both unknowns remain; written about the middle, the $r$ cancels and one of them falls out immediately.

The same trick is worth remembering for three terms of an AP: write them $a - d$, $a$, $a + d$ and their sum is $3a$, with the $d$ gone. Whenever a question gives you a symmetric fact about an odd number of terms — their sum, their product — centring the notation on the middle one is what makes it fall out.
