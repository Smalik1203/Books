Two lists, then, that looked like separate curiosities, and each turns out to be the running total of a list you already had. That is the first hint of what this chapter is about: sequences are not a collection of tricks to be memorised one at a time, but a small number of ideas that keep reappearing.

8.1.2 Naming the terms

To say anything general about a sequence we need to be able to point at a particular term without writing the whole list out. The usual notation is a letter with a subscript: $t_1$ for the first term, $t_2$ for the second, and $t_n$ for the term in position $n$. The subscript is the position, and nothing else.

So for the odd numbers,

$t_1 = 1, \quad t_2 = 3, \quad t_3 = 5, \quad t_4 = 7, \quad \ldots$

and $t_4 = 7$ says exactly one thing: the term in the fourth position is $7$. When two sequences are in play at once they take different letters — $t_1, t_2, t_3, \ldots$ for one, $s_1, s_2, s_3, \ldots$ for another, $u_1, u_2, u_3, \ldots$ for a third.

The subscript $n$ counts positions, so it is a natural number. The term $t_n$ is under no such rule: it may be a fraction, a negative number, or any real number at all.

The terms need not grow. Every sequence so far has increased, but

$1,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac14,\ \ldots$

$7,\ 3,\ -1,\ -5,\ -9,\ \ldots$

are sequences too. The first shrinks towards nothing without ever reaching it; the second falls by $4$ each time and goes on past zero into the negatives. Both have a rule, and the rule is what makes them sequences rather than lists.
