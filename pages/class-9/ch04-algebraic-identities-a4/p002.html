4.2Visualising Identities

You already know several identities from earlier grades. This section meets them from a different direction: instead of pushing symbols around, we read the identity off a picture. Area does the work — $a^2$ is a square of side $a$, and $ab$ a rectangle.

Start with two segments, of lengths $a$ and $b$ units. Laid end to end they make one segment of length $(a + b)$ units, as in Fig. 4.1.

Now build a square on that segment — side $(a + b)$ units — and cut it with one vertical and one horizontal line, each at distance $a$ from the top left corner. Four pieces appear, as in Fig. 4.2.

  • Top left: a square of side $a$, area $a^2$.
  • Bottom right: a square of side $b$, area $b^2$.
  • Two rectangles, each $a$ by $b$, so each has area $ab$.
The whole square has area $(a + b)^2$.

The four pieces make up the whole square, so their areas must add to its area:

$$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$

But look at what that argument used: a picture of lengths. Every quantity in it had to be positive — you cannot draw a square whose side is $-2$ units. So the drawing proves the identity for positive $a$ and $b$, and nothing more. Does it survive other kinds of number?
