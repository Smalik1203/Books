4.3Factorisation Using Identities

Read the identity from right to left and it does a different job: given an expression shaped like $a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, it tells you the expression is a perfect square and hands you the factors. The work is all in the recognising — two perfect squares, and a middle term that is exactly twice the product of their roots.

Example 5

Factorise $x^2 + 4x + 4$.

The first and last terms are squares: $x^2 = (x)^2$ and $4 = 2^2$. Is the middle term twice their product? Twice $x \times 2$ is $4x$, which is exactly what is there. So with $a = x$ and $b = 2$,

$x^2 + 4x + 4 = x^2 + 2(x)(2) + 2^2 = (x + 2)^2$

Example 6

Factorise $36x^2 + 12x + 1$.

Again, look for the two squares: $36x^2 = (6x)^2$ and $1 = 1^2$. Twice $6x \times 1$ is $12x$, the middle term. So $a = 6x$, $b = 1$, and

$36x^2 + 12x + 1 = (6x)^2 + 2(6x)(1) + 1^2 = (6x + 1)^2$

In both cases the expression is a perfect square, so the factor appears twice: $x^2 + 4x + 4 = (x + 2)(x + 2)$ — easy to miss.

Example 7

Factorise $50p^2 + 60pq + 18q^2$.

What are $a$ and $b$ here? You would need a term whose square is $50p^2$, and that is $\sqrt{50}\,p$ — a surd, and awkward to carry through the working.

So look again before reaching for a square root. All three terms share a factor of 2:

$50p^2 + 60pq + 18q^2 = 2\left(25p^2 + 30pq + 9q^2\right)$

The bracket is friendly: $25p^2 = (5p)^2$, $9q^2 = (3q)^2$, and twice $5p \times 3q$ is $30pq$ — so $a = 5p$, $b = 3q$:

$50p^2 + 60pq + 18q^2 = 2\left[(5p)^2 + 2(5p)(3q) + (3q)^2\right]$

$= 2(5p + 3q)^2$
