1.7Rough Answers, Honestly Got

A great deal of scientific thinking happens before any exact calculation, and often instead of one. Faced with a new question, the useful first move is to understand the situation, decide which quantities matter, and then get a rough number — not to five figures, but to the nearest power of ten.

An estimate of that kind will not win an argument, but it will tell you whether an answer is even possible. If a calculation gives a person a mass of 3000 kg, or a bus a speed of 4000 km/h, no further checking is needed: something has gone wrong, and you knew it in a second without finding the mistake.

This is a skill that improves with practice. It rests on carrying a few sizes in your head — a person is about 1.7 m and 60 kg, a room 3 m high, a bucket 15 litres — and on being willing to be wrong by a factor of two.

Imagine This
How much rice would feed a family for a month?

For an energy-equivalent estimate, assume four adults each need 2100 kilocalories daily and uncooked rice supplies 350 kilocalories per 100 g. That gives 600 g per adult daily, or about 72 kg for four adults over 30 days. These are stated model assumptions, not a diet recommendation.

A family of four is then about 2.4 kg a day, and a month is about 70 kg. Nobody should trust that to the nearest kilogram. What it is good for is knowing that a 5 kg bag will not do, a tonne is absurd, and a household sack of 50 to 75 kg is about right — which is roughly what such a family buys.

Every number in that working could be argued with, and none had to be right. What mattered was that each was the correct sort of number.
