Models improve by being wrong in a way somebody can point at. Put air resistance back into the cricket ball and the flight shortens; measure a real six and you find out whether that correction was worth making. A model that can never be caught out is not a careful model — it is one that has stopped saying anything.

Meghnad Saha
1893–1956

In 1920, Meghnad Saha developed an equation relating the ionisation of a hot gas to its temperature and electron pressure. Ionisation means that atoms lose or gain electrons. The equation helped explain the absorption lines seen in stellar spectra. It models gas under stated conditions; it does not treat every detail of a whole star.

1.3Saying Exactly What You Mean

Science borrows most of its words from ordinary speech and then quietly narrows them. In conversation, work is anything that tires you out and force is roughly the same as effort. In physics, holding a bag still does no mechanical work on the bag, though your muscles use energy, however much your arm disagrees. Neither meaning is the correct one; they are simply different words that happen to be spelled alike.

The narrowing is not fussiness. Thousands of people in dozens of languages have to be able to read the same result and take the same meaning from it, without meeting, and often centuries apart. That only works if every load-bearing word has one agreed sense.

You have met this before without noticing. In the kitchen a tomato is a vegetable; in botany it is a fruit. Neither the cook nor the botanist is mistaken. They are sorting the same object for different purposes — one by how it tastes and what it is served with, the other by where the seeds sit — and each sorting is exactly right for the questions its owner asks.

The same discipline applies to quantities. Anything that can be measured gets a symbol to stand for it and a unit to be counted in, and both are agreed internationally rather than chosen by whoever is writing.
