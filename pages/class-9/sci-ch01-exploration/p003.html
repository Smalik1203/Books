Example 1.1

A batter hits a ball high towards the boundary and everyone in the ground looks up. Will it clear the rope without bouncing? Build the simplest model that could answer that, and say what you have thrown away.

Three things have to be in it: how fast the ball left the bat, the direction it left in, and gravity pulling it down all the way. With those three you can work out the whole flight.

Three things can go straight out: the make of the bat, the colour of the ball, and how recently the outfield was mown. None of them changes where the ball lands, and no amount of care about them would improve the answer.

And three things sit in between — the air pushing back, the spin the ball carries, the stitching at the seam. These are real, and they do change the answer a little. We leave them out of a first model knowing that we are doing it, and put them back if the first answer turns out not to be good enough.

Activity 1.1 · Let us model

You ride from school to home. Build a model that predicts how long the ride will take.

Write two lists: what your model keeps, and what it leaves out. Then swap lists with somebody who takes a different route home and argue about the differences. Finally, say what your model would get badly wrong — every model has an answer to that.

Safety first — do the timing from memory or with somebody else holding the watch. Nobody works out a model while riding.

Models improve by being wrong in a way somebody can point at. Put air resistance back into the cricket ball and the flight shortens; measure a real six and you find out whether that correction was worth making. A model that can never be caught out is not a careful model — it is one that has stopped saying anything.
