The same discipline applies to quantities. Anything that can be measured gets a symbol to stand for it and a unit to be counted in, and both are agreed internationally rather than chosen by whoever is writing.

mass $m$ kilogram, kg
length $l$ metre, m
time $t$ second, s
velocity $v$ metre per second, m/s
force $F$ newton, N
electric current $I$ ampere, A

A number on its own is not a measurement. Write that a rod is 2 long and you have said nothing: two metres is a walking stick, two centimetres is a nail, two kilometres is a bridge. The unit is not an ornament on the end of the number — it is half of the statement, and dropping it is the commonest way to be wrong while looking right.

One unit has to stretch from the width of a cell to the distance to the Sun, and it does so through prefixes — each of them simply a power of ten hung on the front of the name.

milli (m) $10^{-3}$ 1 mm is a grain of sand
centi (c) $10^{-2}$ 1 cm is a fingernail
kilo (k) $10^{3}$ 1 km is a short walk
mega (M) $10^{6}$ 1 Mm is roughly Delhi to Mumbai
Threads of Curiosity
Why is the speed of light called $c$?

Not because it stands for anything in English. The symbol comes from the Latin celeritas, meaning swiftness, and it stuck through habit and agreement rather than logic — as a good many symbols have.

Its value is stranger still. The speed of light in vacuum is 299 792 458 metres per second exactly, and not because anyone measured it that precisely. Since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in a fixed fraction of a second. So the number cannot be measured any more, and cannot change: measure light more carefully today and what you improve is your knowledge of the metre.
