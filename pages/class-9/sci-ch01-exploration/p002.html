1.2Leaving Things Out

The world does not arrive in pieces the right size to think about. A falling mango is also a chemical process, a home for bacteria, and a parcel of matter being pulled by every star in the galaxy. Studying all of that at once is not thoroughness; it is paralysis.

So science works with models. A model is a deliberately simplified stand-in for a real system, keeping only what matters for the question being asked and setting the rest aside. When we work out how fast the mango hits the ground we ignore its bacteria, its colour, and every star in the galaxy, and we usually ignore the air as well.

The word deliberately is the important one. What is left out of a model has not been overlooked — it has been judged not to matter here, by somebody who could name it if asked. That is the difference between a model and a mistake, and it is a difference you will be asked to make for yourself many times in the next two years.

Models make claims that can agree or disagree with evidence. Their usefulness depends on the purpose and accuracy required; calling something a model does not exempt it from testing.

So the first question to ask of any model is not is this right? but what was this built to answer? — and the second is what it will get wrong if you push it past that. A model of a car as a point says nothing at all about whether the doors shut properly, and it was never meant to. Asking it anyway is not a fault in the model but a fault in the asking.
