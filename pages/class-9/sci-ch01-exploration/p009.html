1.5Saying What Will Happen

The most remarkable thing science does is tell you about events that have not happened yet. Given how hard a football is kicked and at what angle, you can say where it will land before it lands. Given a chemical equation and a mass of fuel, you can say how much carbon dioxide will come out. Given what you know about muscles and blood, you can say how somebody's breathing will change when they start to run.

A prediction specifies an expected observation before the test, clearly enough to compare it with a result. It may follow from a model or a tentative explanation. A prediction can be qualitative or numerical.

Both outcomes matter. Agreement supports a model within the tested conditions, though alternatives may fit too. Disagreement prompts checks of observations, assumptions and calculations. Neither outcome alone settles the whole theory.

Checking predictions

An explanation built after an event can fit it without predicting anything new. Test it against further observations. A prediction made in advance is useful because it fixes an expectation before the result is known.

In 1846, astronomers found Neptune near a position predicted from irregularities in Uranus's orbit. Finding a previously unseen object supported the calculation; a mismatch would have required checking the assumptions and observations.
