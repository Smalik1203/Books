1.5Saying What Will Happen

The most remarkable thing science does is tell you about events that have not happened yet. Given how hard a football is kicked and at what angle, you can say where it will land before it lands. Given a chemical equation and a mass of fuel, you can say how much carbon dioxide will come out. Given what you know about muscles and blood, you can say how somebody's breathing will change when they start to run.

These are not guesses dressed up. A guess cannot be wrong in any useful way — it is just a preference. A prediction commits you: it names an outcome, in advance, precisely enough that the world can contradict it. That exposure is the whole point.

Notice which arrow does the work. When a prediction comes true, very little happens: the model was already good enough and stays as it was. It is the return arrow — the day the measurement disagrees — that forces somebody to look again at the model, the assumptions, or the instrument, and that is where new science comes from.

Predicting is harder than explaining

Explaining something after it has happened is not as impressive as it feels. Whatever the result, some story can usually be told that fits it — and a story told afterwards has been allowed to see the answer. A prediction is made blind, and that is what makes it evidence.

The strongest case is predicting something nobody has ever seen. In the 1840s the planet Uranus would not keep to its calculated orbit. Rather than abandon the law of gravitation, astronomers asked what else would have to exist for the law to hold, and worked out the mass and position of an unseen eighth planet. In 1846 a telescope was pointed where the arithmetic said to point it, and Neptune was there, close to the predicted place.
