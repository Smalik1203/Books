You may not go on to study science after Grade 10, and that is a perfectly good decision. The habits in this chapter will still be worth having. Asking what a claim would predict, noticing when a number has lost its unit, telling a rough answer from a made-up one, and being willing to change your mind when the evidence changes — none of that belongs to a laboratory. It belongs to anybody who would rather know than merely believe.

At a glance
  1. A model is a deliberate simplification, keeping what matters for one question and setting the rest aside. What it leaves out has been judged, not overlooked.
  2. A model must be tested against evidence, and only for a stated purpose.
  3. Scientific words are narrowed on purpose, and every measured quantity carries a symbol and a unit. A number without its unit is a much weaker statement, not a shorter one.
  4. The SI has seven base units and everything else is built from them.
  5. A law states a pattern, a theory explains why it holds, and a principle is a broad rule for reasoning about new cases. A scientific theory is a tested explanatory framework, not a promotion stage between a hypothesis and a law.
  6. A prediction can be checked against an observation. Both agreement and disagreement provide evidence; neither alone proves or disproves every aspect of a model.
  7. Every theory has a range, and stating where it stops is part of understanding it. Only evidence settles the matter.
  8. An estimate to the nearest power of ten will tell you whether an answer is possible, and two rough routes that agree are worth more than one exact-looking number.
