Threads of Curiosity
Is food harmful during an eclipse?

The claim goes round every time there is an eclipse: food cooked or eaten while it lasts becomes unsafe. You do not need a laboratory to take it apart — only the habit of asking what would have to be true.

An eclipse is a shadow. The Moon passes between the Earth and the Sun, and for a few minutes a small patch of the Earth's surface is in the dark (Fig. 1.5). Nothing else about that patch changes. No radiation arrives that was not arriving before; less does. The temperature drops slightly, as it does at dusk every single evening.

So ask: by what mechanism? What reaches the food that did not reach it an hour earlier? What chemical change is being proposed, and why does the same food keep perfectly well in the shade of a tree, or indoors at night, when the shadow is far deeper and lasts far longer? No answer survives being asked, which is how you know.

There is something worth doing during an eclipse, and it is the opposite of avoiding it. Totality is the only occasion on which the Sun's outer atmosphere — the corona, normally drowned out by the glare — becomes visible from the ground. A great deal was learnt about the Sun in those few minutes, before instruments could make an artificial eclipse.

Look at it only through a proper eclipse filter — never sunglasses or smoked glass. That is the one real hazard here, and unlike the food it has a mechanism anybody can state.
