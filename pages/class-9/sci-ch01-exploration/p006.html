One system with seven base units

For most of history a unit was a local arrangement. A measure of grain in one town was not the measure in the next, and a mile could change length as you crossed a border. That is tolerable for a village market and impossible for a laboratory, so the world settled on one agreed set: the SI, from the French Système International d'Unités.

What is surprising is how small it is. Seven base units carry everything: the second for time, the metre for length, the kilogram for mass, the ampere for electric current, the kelvin for temperature, the mole for amount of substance, and the candela for luminous intensity in a specified direction. There is no eighth.

Every other unit is built from those seven, and its name is only a shorthand for the recipe. Speed is length divided by time, so it is measured in metres per second. Force turns out to be mass times acceleration, so its unit is kilogram-metres per second squared — which is a mouthful, so it is called the newton.

$$1\ \text{N} \;=\; 1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m}/\text{s}^{2}$$

Read that as a sentence rather than a formula and there is nothing to memorise: it says what a newton is. Any unit you meet in this book can be unpacked the same way, back to the seven.

What this rules out

The aircraft that was fuelled in the wrong units

In 1983 a Boeing 767 flying across Canada needed 22 300 kilograms of fuel. The ground crew worked out how many litres that came to — and used a figure in pounds per litre instead of kilograms per litre. A pound is a little under half a kilogram, so the aircraft left with roughly half the fuel it needed and nobody noticed.

Both engines stopped at cruising altitude. The crew glided the aircraft down to a disused airfield at Gimli, in Manitoba, and landed it without engines. The aircraft was damaged and no one was killed, which was a good deal better than it might have been.

Nobody in that story was careless with arithmetic. The multiplication was done correctly. What went wrong was that a number was carried from one place to another without its unit, and by the time it mattered there was no way to tell which unit it had been.

That is the reason every number in this book is written with its unit attached, even when the unit seems obvious, and why you will be asked to do the same. A bare number is not a shorter way of saying the same thing. It is a different and much weaker statement.
