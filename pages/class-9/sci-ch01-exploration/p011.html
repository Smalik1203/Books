1.6Where Theories Stop

Every theory has a range it works in, and outside that range it stops being reliable. This is not a defect to be apologised for. Knowing where an idea stops is part of understanding it, and a theory whose limits nobody can state is a theory nobody has tested hard enough.

Newton's mechanics is the standard example. It is superbly accurate for cricket balls, bridges, buses and planets — accurate enough to put a spacecraft in orbit around another world. It also fails, in ways that can be measured, when things move close to the speed of light or sit in very strong gravity. Neither fact cancels the other. The mechanics you will learn in Grade 9 is not a simplified lie told to children; it is the right theory for the range you will use it in.

Failures like that are how the subject moves. When a prediction and a measurement disagree and the measurement holds up, something has to give — and what replaces the old idea has to explain everything the old idea explained, plus whatever broke it. That is a demanding condition, and it is why new theories are rare and old ones survive so long.

What settles it

A result matters because of the quality of its evidence, not the status of its author. Check the method, uncertainty and whether independent observers can reproduce the finding.

Openness to correction helps science improve. Agreement and disagreement both require scrutiny: one poorly controlled result does not outweigh a large body of reliable evidence.

The test to apply is simple to state and surprisingly sharp. Ask what would have to be observed for the claim to be wrong. If you can describe that observation, the claim is doing scientific work, whether it turns out to be true or false. If nothing whatever could count against it — if every possible result would be taken as confirmation — then it is not a claim about the world at all, however scientific it sounds.

For a claim shared on a phone, first ask what it predicts and where the evidence comes from. Some claims are testable but unsupported; others are too vague to test. These are different defects.
