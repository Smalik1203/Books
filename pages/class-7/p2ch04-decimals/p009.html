Does this ever end?

Find $10 \div 3$ by long division. 10 ones $\div$ 3 gives 3 ones, with 1 one left. That becomes 10 tenths, and 10 tenths $\div$ 3 gives 3 tenths, with 1 tenth left. That becomes 10 hundredths: 3 hundredths, with 1 hundredth left. Every step leaves a remainder of 1, so the division never ends. We write $10 \div 3 = 3.333\ldots$; it cannot be written as a decimal with a fixed number of digits. You will study such numbers in a later class.

Find $10 \div 9$ and $100 \div 11$. Now find $1 \div 7$. Does it end? Keep track of the remainders: 1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, and then 1 again, so they repeat in the same order for ever. Can you explain why the division can never end? Because the remainders repeat, the digits of the quotient repeat too: $1 \div 7 = 0.142857\,142857\ldots$

A magic number: 142857

Multiply 142857 by each of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. What do you notice? Each product has the same six digits, in the same order round a circle, only starting at a different place. Now multiply 142857 by 7. What happens?

Are there other numbers like this? Yes: the repeating block of $1 \div 17$ is one. In 1927 Emil Artin conjectured that there are infinitely many such cyclic numbers, and no one has yet proved or disproved it.

Dividend, divisor and quotient

When we divide one counting number by another, larger than 1, the quotient is less than the dividend: $128 \div 4 = 32$. But $128 \div 0.4 = 320$ is greater than the dividend. Is the quotient always greater than the dividend when the divisor is a decimal? Try different divisors.
