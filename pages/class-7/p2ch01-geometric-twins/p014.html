Look at $\triangle ADB$ and $\triangle ADC$. AB = AC, as given. $\angle ADB = \angle ADC = 90^\circ$, because AD is an altitude. And AD is a side of both triangles. So the RHS condition holds, and $\triangle ADB \cong \triangle ADC$. This shows that $\angle B = \angle C$, because they are corresponding parts of congruent triangles.

Angles opposite equal sides

In a triangle, the angles opposite equal sides are equal.

Use this fact to find $\angle B$ and $\angle C$ in Fig. 1.24.

Angles in an equilateral triangle

In an equilateral triangle, all three sides are equal. What can we say about its angles? In $\triangle ABC$ of Fig. 1.25, AB = AC, so the angles opposite these sides are equal: $\angle C = \angle B$. Also AB = BC, so $\angle C = \angle A$. So all three angles are equal, just like the three sides.

How large is each angle? The three angles add up to $180^\circ$, so three times one angle is $180^\circ$, and each angle is $60^\circ$. Check this by constructing an equilateral triangle and measuring its angles. Using nothing but congruence, we have shown that every angle of an equilateral triangle is $60^\circ$.

Congruent triangles around us

Congruent triangles are found in buildings and designs, both old and new: the faces of the pyramids of Giza in Egypt, the glass pyramid of the Louvre Museum in Paris, domes built from triangles, rangoli patterns, and the steel frame of Rabindra Setu, the Howrah Bridge in Kolkata (Fig. 1.26).
