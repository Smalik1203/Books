1.2Congruence of Triangles

Meera and Rabia have to make a cardboard cut-out with the same shape and size as a triangular frame in their school. The frame is too big to trace on paper. What can they do?

Measuring the side lengths

Can a few measurements of the triangle help? The girls use a measuring tape and find that the sides of the frame are 40 cm, 60 cm and 80 cm long. Rabia then takes out her protractor to measure the angles, but Meera stops her.

“We do not need the angles,” says Meera. “With these three side lengths we can make a triangle congruent to the frame.” Rabia is not sure: she wonders whether a triangle could change its shape while its sides stay the same. Do you agree with Meera? Give a reason for your answer.

To test her claim on paper, take sides of 4 cm, 6 cm and 8 cm instead, a triangle that fits on the page and is easy to measure. Are these three lengths enough to draw a copy with the same shape and size? If they are, draw it.

Rabia plans the construction. “I would draw a segment AB of 6 cm. Then I would draw a circle of radius 4 cm with centre A, and a circle of radius 8 cm with centre B. But the circles cross at two points, E and F, so I get two triangles, $\triangle ABE$ and $\triangle ABF$ (Fig. 1.8). If they do not have the same shape and size, how do I know which one matches the frame?” Two different triangles from one set of lengths would be a real problem.
