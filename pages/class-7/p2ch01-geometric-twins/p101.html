Using What You Know

The chapter gave you five conditions that make two triangles congruent, the way a congruence is written, and the fact that angles opposite equal sides are equal. The questions below need only those. What is new is that no question says which condition to use, or whether any of them applies.

One triangle has sides of 5 cm, 7 cm and 9 cm. Another has sides of 9 cm, 5 cm and 7 cm. Are the two triangles congruent?

They are. The sides are listed in a different order, but each length in one triangle is also a length in the other, so the three sides of one are equal to the three sides of the other. By the SSS condition the triangles are congruent. To write the congruence, match the vertex between the 5 cm and 7 cm sides in one triangle with the vertex between the same two sides in the other, and so on.

In $\triangle PQR$ and $\triangle LMN$, PQ = LM, QR = MN and $\angle P = \angle L$. Must the triangles be congruent?

Mark the parts on a rough sketch. The angle between PQ and QR is $\angle Q$, not $\angle P$. So the equal angle is not included between the equal sides, and this is the SSA condition. The triangles may be congruent, but they need not be; Fig. 1.16 showed two triangles that fit a condition of this kind and are not congruent.

In $\triangle ABC$, AB = AC and $\angle B = 55^\circ$. Find $\angle A$.

The angles opposite the equal sides AB and AC are $\angle C$ and $\angle B$, so $\angle C = 55^\circ$ as well. The three angles add up to $180^\circ$, so $\angle A = 180^\circ - 55^\circ - 55^\circ = 70^\circ$. We did not need to draw the triangle or measure anything.

In the four-sided figure ABCD, AB = CD and AD = CB. Show that $\angle A = \angle C$.

Draw the diagonal BD to make two triangles. In $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle CDB$, AB = CD, AD = CB, and BD is a side of both. By SSS, $\triangle ABD \cong \triangle CDB$. In this congruence A corresponds to C, so $\angle A = \angle C$.
