Does the triangle inequality guarantee a triangle?

In every example so far, lengths that satisfy the triangle inequality have made a triangle. Will they always? To be sure, we must show that the two circles always cross. Draw the longest length as the base AB, and use the two shorter lengths as the radii of the circles. Fig. 7.10 shows the three things that can happen.

Only in (iii) do we get a third vertex, because only there do the two circles have a point off the line AB in common. Compare the sum of the radii with AB in each case.

(i) The circles touch at one point of AB, so the two radii exactly fill AB, and the sum of the radii equals AB.

(ii) The circles do not meet, so the radii leave a gap on AB, and the sum of the radii is less than AB.

(iii) The circles cross, so AB is made of one whole radius and only part of the other, and the sum of the radii is more than AB.

If the lengths satisfy the triangle inequality, the two shorter lengths add up to more than the longest. That is case (iii), so the circles cross and the triangle exists.

When three lengths make a triangle

Three lengths are the sides of a triangle exactly when they satisfy the triangle inequality. It is enough to check that the longest length is less than the sum of the other two.

Find three sets of lengths for which the circles only touch, and three for which they do not meet. Then write out, step by step, how you would decide whether any three lengths make a triangle.
