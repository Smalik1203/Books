Exterior angles

If one side of a triangle is extended, the angle between the extension and the next side is an exterior angle of the triangle. In Fig. 7.18, BC is extended to D, and $\angle ACD$ is an exterior angle.

Find $\angle ACD$ when $\angle A = 50^\circ$ and $\angle B = 60^\circ$. By the angle sum property, $50^\circ + 60^\circ + \angle ACB = 180^\circ$, so $\angle ACB = 70^\circ$. The angles $\angle ACB$ and $\angle ACD$ make a straight angle, so $\angle ACD = 180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$.

Try other sizes for $\angle A$ and $\angle B$, and find the exterior angle each time. What do you notice? Here is a hint: $\angle A + \angle B + \angle ACB = 180^\circ$, and also $\angle ACD + \angle ACB = 180^\circ$. What must be true?

7.4Altitudes

We often speak of heights: the height of a person, of a tree or of a building. What is the height of a vertex of a triangle above the opposite side, and how can it be measured? In Fig. 7.19, AD is drawn from A perpendicular to BC. Its length is the height of A above BC, and the segment AD is called an altitude of the triangle.

Every triangle has three altitudes, one from each vertex to the opposite side. In Fig. 7.20 they are AD, BE and CF. When we speak of the height of a triangle, we mean the altitude to whichever side we take as the base.
