3.2The Lowest Common Multiple

Torans of the same length

Anshu and Guna make torans by placing strips of cloth end to end. Anshu’s strips are 6 cm long and Guna’s are 8 cm long. They want to make torans of the same length. What is the shortest toran they can both make?

Any toran Anshu makes is a multiple of 6 cm long: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48 cm, … Any toran Guna makes is a multiple of 8 cm: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48 cm, … A toran both can make must be a common multiple of 6 and 8, such as 24 cm or 48 cm (Fig. 3.4). The smallest common multiple is 24, so the shortest toran is 24 cm. Is there a largest common multiple?

Free gajak

A sweet shop gives free gajak, a sweet made of sesame seeds, jaggery and ghee, to school children every Monday. Today is Monday, and Kabamai had some. She visits the shop once every 10 days. After how many days will she next get free gajak?

The shop gives gajak again after 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, 56, 63, 70, … days, the multiples of 7. Kabamai visits after 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, … days, the multiples of 10. The first day in both lists is day 70, so she next gets free gajak after 70 days. Again the answer is the smallest of the common multiples.

The lowest common multiple (LCM) of two or more numbers is the smallest of their common multiples. The LCM of 6 and 8 is 24, and the LCM of 7 and 10 is 70.

Remember the Idli-Vada game from Class 6? Players count in turn, and say “idli” for a multiple of the first number, “vada” for a multiple of the second, and “idli-vada” for a multiple of both. For each pair, find the first number at which “idli-vada” is said: (a) 4 and 6, (b) 7 and 11, (c) 14 and 30, (d) 15 and 55. Is it always the LCM? Explain.

The LCM from prime factorisations

Listing multiples is slow for larger numbers, as (c) and (d) show. Prime factorisation helps here too. Compare a number with one of its multiples, say 36 and $648 = 36 \times 18$.

$36 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3$

$648 = (2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3 \times 3)$

The factorisation of the multiple contains the whole factorisation of the number, and some more primes. Does this happen for every multiple? How can it help us find the LCM?
