A quicker way to find the HCF and the LCM

Fig. 3.5 shows a procedure for 84 and 180 together. Can you explain it? It is like the division method, but at each step both numbers are divided by a prime that is a factor of both, and the two quotients are written on the next line. We stop when the two numbers have no common prime factor. How can we use it to find the HCF of 84 and 180? (Hint: it shows that $84 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 7$ and $180 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 15$.)

The divisors on the left make up the largest common part, so the HCF of 84 and 180 is $2 \times 2 \times 3 = 12$. Fig. 3.6 shows two more pairs.

(i) HCF $= 2 \times 5 \times 5 \times 3 = 150$    LCM $= 2 \times 5 \times 5 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 300$

(ii) HCF $= 2 \times 5 \times 7 = 70$    LCM $= 2 \times 5 \times 7 \times 9 \times 11 = 6930$

The same procedure gives the LCM as well: multiply all the divisors on the left by the two numbers left at the bottom. Why is this the LCM? (Hint: does this product contain the prime factorisations of both 300 and 150? Is it the smallest such number?)

Guna says, “I have a faster way. For 300 and 150, I first divide both by 50, then by 3. The HCF is $50 \times 3$ and the LCM is $50 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1$.” Anshu tries the same idea: “For 630 and 770, I divide by 10 and then by 7. The HCF is $10 \times 7 = 70$ and the LCM is $10 \times 7 \times 9 \times 11 = 6930$.” Can you see why this works? We do not have to divide by one prime at a time; we can divide both numbers by any common factor we notice (Fig. 3.7). Try it for (a) 90 and 150, (b) 84 and 132.
