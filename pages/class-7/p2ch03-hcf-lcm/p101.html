Using What You Know
The chapter’s ideas, in questions that do not say which one to use.

The chapter gave you prime factorisation, the HCF and the LCM, and the fact that their product is the product of the two numbers. The questions below need only those. What is new is deciding whether a question is asking for a common factor or a common multiple. Once that choice is made, each answer takes only a few lines.

The HCF of two numbers is 6 and their LCM is 72. One of the numbers is 18. What is the other?

The product of the two numbers is $\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM} = 6 \times 72 = 432$. So the other number is $432 \div 18 = 24$. Check: $18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3$ and $24 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$ give an HCF of $2 \times 3 = 6$ and an LCM of $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 72$. We did not need to search for the number: the rule for the product gave it directly.

Can two numbers have an HCF of 4 and an LCM of 30?

The HCF divides both numbers, and each number divides the LCM, so the HCF must divide the LCM. But 4 does not divide 30. So no such numbers exist, and we can say so without trying a single pair.

A floor 18 m by 30 m is to be covered with the fewest square tiles of the same whole-number size. How many tiles are needed?

The side of a tile must divide both 18 and 30, and the fewest tiles means the largest side: the HCF, 6 m. Then $18 \div 6 = 3$ tiles fit across and $30 \div 6 = 5$ along, so $3 \times 5 = 15$ tiles.
