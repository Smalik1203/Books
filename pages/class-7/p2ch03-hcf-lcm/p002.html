Primes and prime factorisation

Listing every factor gets slow when numbers have many factors, as you may have found with 30 and 50, and with 28 and 42. It is also easy to miss a factor. Is there a quicker and more reliable way? There is, and it uses prime factorisation.

Recall that a prime is a number greater than 1 whose only factors are 1 and itself. Every number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes: keep splitting composite factors until only primes are left. This is its prime factorisation. For example, $90 = 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5$.

We could also start with $90 = 3 \times 30$ or $90 = 2 \times 45$. Do these lead to the same primes? They always do; only the order may change. For example, $90 = 3 \times 30 = 3 \times 2 \times 15 = 3 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5$. The prime factorisation of a prime is the prime itself.

The division method

Fig. 3.2 shows a tidy way to find a prime factorisation. At each step, divide by a prime that is a factor, and write the quotient on the next line. Stop when the quotient is a prime. The prime factors are the divisors on the left together with the last number at the bottom, so $105 = 3 \times 5 \times 7$ and $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$. We call this the division method. It keeps all the working in one column, so no prime gets lost.
