Draw a line through the bend, parallel to $l$ and $m$. The part of the bend above it and the $30^\circ$ angle are alternate angles, so that part is $30^\circ$. In the same way, the lower part is $45^\circ$. So the angle at the bend is $30^\circ + 45^\circ = 75^\circ$. The extra line turned one unknown angle into two known ones.

Two lines cross. Can three of the four angles be $60^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $100^\circ$?

Any three of the four angles include two that sit next to each other, and two neighbouring angles form a linear pair. The pair here is either $60^\circ$ and $60^\circ$, which make $120^\circ$, or $60^\circ$ and $100^\circ$, which make $160^\circ$. Neither is $180^\circ$. So no such crossing exists.

Problem 1

Two lines cross, and one of the four angles is $47^\circ$. Going round the point from that angle, the other three angles are

  1. $133^\circ$, $47^\circ$, $133^\circ$
  2. $43^\circ$, $47^\circ$, $43^\circ$
  3. $47^\circ$, $47^\circ$, $47^\circ$
  4. $133^\circ$, $133^\circ$, $133^\circ$
Solution

The next angle round forms a linear pair with the $47^\circ$ angle, so it is $180^\circ - 47^\circ = 133^\circ$. The angle after that is vertically opposite the $47^\circ$ angle, so it is $47^\circ$, and the last is vertically opposite the $133^\circ$ angle.

The answer is (a). Option (b) takes the neighbouring angle from $90^\circ$ instead of $180^\circ$, as if the lines made a right angle between them. Option (c) makes every angle equal, which happens only when the lines are perpendicular. Option (d) forgets that opposite angles are equal.
