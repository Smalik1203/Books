Parallel lines by folding

We can do the same with paper folding. Suppose a crease $l$ and a point A are marked on a sheet. We already know how to fold a crease perpendicular to $l$. Fold one that passes through A, and call it $t$. Now fold a crease through A that is perpendicular to $t$, and call it $m$. Fig. 5.20 shows the steps. Lines $l$ and $m$ are parallel.

Why are lines $l$ and $m$ parallel? Think of $t$ as a transversal of $l$ and $m$.

5.8Alternate Angles

In Fig. 5.21, the parallel lines $l$ and $m$ are crossed by the transversal $t$. The angles $\angle d$ and $\angle f$ lie between the two lines, on opposite sides of $t$. They are called alternate angles. $\angle c$ and $\angle e$ are also alternate angles.

To find the alternate angle of $\angle f$, first find its corresponding angle, $\angle b$. The angle vertically opposite $\angle b$ is $\angle d$, the alternate angle of $\angle f$.
