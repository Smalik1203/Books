3.4Decimal Place Value

Each time we needed to measure more exactly, we split a part into 10 equal smaller parts. Could we split a unit into 4 equal parts, or 5, or 8, instead? We could. A unit split into 4 gives quarters, and each quarter split into 4 again gives sixteenths, because 16 of them make a unit. So why do we choose 10 every time?

The reason is the part that 10 plays in our place value system. In 281, the 2 stands for 2 hundreds, the 8 for 8 tens and the 1 for 1 one. Each place is 10 times the place on its right: 10 ones make a ten, 10 tens make a hundred, 10 hundreds make a thousand.

To go on to quantities smaller than one, we split one into 10 equal parts, which gives a tenth. A tenth split into 10 gives a hundredth, and a hundredth split into 10 gives a thousandth, $\tfrac{1}{1000}$. Table 3.2 shows how the places carry on in both directions.

Table 3.2 Each place is 10 times the place on its right
ThousandsHundredsTensOnesTenthsHundredthsThousandths
1000100101$\tfrac{1}{10}$$\tfrac{1}{100}$$\tfrac{1}{1000}$

This way of writing numbers is called the decimal system, because it is built on 10. The Latin word decem means ten. It is related to the Sanskrit word daśa, and words like it for ten are found in many Indian languages, including Odia, Konkani, Marathi, Gujarati, Hindi, Kashmiri, Bodo and Assamese.
