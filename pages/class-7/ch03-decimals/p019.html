Numbers that only look like decimals

Sarayu gets a message: “The bus will reach the station 4.5 hours after noon.” When will it arrive: at 4:05, at 4:50, or at 4:25?

None of these. The 0.5 hour means an hour split into 10 equal parts, and 5 of those parts taken. Each part is $60 \div 10 = 6$ minutes, and 5 parts are 30 minutes. So the bus arrives at 4:30 in the afternoon.

A doorway is 2 feet 5 inches wide, and a girl orders a door 2.5 feet wide. A foot is 12 inches, so she gets 2 feet 6 inches, and the door does not fit.

In cricket, a scoreboard may show Overs: 5.5. An over has 6 balls, so this means 5 overs and 5 balls, not five and a half overs. It is a way of writing that looks like a decimal but is not one. Where else have you seen this?

A short history of the decimal point

Fractions with denominators like 10, 100 and 1000 appear in the works of many Indian astronomers and mathematicians of long ago. Among them are the 8th-century books of Śrīdharācārya on arithmetic and algebra.

Decimal notation much like ours was explained in detail around 950 CE by the Arab mathematician al-Uqlīdisī. His book was called The Book of Chapters on Indian Arithmetic. He wrote 0.059375 as 0ˈ059375, with a small mark after the whole number part.

In the 15th century, people separated the whole number part from the rest in several ways. Some put a vertical mark on the last whole number digit. Some wrote the two parts in different colours. Others wrote a small raised number saying how many digits came after the whole number part.

In the 16th century, John Napier of Scotland and Christopher Clavius of Germany used a point, and François Viète of France used a comma. Many countries still use a comma, and write 1,000.5 as 1 000,5. But the point is the mark most widely used in our place value system.
