Estimating sums and differences

Sonu has been adding decimals, and he makes a claim. “When we add two decimal numbers, the sum is always more than the sum of their whole number parts. It is also always less than that sum plus 2.”

Take 25.936 and 8.202. Their whole number parts are 25 and 8. Sonu says the sum is more than $25 + 8 = 33$, and less than $33 + 2 = 35$. Check whether he is right for these numbers. Will it work for any two decimal numbers? What about $25.93603259 + 8.202$? And what happens if both numbers are whole, such as 3.0 and 4.0?

Find a similar rule for a difference. Between which two whole numbers must the difference of two decimal numbers lie? Test your rule on $17.4 - 9.85$.

Estimating an answer before working it out is a good habit. If the worked answer is far from the estimate, a digit has probably slipped into the wrong place. So estimate first, then work it out, and then compare the two.

3.8More on the Decimal System

A decimal point in the wrong place, or a unit mixed up with another, can look like a small slip. It can cause serious trouble. Here are two things that really happened.

In 2013, a city office in Amsterdam, in the Netherlands, sent out 188 million euros in housing money instead of 1.8 million euros. A computer program had read the amounts in cents instead of euros, and 1 cent is one-hundredth of a euro.

In 1983, ground staff filled an Air Canada aeroplane with 22,300 pounds of fuel when 22,300 kilograms were needed. A pound is only about 0.45 kg, so the plane had less than half the fuel it needed. It ran out in the air, and the pilots landed it safely on an old, unused airfield.

Mistakes like these also happen with medicines. Reading 0.05 mg as 0.5 mg gives a patient 10 times the right amount. So always look carefully at the unit and at where the decimal point is.
