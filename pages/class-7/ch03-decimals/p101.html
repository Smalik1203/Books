Using What You Know
The chapter’s ideas, in questions that do not say which one to use.

The chapter gave you tenths, hundredths and thousandths, the decimal point, and ways to compare, add and subtract decimals. Every question below needs only those. What is new is that the question does not tell you which one to use.

Which is larger, 0.7 or 0.65?

It is tempting to say 0.65, because 65 is more than 7. But the digits after the point are not a whole number. Write a zero at the end of 0.7, which does not change its value: $0.7 = 0.70$, which is 70 hundredths. And 0.65 is 65 hundredths. So 0.7 is larger, by 5 hundredths. We could also compare the tenths straight away: 7 tenths against 6 tenths. What decided it was the place of each digit, not how many digits there were.

Without adding, is $3.48 + 2.67$ more than 6 or less than 6?

The whole number parts give $3 + 2 = 5$. The rest depends on whether 0.48 and 0.67 together make at least one more whole. Now 0.48 is nearly half, and 0.67 is more than half, so together they are more than 1. The sum is more than 6. Adding confirms it: $3.48 + 2.67 = 6.15$. We only had to ask whether the parts after the point made a whole.

A rope is 5 m long. Pieces of 1.35 m and 2.8 m are cut from it. How much rope is left?

Write 5 as 5.00 and 2.8 as 2.80, so that every number has two digits after the point and the columns line up. The two pieces together are $1.35 + 2.80 = 4.15$ m.
