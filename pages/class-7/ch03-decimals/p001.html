The Decimal Point

3.1The Need for Smaller Units

Sonu’s mother is mending a toy. She tries to fix two pieces together with a screw, but it will not hold. “This screw is the wrong size,” she says. She takes another screw from the box, and this one works.

To Sonu the two screws look exactly the same. When he looks closely, he sees that one is a little longer than the other. He wonders how such a tiny difference can matter so much, and how big the difference really is.

In Fig. 3.1, the same screw lies above three rulers. The first ruler is marked only in centimetres. On the first ruler we can say only that the screw is between 2 cm and 3 cm long. The second is also marked in half centimetres, so we can say that it is more than $2\tfrac{1}{2}$ cm but less than 3 cm. The third ruler has each centimetre split into 10 equal parts, and the screw ends at the seventh part after 2.

Which ruler measures the screw most exactly? Why?

On the third ruler, each centimetre is split into 10 equal parts, and one part is $\tfrac{1}{10}$ cm. To reach the end of the screw, we go from 0 to 2, and then 7 more of these parts. So the screw is 2 cm and $\tfrac{7}{10}$ cm long. We write this as $2\tfrac{7}{10}$ cm, and read it as “two and seven-tenths centimetres”. In the same way, $3\tfrac{2}{10}$ cm is “three and two-tenths centimetres”: 3 whole centimetres and 2 of the small parts.

Splitting a unit into smaller parts lets us measure more exactly. When a whole unit is too big to describe a length, we count the smaller parts too.
