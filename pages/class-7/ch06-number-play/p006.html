6.3Sums in Grids

In Fig. 6.9 each of the numbers 1 to 9 appears once in a $3 \times 3$ grid. What do the circled numbers show?

Each circle holds the sum of its row or its column: $4 + 7 + 5 = 16$, and $4 + 6 + 3 = 13$. Fill each grid in Fig. 6.10 with the numbers 1 to 9, using each number once, so that the rows and columns have the sums shown. Is there only one way? Then make up a grid like this for a classmate.

Now try Fig. 6.11. Why can nobody fill it? The smallest sum of three different numbers from 1 to 9 is $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$, and the largest is $7 + 8 + 9 = 24$. So no circle can hold 5 or 26.

Look again at the grids you filled, and add up the three row sums of each. Then add up the three column sums. Kishor noticed that all six circled numbers of a grid always add up to 90, and Vidya that the three rows alone, or the three columns alone, always make 45. Why? Between them the three rows hold every number from 1 to 9 exactly once, so their sums add up to $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + 9 = 45$. The same is true of the columns.
