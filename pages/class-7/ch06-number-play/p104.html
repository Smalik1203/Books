Problem 4

In PQ $+$ QP $= 121$, P and Q are digits, and PQ and QP are two-digit numbers. What is ${\mathrm{P} + \mathrm{Q}}$?

  1. 12
  2. 10
  3. 1
  4. 11
Solution

PQ is 10 lots of P and Q ones, and QP is 10 lots of Q and P ones. Together they make 11 lots of P and 11 lots of Q, so the sum is a multiple of 11. The digits themselves cannot be found this way, only their sum, which is why the question asks for P $+$ Q.

$11 \times (\mathrm{P} + \mathrm{Q}) = 121$, so P $+$ Q $= 11$.

The answer is (d). For example, $29 + 92 = 121$ and $56 + 65 = 121$. Option (a) adds the digits of 121 as if they were P, Q and a carry. Option (b) forgets the carry from the ones place. Option (c) is the first digit of the sum.

Problem 5

For $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, the expression $5n + 3$ gives

  1. only even numbers
  2. only odd numbers
  3. even and odd numbers in turn
  4. only multiples of 5
Solution

Its values are 8, 13, 18, 23, and so on. Each step adds 5, an odd number, and adding an odd number changes the parity. So the values go even, odd, even, odd.

The answer is (c). Options (a) and (b) each look at only one value of $n$. Option (d) forgets the 3: the values are 3 more than multiples of 5.

Most of these problems were settled by structure rather than by arithmetic: how many odd numbers there are, how a total is shared among rows, how a rhythm must begin, and what a two-digit number is made of.
