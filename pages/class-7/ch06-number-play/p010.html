A $4 \times 4$ magic square from Khajuraho

The oldest known $4 \times 4$ magic square is carved in a 10th-century inscription at the Parshvanath Jain temple in Khajuraho. It is called the Chautisa Yantra; chautisa means thirty-four (Fig. 6.14). Each of the numbers 1 to 16 appears in it exactly once, just as 1 to 9 do in a $3 \times 3$ square.

Check that every row, column and diagonal adds up to 34. Then look for other groups of four numbers that make 34: try the four corners, or any $2 \times 2$ block of squares. How many $2 \times 2$ blocks are there, and do they all work?

Magic squares around the world

The oldest recorded magic square is the Lo Shu square of China, more than 2000 years old. A story tells of a turtle that came out of the Lo River with the square on its shell. Magic squares were later studied in India, Japan, Central Asia and Europe, and Indian mathematicians found general ways of building squares of size $5 \times 5$ and larger. Magic squares are carved on a pillar of an 8th-century temple at Palani in Tamil Nadu, and they are found in homes and shops as yantras, such as the Kubera Yantra in Fig. 6.15. What is the magic sum of each square in Fig. 6.15?

The Lo Shu square uses the numbers 1 to 9, so its magic sum must be 15; compare it with the square you made. The Kubera Yantra uses the nine consecutive numbers 20 to 28, with 24 in the centre. Fig. 6.13 says its magic sum is $3 \times 24 = 72$. Check this by adding up one row and one diagonal.
