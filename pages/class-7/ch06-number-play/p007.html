Magic squares

A square grid of numbers is a magic square if every row, every column and both diagonals have the same sum. That sum is the magic sum.

There are 3,62,880 different ways to put the numbers 1 to 9 into a $3 \times 3$ grid, so guessing is hopeless. Instead, let us ask questions and let the answers build the square.

What must the magic sum be?

The row sums of any grid of the numbers 1 to 9 add up to 45. In a magic square the three row sums are equal, so each of them is $45 \div 3 = 15$.

Observation 1: a magic square of the numbers 1 to 9 has magic sum 15.

What can go in the centre?

The centre lies on a row, a column and both diagonals. Suppose 9 were in the centre. The 8 must go somewhere, and the line through 8 and the centre would need $8 + 9 + {}$another number $= 15$. That is impossible, since $8 + 9$ is already 17. Suppose instead 1 were in the centre. The line through 2 and the centre would need a third number of $15 - 3 = 12$, and there is no 12. Check 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8 in the same way.

Observation 2: the centre must be 5.

Where do 1 and 9 go?

Every square apart from the centre is either a corner or a middle square (Fig. 6.13). A corner lies on three lines: a row, a column and a diagonal. If 1 were in a corner, each of those three lines would need its own two numbers adding up to 14. But from the numbers 2 to 9 there are only two such pairs, $5 + 9$ and $6 + 8$. So 1 cannot be in a corner. The same reasoning works for 9, whose partners must add up to 6: there are only $1 + 5$ and $2 + 4$.
