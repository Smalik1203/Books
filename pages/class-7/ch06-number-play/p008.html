Observation 3: 1 and 9 go in middle squares, on opposite sides of the centre.

So one row or column of the square is 1, 5, 9. Put 1 in the top middle square. The top row then needs two more numbers adding up to 14, other than 5 and 9, and those are 8 and 6. Now finish the square yourself.

The general $3 \times 3$ magic square

Take a magic square of consecutive numbers, and call the number in its centre $m$. Every other number is some amount more or less than $m$. In the square of 1 to 9, with 5 in the centre, 8 is $m + 3$ and 1 is $m - 4$. Writing every number in this way gives Fig. 6.14. Try it with your square of 2 to 10, where $m = 6$: there 9 is $m + 3$ and 2 is $m - 4$, just as before.
