Where do 1 and 9 go?

Every square apart from the centre is either a corner or a middle square (Fig. 6.12). A corner lies on three lines: a row, a column and a diagonal. If 1 were in a corner, each of those three lines would need its own two numbers adding up to 14. But from the numbers 2 to 9 there are only two such pairs, $5 + 9$ and $6 + 8$. So 1 cannot be in a corner. The same reasoning works for 9, whose partners must add up to 6: there are only $1 + 5$ and $2 + 4$.

Observation 3: 1 and 9 go in middle squares, on opposite sides of the centre.

So one row or column of the square is 1, 5, 9. Put 1 in the top middle square. The top row then needs two more numbers adding up to 14, other than 5 and 9, and those are 8 and 6. Now finish the square yourself.

Exercise Set 6.3
  1. How many different magic squares can be made with the numbers 1 to 9? Is a square that has been turned round or flipped over a different one?
  1. Make a magic square with the numbers 2 to 10. How did you do it? How does it compare with your square of the numbers 1 to 9?
  1. Take a magic square and
    1. add 1 to every number
    2. double every number.
    Is each new grid a magic square? What happens to the magic sum?
  1. What else could you do to every number in a magic square and still have a magic square?
  1. How would you make a magic square from any nine consecutive numbers, such as 3 to 11 or 9 to 17?
