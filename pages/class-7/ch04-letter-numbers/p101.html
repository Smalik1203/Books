Using What You Know

The chapter gave you letter-numbers, the rules for simplifying expressions, and a way of showing that a pattern always holds. Every question below needs only those. What is new is that the question does not tell you which one to use.

Rani says that $3n$ and $n + 3$ are equal, because both have a 3 and an $n$. Is she right?

Test a value. When $n = 5$, $3n$ is 15 but $n + 3$ is 8. The two expressions have different values, so they are not equal. $3n$ means 3 times $n$, and $n + 3$ means 3 more than $n$. To show that two expressions are not equal, one value where they differ is enough.

Think of a number. Add 5. Double the result. Subtract 10. Why do you always end with double your number?

Call the number $n$. Follow the steps with the letter-number instead of a particular number.

$n \;\to\; n + 5 \;\to\; 2(n + 5) = 2n + 10 \;\to\; 2n + 10 - 10 = 2n$

The result is $2n$ whatever $n$ is. Trying numbers could only show that the trick worked for those numbers. The letter-number shows why it works for all of them.

Add any three counting numbers that come one after another, such as 6, 7 and 8. How is the sum related to the middle number?

$6 + 7 + 8 = 21$, which is 3 times 7. Let the middle number be $m$. The number before it is $m - 1$, and the number after it is $m + 1$.

$(m - 1) + m + (m + 1) = m + m + m + (-1) + 1 = 3m$

The sum is always 3 times the middle number. Choosing the middle number as $m$, rather than the smallest, made the 1s cancel.
