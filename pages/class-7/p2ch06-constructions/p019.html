In the coloured problem, every tile must be placed with its dark square on a dark square of the grid and its white square on a white square. If the plain region can be tiled, can the coloured one? If the coloured region can be tiled, can the plain one? The answer to both questions is yes, because a $2 \times 1$ tile placed anywhere on a chessboard colouring always covers one dark square and one white square.

Counting colours

A region tiled with $2 \times 1$ tiles, coloured like a chessboard, always has as many dark squares as white squares.

Is the coloured region in Fig. 6.32 tileable? It has 8 white squares and only 6 dark squares, so it can never be tiled! Use this idea to find another square which, when removed from a $5 \times 3$ grid, leaves a region that cannot be tiled.

Isn't it surprising that adding colours, which seems to make the problem more complicated, makes it easier to solve?
