Tiling the whole plane

So far we have tiled a given region. What about tiling the entire plane, going on for ever in every direction? Can you think of a shape whose copies can tile the whole plane? Squares clearly can.

Can any other regular polygon tile the plane? What about equilateral triangles? Copies of equilateral triangles do tile the plane, and so do regular hexagons. Look at the angles at a point where the tiles meet in Fig. 6.34. What do they add up to?

The plane can also be tiled with more than one shape, and with polygons that are not regular. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher (1898–1972), whose work explored mathematical ideas such as tiling, found clever ways to tile the plane with shapes of birds, fish and lizards. Mathematicians are still finding new ways to tile the plane; a shape that tiles it without ever repeating its pattern was found as recently as 2023. Some of the tilings mathematicians have found use pentagons that are not regular. In India, the floors, walls and carved stone screens of many old buildings are decorated with tilings that repeat over and over.

Have you seen tilings in daily life? They are used in floors, walls and designs. Tilings are found in nature too. The front of a honeycomb, and of some wasp nests, is tiled with hexagonal cells. The insects keep their eggs and young safe in these cells and store food in them, and since the region is tiled, no space is wasted. Scientists still wonder how bees and wasps manage to build hexagonal cells. The next time you see a tiling, look at it closely!

What this chapter showed
  1. Dividing a line segment, or any geometric figure, into two identical parts is called bisection.
  2. Any point at the same distance from the two ends of a segment lies on its perpendicular bisector. This lets us construct the perpendicular bisector with a ruler and a compass.
  3. The same method, changed slightly, constructs a $90^\circ$ angle at any point on a line.
  4. An angle can be bisected, and copied, with a ruler and a compass. Both constructions work because of congruent triangles.
  5. A $60^\circ$ angle is constructed by constructing an equilateral triangle, and six equilateral triangles make a regular hexagon.
  6. Angles placed around a point without overlapping fill the space exactly when they add up to $360^\circ$.
  7. Covering a region with shapes, with no gaps and no overlaps, is called tiling. Colouring a region like a chessboard shows when $2 \times 1$ tiles cannot cover it.
