Can a $4 \times 7$ grid be tiled with $2 \times 1$ tiles? What about a $5 \times 7$ grid?

There is no way to tile a $5 \times 7$ grid with $2 \times 1$ tiles. To see why, notice that the grid has 35 unit squares, and each tile covers exactly 2 of them. Complete the explanation.

Can an $m \times n$ grid be tiled with $2 \times 1$ tiles if $m$ and $n$ are both even? If so, find a general method. One method is to fill every column with upright tiles, which works because the number of rows is even.

Can an $m \times n$ grid be tiled with $2 \times 1$ tiles if one of $m$ and $n$ is even and the other is odd? If so, find a general method. What if $m$ and $n$ are both odd? Give reasons.

Fig. 6.31(a) shows a $5 \times 3$ grid with one unit square removed. Now it has an even number of squares. Can it be tiled with $2 \times 1$ tiles? What about the region in Fig. 6.31(b)?

Now try the region on the left of Fig. 6.32, a $5 \times 3$ grid with the middle square of the top row removed. Were you able to tile it? How can we be sure that it cannot be tiled? Can you find another square which, when removed from a $5 \times 3$ grid, leaves a region that cannot be tiled?

There is a clever way to look at such questions. Colour the unit squares dark and white, like a chessboard, so that every dark square has only white neighbours and every white square has only dark neighbours. Then use tiles with one dark square and one white square. The region of Fig. 6.32 becomes the coloured grid on the right.
