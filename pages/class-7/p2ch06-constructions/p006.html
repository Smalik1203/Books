Their constructions use a different kind of compass from yours: a rope. A rope can draw circles and arcs, and when it is pulled tight it gives a straight line. The Sulba-sutras also have other ways to construct perpendicular lines. Pegs and ropes could lay out altars on the ground far larger than any compass could draw. Here is a rope construction of the perpendicular bisector, from the Katyayana-sulbasutra (1.2).

  1. XY is a segment drawn on the ground. Fix a peg upright in the ground at X and another at Y.
  2. Take a long rope and make a loop at each end. Leaving out the rope used in the loops, fold the rope in half to find its midpoint, and mark it.
  3. Fasten the loops to the pegs at X and Y.
  4. Pull the midpoint of the rope away from XY on one side, until both halves of the rope are tight. Mark the midpoint's position as A.
  5. In the same way, pull the midpoint tight on the other side of XY, and mark its position as B.
  6. AB is the perpendicular bisector of XY.

Bisecting an angle for a design

How do we construct the flower in Fig. 6.9(a)? Its supporting lines are shown in Fig. 6.9(b). What is the angle between two lines next to each other? Each petal is drawn with two arcs of equal radius, like the petals of Fig. 6.6, and each lies along one of the supporting lines. For the eight petals to be spaced evenly around the centre, the supporting lines must be spaced evenly too, so every angle between neighbouring lines must be the same.
