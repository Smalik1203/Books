Using What You Know

The chapter gave you three facts to build with: points at equal distances from X and Y lie on the perpendicular bisector, angles can be bisected and copied, and angles around a point add up to $360^\circ$. What changes now is that the question does not tell you which fact to use.

A point P is 5 cm from X and 5 cm from Y. Another point Q, on the other side of XY, is 3 cm from X and 3 cm from Y. What can you say about the line PQ?

P and Q are each at equal distances from X and Y, so both lie on the perpendicular bisector of XY. A line is fixed by two of its points, so PQ is the perpendicular bisector of XY. We did not need to know the length of XY, or to draw anything.

Using only a ruler and a compass, can you construct angles of $75^\circ$, $105^\circ$ and $22.5^\circ$?

We can construct $60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, and bisecting gives $30^\circ$, $15^\circ$ and $45^\circ$. Copying one angle beside another adds them: $75^\circ = 60^\circ + 15^\circ$ and $105^\circ = 90^\circ + 15^\circ$. Bisecting $45^\circ$ gives $22.5^\circ$. So all three can be constructed, each from angles we already have.

Two opposite corner squares are removed from a $6 \times 6$ grid. Can the rest be tiled with $2 \times 1$ tiles?

Colour the grid like a chessboard. Opposite corners of a $6 \times 6$ grid have the same colour, so removing them leaves 18 squares of one colour and 16 of the other. Every tile covers one of each, so no tiling is possible. There are 34 squares, an even number, so counting squares alone would not have shown this.

Can copies of a regular pentagon tile the plane? Each angle of a regular pentagon is $108^\circ$.

Where tiles meet at a point, their angles must add up to exactly $360^\circ$. Three pentagons give $3 \times 108^\circ = 324^\circ$, which leaves a gap, and four give $432^\circ$, which is too much. No number of pentagon corners fills the space around a point, so regular pentagons cannot tile the plane.
