Can copies of a regular pentagon tile the plane? Each angle of a regular pentagon is $108^\circ$.

Where tiles meet at a point, their angles must add up to exactly $360^\circ$. Three pentagons give $3 \times 108^\circ = 324^\circ$, which leaves a gap, and four give $432^\circ$, which is too much. No number of pentagon corners fills the space around a point, so regular pentagons cannot tile the plane.

Problem 1

A point A lies on the perpendicular bisector of XY, and $AX = 7$ cm. Then AY is

  1. 7 cm
  2. 14 cm
  3. 3.5 cm
  4. cannot be found
Solution

Every point on the perpendicular bisector of XY is at the same distance from X and from Y. This follows from the congruent triangles AOX and AOY, where O is the midpoint of XY. So $AY = AX = 7$ cm, and the answer is (a). Option (b) doubles the length and (c) halves it, as if A were the midpoint of something. Option (d) forgets that the position of A on the bisector is all we need.

Problem 2

A $90^\circ$ angle is bisected, and then one of the two halves is bisected. The smallest angle made is

  1. $45^\circ$
  2. $30^\circ$
  3. $11.25^\circ$
  4. $22.5^\circ$
Solution

first bisection: $90^\circ \div 2 = 45^\circ$

second bisection: $45^\circ \div 2 = 22.5^\circ$

The answer is (d). Option (a) stops after one bisection, (c) bisects three times, and (b) is a third of $90^\circ$, which bisection never gives.
