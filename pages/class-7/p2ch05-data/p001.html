Connecting the Dots…

5.1Of Questions and Statements

Your teacher is meeting two childhood friends this evening. One friend is 5 feet tall and the other is 6 feet tall. Can you guess which friend is a man and which is a woman? You know nothing about them except their heights, so any answer can only be a guess.

Many of us would guess that the 5-foot friend is a woman and the 6-foot friend is a man. The guess could be wrong. But in our experience men are more often taller than women, and a 5-foot man or a 6-foot woman is less common. Making a sensible guess from what usually happens is a simple example of statistical thinking. Statistics studies such patterns in data, so that our guesses can be as good as they can possibly be.

We hear statements of this kind all the time.

  • Jemimah has batted very steadily all year, so we can expect a century from her in tomorrow’s match.
  • It takes me about 15 minutes to cycle home from school.
  • My pen will probably last two more weeks, so I should buy a new one soon.
  • The population of their village has fallen by about 100 in the last ten years.
  • Since I began eating more fruit and vegetables, I can run 2 km more each day.
  • David spends about 7 hours a day at school.

These are statistical statements. A statistical statement is a claim or a summary about something, given in numbers: amounts, parts of a whole, chances or predictions.

A statistical question is a question that we answer by collecting data. “How tall are the Class 7 students in our school?” is one. The students are not all the same height, so no single measurement answers it. We collect the heights, study them, and then say what we find.

“Are onions usually costlier in Yahapur or in Wahapur?” is also a statistical question. Prices change from month to month, so we need data from many months before we can make a statement about them.
