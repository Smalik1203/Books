1. Group project. Make groups of 8 to 10 students and choose at least one of these. Each of you collects data, and then the group puts everyone’s data together, studies it and shows it in pictures.
    1. Our heights and our families’ heights. Collect the heights of your family members. (i) Make a dot plot of your own family’s heights, and describe how they vary and where their centre lies. (ii) Make a double bar graph showing each student’s height beside the mean height of their family. (iii) Look at everyone’s data and share your observations.
    2. Estimating time. Check the time and close your eyes. Open them when you think 1 minute has passed, without counting, and note after how many seconds you opened them. Collect this data for yourself and your family members, and repeat it for 3 minutes. (i) Make two dot plots, for 1 minute and for 3 minutes, of your family’s estimates. (ii) Mark the mean and the median on each, and describe how the data varies and where its centre lies. (iii) Make a double bar graph of each family’s mean estimates for 1 minute and for 3 minutes. (iv) Look at everyone’s data and share your observations.
What this chapter showed
  1. A statistical question is one we answer by collecting data. A statistical statement sums up what data shows, in numbers.
  2. The arithmetic mean, or average, of some data is the sum of all the values divided by the number of values. It is the fair share each would get if the total were shared equally.
  3. The median is the middle value when the data is put in order. If there is an even number of values, the median is the average of the two middle values.
  4. An outlier is a value far away from the rest of the data. It pulls the mean towards itself, but moves the median very little.
  5. We can describe and compare data using its smallest and largest values, its total, the difference between its largest and smallest values, its mean and its median.
  6. A dot plot gives a quick picture of how data varies: its ends, and where it bunches together or spreads out.
  7. Clustered, or double, column and bar graphs compare values across groups and over time. To read one, first see what is given, and then draw conclusions.
  8. Looking closely at data can lead to new questions to explore.
