Are we on the same page?

Do you read a newspaper? Does it have the same number of pages every day of the week? One newspaper had 16, 18, 20, 22, 26, 16 and 10 pages from Monday to Sunday. Draw a dot plot of this data on a number line from 0 to 30, and mark the mean and the median on it.

Now look back at the three examples: the heights, the short stories and the newspaper pages. How is the data spread out when

  1. the mean and the median are close to each other?
  2. the mean and the median are far apart, and the mean is less than the median?
  3. the mean and the median are far apart, and the mean is greater than the median?

When the data is balanced, or spread out evenly, the mean and the median are close to each other. When there is an outlier at the lower end, it pulls the mean down, so the mean is less than the median. When there is an outlier at the higher end, it pulls the mean up, so the mean is greater than the median.

What happens to the mean and the median when there are outliers at both ends? Make up some data to test your answer, and explain what you find.

Measures of central tendency

The mean and the median are called measures of central tendency. The values in data tend to bunch around some central value, and these two numbers describe that centre of the data.

Of ends and the middle

We have seen that the mean and the median can give different views of the same data. When we study data, it is also useful to look at how much it varies, and at its two ends: its smallest and largest values.
