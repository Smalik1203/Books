So we can compare data by its smallest and largest values, its total, its average, and the difference between the largest and smallest. Can you think of other ways?

A picture of the data helps too. Fig. 5.1 shows one kind of picture, called a dot plot. Each price is a dot placed above its value on a number line. When a value occurs more than once, the dots are stacked one above another. A dot plot shows at a glance how the data varies, and where it bunches together.

Does the dot plot show all the data in Table 5.3? Can you read from it the price of onions in Yahapur in January?

A dot plot puts the values in order, but it loses the months they belonged to. In return, it lets us group the values in any way we like, as Vishal did. For example, Wahapur has 2 prices between 11 and 20, and Yahapur has none. We can see where the values bunch together and where they spread out: Wahapur’s prices are more spread out than Yahapur’s. The highest and lowest values are also easy to spot.

A statement such as “Onions cost ₹35 a kilogram” does not make us ask anything more. But prices that rise and fall through a year can make us curious. Do the seasons change the price of onions? Where are the two towns, and are they near each other? What decides the price of onions? How much do prices differ between shops in one area? Do other things we buy rise and fall in the same way? How do these changes affect farmers, buyers and businesses?
