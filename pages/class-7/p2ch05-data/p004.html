The average as a fair share

An average can also be read as a fair share: what each person gets when a total is shared equally. It is the number each would have if the total were shared out so that nobody had more than anyone else.

Shreyas and 4 of his friends collected 3, 8, 10, 5 and 4 guavas. Parag and 5 of his friends collected 5, 4, 6, 3, 4 and 8 guavas. Each group shares its guavas equally among its members. In which group does each member get more guavas? Make a guess before you work it out, and then check whether your guess was right.

First we find how many guavas each group collected. Then we share that total among the members of the group.

Shreyas’s group: $3 + 8 + 10 + 5 + 4 = 30$ guavas, and $30 \div 5 = 6$ guavas each

Parag’s group: $5 + 4 + 6 + 3 + 4 + 8 = 30$ guavas, and $30 \div 6 = 5$ guavas each

Both groups collected 30 guavas. But each member of Shreyas’s group gets 1 guava more than each member of Parag’s group, because Parag’s group has one more person to share with.

Example 1

Vaishnavi counts the hibiscus flowers that bloom in her garden each day. On five days she counts 2, 7, 9, 4 and 3 flowers. What is the average number of flowers that bloom in a day?

average $= \text{total number of flowers} \div \text{number of days}$

$\phantom{\text{average}} = (2 + 7 + 9 + 4 + 3) \div 5 = 25 \div 5 = 5$

On average, 5 hibiscus flowers bloom each day. That is the number that would bloom on each day if the same number bloomed every day.

Mathematicians in ancient India had several names for the arithmetic mean. Brahmagupta (628 CE) called it samarajju, the mean length of a line segment. Mahaviracharya (850 CE) called it samikarana, levelling or making equal. Sripati (1039 CE) used samya, equality. Bhaskaracharya (1150 CE) and Ganesha (1545 CE) used samamiti, mean measure, where sama means equal. These names show that Indian scholars thought of the mean as the common, levelling value that stands for a whole collection of values.
