Of ends and the middle

We have seen that the mean and the median can give different views of the same data. When we study data, it is also useful to look at how much it varies, and at its two ends: its smallest and largest values.

Suppose someone asks you, “How tall is your class?” What would you say? Table 5.4 gives the heights, in centimetres, of the students of a Class 5.

Table 5.4 Heights of the students of a Class 5 (cm)
Boys147, 135, 130, 154, 128, 135, 134, 158, 155, 146, 146, 142, 140, 141, 144, 145, 150
Girls143, 136, 150, 144, 154, 140, 145, 148, 156, 150, 150

We can show this data as a dot plot, find its ends and its patterns, and see how much it varies. We can also find its measures of central tendency. Fig. 5.3 shows a dot plot of the whole class, then one of the boys and one of the girls, each with its mean and median.

What can we learn from the dot plots and the measures of central tendency? These points help to answer the question of how tall the class is.

  • The boys’ heights are more spread out, from 128 cm to 158 cm. The girls’ heights lie between 136 cm and 156 cm. Both the tallest and the shortest students in the class are boys.
  • Yet the boys’ average height is less than the girls’ average, and less than the average of the whole class. So we can say that the girls in this class are taller than the boys. Of course, this does not mean that every girl is taller than every boy!
  • For the boys, the mean is less than the median (142.94 against 144). This shows that the shorter boys pull the mean down a little. The same is true of the girls (146.91 against 148).
