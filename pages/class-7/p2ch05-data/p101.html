Using What You Know
The mean and the median, in questions that do not say how to use them.

The chapter showed how to find a mean and a median, and what an outlier does to each. What changes now is that the data is not all given: you have to work back from what is known.

The mean of five numbers is 12. Four of them are 10, 15, 8 and 14. What is the fifth number?

A mean of 12 for five numbers means that the five numbers add up to $5 \times 12 = 60$. The four we know add up to $10 + 15 + 8 + 14 = 47$. So the fifth number is $60 - 47 = 13$. We did not need to guess: the mean told us the total, and the total told us the missing number.

Asha’s mean mark in four tests is 70. What must she score in the fifth test to raise her mean to 72?

Four tests with a mean of 70 give a total of $4 \times 70 = 280$ marks. Five tests with a mean of 72 need a total of $5 \times 72 = 360$ marks. So she needs $360 - 280 = 80$ marks in the fifth test. Another way to see it: the fifth mark must reach the new mean, 72, and also make up 2 marks for each of the four earlier tests, so it is $72 + 4 \times 2 = 80$.

Every value in some data is increased by 5. What happens to the mean and to the median?

Try it with 3, 4 and 8, whose mean is 5 and median is 4. Adding 5 to each gives 8, 9 and 13, whose mean is 10 and median is 9. Both went up by 5. This happens every time: the total grows by 5 for each value, so the mean grows by 5; and the values stay in the same order, so the middle one is still the middle one, 5 larger.
