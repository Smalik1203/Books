In the 8th century, Indian mathematical ideas were translated into Arabic. They influenced a well-known mathematician, Al-Khwarizmi, who lived in what is now Iraq. Around 825 CE he wrote a book called Hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala, which means “calculation by restoring and balancing”. Restoring meant removing a subtracted term from one side of an equation by adding it to both sides. Balancing meant taking away equal terms from both sides. These are the moves we used in Section 7.2.

These ideas spread further. By the 12th century, Al-Khwarizmi’s book had been translated into Latin and taken to Europe, where it became very popular. The word al-jabr in its title gave us the word algebra, which we still use today.

Today we use letters of the alphabet for unknowns. From the time of Brahmagupta, Indian mathematicians used short syllables such as ya, ka, ni, pi and lo for different unknowns. Ya was short for yavat-tavat, meaning “as much as needed”. Others, such as ka and ni, were the first syllables of the names of colours: kalaka (black), nilaka (blue), and so on. A known number has a definite form, rupa, and was marked ru. So ya 2 means two of the unknown, which we write as $2x$, and ru 1 means the known number 1.

Example 14

The Bijaganita of Bhaskaracharya (1150 CE) gives this problem. One man has ₹300 and 6 horses. Another man has 10 horses and owes ₹100. The two men are equally rich, and every horse has the same price. What is the price of one horse?

Solution.

Let the price of one horse be ₹$x$. The first man has $300 + 6x$ rupees. The second has $10x - 100$ rupees, because he owes ₹100. They are equally rich.

Step 1 $300 + 6x = 10x - 100$
Step 2 $400 + 6x = 10x$ add 100 to both
Step 3 $400 = 4x$ subtract $6x$ from both
Step 4 $100 = x$ divide both by 4
Answer the price of one horse is ₹100
