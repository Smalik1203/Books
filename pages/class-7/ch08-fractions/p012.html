The beggar got $\frac{1}{1280}$ of a dramma, which is one cowrie shell, the coin of least value. That is Bhaskaracharya’s joke.

Around the 12th century, many kinds of money were used in the kingdoms of the Indian subcontinent: gold coins such as dinars, silver coins such as drammas, copper coins such as panas, and cowrie shells for the smallest amounts and for change. How much each was worth in the others changed from place to place and time to time. Suppose 1 gold dinar $=$ 12 silver drammas, 1 dramma $=$ 4 copper panas, and 1 pana $=$ 30 cowrie shells. Then 1 pana is $\frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{48}$ of a dinar. What fraction of a pana is 1 cowrie shell? What fraction of a dinar?

A little history

Fractions of every kind, such as $\frac{5}{7}$ as well as $\frac{1}{7}$, together with the four operations on them, were developed largely in India. The Sulbasutras used them, and Brahmagupta set out the general rules in 628 CE. Bhaskara II later explained division as multiplying by the reciprocal, and Bhaskara I, in 629 CE, described multiplying fractions by dividing a square into rectangles, as we did. Sridharacharya, Mahaviracharya and Prithudakasvami took the work further. It passed to mathematicians in the Arab world and North Africa, such as al-Hassar of Morocco around 1192 CE, and reached Europe, where it came into general use only around the 17th century.
