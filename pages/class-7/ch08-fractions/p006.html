The rule works for whole numbers too, since a whole number can be written as a fraction with denominator 1.

$3 \times \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{3}{1} \times \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{3 \times 3}{1 \times 4} = \dfrac{9}{4}$

$\dfrac{3}{5} \times 4 = \dfrac{3}{5} \times \dfrac{4}{1} = \dfrac{3 \times 4}{5 \times 1} = \dfrac{12}{5}$

Cancelling before multiplying

Find $\frac{12}{7} \times \frac{5}{24}$ in its lowest form. We could multiply first and simplify afterwards, but there is a shorter way. Write the product as one fraction, and notice that the numerator 12 and the denominator 24 have the common factor 12. Dividing the numerator and the denominator by the same number does not change a fraction, so we divide both by 12 before multiplying.

$\dfrac{12}{7} \times \dfrac{5}{24} = \dfrac{12 \times 5}{7 \times 24} = \dfrac{1 \times 5}{7 \times 2} = \dfrac{5}{14}$

Here is one more. In $\frac{14}{15} \times \frac{25}{42}$, the numbers 14 and 42 have the common factor 14, and 25 and 15 have the common factor 5.

$\dfrac{14}{15} \times \dfrac{25}{42} = \dfrac{14 \times 25}{15 \times 42} = \dfrac{1 \times 5}{3 \times 3} = \dfrac{5}{9}$

Dividing a numerator and a denominator by a common factor before multiplying is called cancelling the common factor. Reducing a fraction to its lowest terms, called apavartana, was so familiar in India that the Jaina scholar Umasvati, around 150 CE, used it as a comparison in a work of philosophy.
