3.3Algebraic Methods of Solving a Pair of Linear Equations

In the last section we solved pairs of linear equations graphically. The graphical method is not convenient when the solution has coordinates that are not integers, such as $\left(\sqrt{3}, 2\sqrt{7}\right)$, $(-1.75, 3.3)$ or $\left(\frac{4}{13}, \frac{1}{19}\right)$. It is very easy to make a mistake when reading such coordinates off a graph. Is there another way to find the solution? There are several algebraic methods, and we now study two of them.

The substitution method

Let us explain the substitution method with some examples.

Example 4

Solve this pair of equations by the substitution method: $7x - 15y = 2$ and $x + 2y = 3$.

Solution.

Step 1 $7x - 15y = 2$   (1)   and   $x + 2y = 3$   (2) name the equations
Step 2 $x = 3 - 2y$   (3) $x$ in terms of $y$, from (2)
Step 3 $7(3 - 2y) - 15y = 2$, that is, $21 - 14y - 15y = 2$ putting (3) in (1)
Step 4 $-29y = -19$, so $y = \dfrac{19}{29}$
Step 5 $x = 3 - 2 \times \dfrac{19}{29} = \dfrac{49}{29}$ putting $y$ in (3)
Answer $x = \frac{49}{29}$, $y = \frac{19}{29}$

You can check that these values satisfy both (1) and (2).

Here are the steps of the substitution method once more, so that you can follow them for any pair.

The substitution method

Step 1. From whichever equation is easier, write one variable, say $y$, in terms of the other, $x$.

Step 2. Put this value of $y$ in the other equation. This gives an equation in $x$ alone, which can be solved. Sometimes, as in Examples 6 and 7 below, you get a statement with no variable in it. If the statement is true, the pair has infinitely many solutions. If it is false, the pair has no solution.

Step 3. Put the value of $x$ from Step 2 into the equation from Step 1, to get the value of $y$.

The method is called the substitution method because we substitute, that is, put in, the value of one variable written in terms of the other.
