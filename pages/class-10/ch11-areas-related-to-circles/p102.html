The piece is the sector of the larger circle less the sector of the smaller one, and both have the same angle. So its area is ${\frac{60}{360} \times \pi \times 14^2 - \frac{60}{360} \times \pi \times 7^2 = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{22}{7} \times (196 - 49)}$, which is ${\frac{1}{6} \times \frac{22}{7} \times 147 = 77}$ cm2. Taking the common factor ${\frac{60}{360} \times \pi}$ out first leaves only ${196 - 49}$ to work out.

Chords AB and CD of a circle subtend $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ at the centre. Is CD's minor segment twice AB's?

The sector for CD is twice the sector for AB, but look at the triangles. If the radius is $r$, the triangle for AB is equilateral with side $r$, so its area is ${\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}r^2}$. For CD, the perpendicular from the centre is $\frac{r}{2}$ and the chord is $r\sqrt{3}$, as in Example 2 of the chapter, so its triangle has the same area, ${\frac{1}{2} \times r\sqrt{3} \times \frac{r}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}r^2}$. So the segment for CD is ${\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}r^2}$, while twice the segment for AB is ${2 \times \left(\frac{1}{6}\pi r^2 - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}r^2\right) = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}r^2}$. The answer is no: the segment for CD is more than twice as large, because only the sector doubled and the triangle did not.
