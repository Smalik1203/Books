For a distribution, the mean is 30 and the median is 32. Use the empirical relationship to estimate the mode.

From ${3\,\text{Median} = \text{Mode} + 2\,\text{Mean}}$, you get ${96 = \text{Mode} + 60}$, so the mode is about 36. The relationship holds only roughly, so this is an estimate and not the value the mode formula would give. It also tells you the order of the three: the mode is twice as far from the median as the mean is, on the other side. When the frequencies rise to one peak and fall away evenly, the three measures lie close together.

This is a more than type distribution of the marks of 40 students. Find the median class, and then the median.

Subtracting each count from the one before it gives the frequencies of 0 – 10, 10 – 20, 20 – 30, 30 – 40 and 40 – 50: 6, 9, 13, 8 and 4. Their less than cumulative frequencies are 6, 15, 28, 36 and 40. Here ${\frac{n}{2} = 20}$, and 28 is the first cumulative frequency greater than 20, so the median class is 20 – 30. Then the median is ${20 + \frac{20 - 15}{13} \times 10 = 23.85}$. A more than table holds the same information as a less than table, in the other direction. Either table gives the frequencies, so either gives the median.

The daily wages, in rupees, of the 7 people in a workshop are 500, 520, 520, 540, 560, 580 and 3000, the last being the owner's. Find the mean and the median. Which of the two describes a typical wage better?

The sum is 6220, so the mean is ${6220 \div 7 = 888.57}$. The median is the 4th wage, 540. Six of the seven people earn less than the mean, so the mean does not describe a typical wage; the median does. The median would still be 540 however large the owner's wage became, while the mean would go on rising with it. This is the chapter's point about extreme values, seen in seven numbers.
