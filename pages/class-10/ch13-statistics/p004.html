This way of finding the mean is called the direct method.

Tables 13.1 and 13.3 use the same data and the same formula, but the two means are different. Why, and which one is more accurate? The difference comes from assuming that each class is centred at its mid-point. The mean 59.3 is exact, and 62 is an approximate mean.

When the values of $x_i$ and $f_i$ are large, multiplying them takes a long time. So let us find a way to make the numbers smaller.

The $f_i$ cannot be changed, but each $x_i$ can be replaced by a smaller number. One way is to subtract the same number from every $x_i$.

First, choose one of the $x_i$ as the assumed mean, and call it $a$. The work is least if $a$ is an $x_i$ near the middle of $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$. Here you could choose ${a = 47.5}$ or ${a = 62.5}$. Let us choose ${a = 47.5}$.

Next, find the difference $d_i$ between each $x_i$ and $a$. This is the deviation of $x_i$ from $a$:

$$d_i = x_i - a = x_i - 47.5$$

Then multiply each $d_i$ by its $f_i$, and add all the products $f_id_i$. Table 13.4 shows the working.

From Table 13.4, the mean of the deviations is ${\bar{d} = \frac{\sum f_id_i}{\sum f_i}}$. How is $\bar{d}$ related to $\bar{x}$? Each $d_i$ was found by subtracting $a$ from $x_i$. So to get $\bar{x}$ back, you add $a$ to $\bar{d}$. Here is the algebra:

$\bar{d} = \dfrac{\sum f_id_i}{\sum f_i} = \dfrac{\sum f_i(x_i - a)}{\sum f_i} = \dfrac{\sum f_ix_i}{\sum f_i} - \dfrac{a\sum f_i}{\sum f_i} = \bar{x} - a$
