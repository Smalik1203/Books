Using What You Know

The chapter gave you a formula for each measure and a table to put into it. What changes now is that a question may hide a frequency, change every observation at once, or give you two measures and ask about the third. Often a careful look at the table does more than a long calculation.

Every class mark in Table 13.18 is increased by 10, and the frequencies stay the same. What is the new mean? What if every class mark is doubled instead?

Table 13.18 (repeated from Section 13.2, for this question)
Class interval10 – 2525 – 4040 – 5555 – 7070 – 8585 – 100Total
Number of students ($f_i$)23766630
Class mark ($x_i$)17.532.547.562.577.592.5
$f_ix_i$35.097.5332.5375.0465.0555.01860.0

Adding 10 to every $x_i$ adds ${10 \times \sum f_i}$ to $\sum f_ix_i$, and $\sum f_i$ does not change. So the mean goes up by exactly 10, from 62 to 72. Doubling every $x_i$ doubles $\sum f_ix_i$, so the mean doubles, to 124. This is why the assumed mean and step-deviation methods work: subtracting $a$ and dividing by $h$ change the mean in the same way, and ${\bar{x} = a + h\bar{u}}$ undoes both changes.

The mean of the distribution below is 50, and the total frequency is 20. Find $p$ and $q$.

Class interval0 – 2020 – 4040 – 6060 – 8080 – 100
Frequency2$p$7$q$3

The total gives ${2 + p + 7 + q + 3 = 20}$, so ${p + q = 8}$. For the mean, take ${a = 50}$ and ${h = 20}$, so the $u_i$ are $-2$, $-1$, 0, 1 and 2, and ${\sum f_iu_i = -4 - p + q + 6 = 2 - p + q}$. The mean is 50, which is $a$ itself, so ${\bar{u} = 0}$ and ${\sum f_iu_i = 0}$. So ${p - q = 2}$. Together with ${p + q = 8}$, this gives ${p = 5}$ and ${q = 3}$. Choosing $a$ equal to the given mean made one equation very short.
