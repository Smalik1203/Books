Now suppose your collection holds every prime there is. There are infinitely many primes. So if we combine them in every possible way, we get an infinite collection of numbers: all the primes, and all their possible products. Here is the question. Does this collection contain every composite number? Or could there be a composite number that is not a product of powers of primes? Think about it before you read on.

To answer this, let us go the other way and break numbers into primes. Take a large number, say 32760, and factorise it with a factor tree, as in Fig. 1.1.

Reading the boxed primes gives the factorisation of 32760, first as a product of primes and then with powers.

$32760 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 \times 13$

$32760 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 \times 13$

Now try another number, say 123456789. It can be written as $3^2 \times 3803 \times 3607$. Check for yourself that 3803 and 3607 are primes, and then try the same with a few natural numbers of your own.

Each step of a factor tree splits a number into a prime and the factor left over. The factors keep getting smaller, so the tree stops, and every branch ends in a prime.

It works every time. This leads us to a conjecture: every composite number can be written as a product of powers of primes. The conjecture is true. It is so basic to the study of integers that it is called the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic.

Theorem 1.1 · Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

Every composite number can be written as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.
