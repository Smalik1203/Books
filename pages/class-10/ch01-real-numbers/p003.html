A form of this theorem was probably first written down as Proposition 14 of Book IX of Euclid’s Elements, long before it got its present name. The first correct proof was given by Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855) in his book Disquisitiones Arithmeticae. Gauss is often called the Prince of Mathematicians. He is counted with Archimedes and Newton among the three greatest mathematicians of all time, and he made basic contributions to both mathematics and science.

The theorem says more than that a composite number can be factorised into primes. It says that the factorisation is unique. Apart from the order of the primes, there is one and only one way to write the number as a product of primes. So we take $2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7$ and $3 \times 5 \times 7 \times 2$ to be the same factorisation, and the same goes for any other order of these four primes. We can also say it like this: the prime factorisation of a natural number is unique, except for the order of its factors.

In general, we write a composite number $x$ as $x = p_1 p_2 \ldots p_n$, where $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$ are primes written in ascending order, that is, $p_1 \leq p_2 \leq \ldots \leq p_n$. When we put equal primes together, we get powers of primes. For example,

$$32760 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 \times 13 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 \times 7 \times 13$$

Once we agree to write the primes in ascending order, there is exactly one way to write the factorisation of a number. The theorem has many uses, both inside mathematics and in other fields. Let us look at some examples.

Example 1

Take the numbers $4^n$, where $n$ is a natural number. Is there any value of $n$ for which $4^n$ ends with the digit zero?

Suppose that $4^n$ ended with the digit zero for some $n$. Then it would be divisible by 10, and so by 5. That means the prime 5 would appear in the prime factorisation of $4^n$. But $4^n = (2^2)^n = 2^{2n}$, so the only prime in its factorisation is 2. By the uniqueness part of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, no other prime can appear. So there is no natural number $n$ for which $4^n$ ends with the digit zero.
