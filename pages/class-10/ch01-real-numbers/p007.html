We are now ready to prove that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. The proof uses a method called proof by contradiction. We assume the opposite of what we want to prove, and show that this assumption leads to something impossible.

Theorem 1.3

$\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

Proof. Let us assume the opposite, that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational. Then we can find integers $r$ and $s$ such that $\sqrt{2} = \frac{r}{s}$ and $s \neq 0$. If $r$ and $s$ have a common factor other than 1, we divide both of them by it. This gives $\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}$, where $a$ and $b$ are coprime. Now follow the steps.

1 $b\sqrt{2} = a$ multiplying both sides by $b$
2 $2b^2 = a^2$ squaring both sides
3 2 divides $a^2$, so 2 divides $a$ Theorem 1.2 with $p = 2$
4 $a = 2c$ for some integer $c$ from step 3
5 $2b^2 = 4c^2$, that is, $b^2 = 2c^2$ putting $2c$ for $a$ in step 2
6 2 divides $b^2$, so 2 divides $b$ Theorem 1.2 again

So $a$ and $b$ have 2 as a common factor. But this contradicts the fact that $a$ and $b$ have no common factor other than 1. The contradiction arose from our assumption that $\sqrt{2}$ is rational, so that assumption is false. So $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.

Example 5

Prove that $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.

Let us assume the opposite, that $\sqrt{3}$ is rational. Then we can find integers $a$ and $b$, with $b \neq 0$, such that $\sqrt{3} = \frac{a}{b}$. If $a$ and $b$ have a common factor other than 1, we can divide both by it, so we may take $a$ and $b$ to be coprime. Then $b\sqrt{3} = a$.

$3b^2 = a^2$   (squaring both sides and rearranging)

So $a^2$ is divisible by 3, and by Theorem 1.2, $a$ is divisible by 3.

$a = 3c$ for some integer $c$, so $3b^2 = 9c^2$, that is, $b^2 = 3c^2$

So $b^2$ is divisible by 3, and by Theorem 1.2 with $p = 3$, $b$ is divisible by 3.
