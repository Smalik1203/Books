Using What You Know

The chapter gave you two tools: the unique prime factorisation of a number, and proof by contradiction. What changes now is that a question does not tell you which tool it needs, and some questions ask about every number of a kind rather than one number.

The HCF of two numbers is 18 and their product is 12960. What is their LCM? Can two numbers have HCF 18 and LCM 380?

For two numbers, $\text{HCF} \times \text{LCM}$ is their product, so the LCM is $12960 \div 18 = 720$. For the second question, think about what the HCF and LCM are. The HCF divides each number, and each number divides the LCM, so the HCF must divide the LCM. Now $18 = 2 \times 3^2$, but $380 = 2^2 \times 5 \times 19$ has no factor 3, so 18 does not divide 380. So no two numbers have HCF 18 and LCM 380. We did not need to try a single pair of numbers to be sure.

Find the largest number that divides 1251, 9377 and 15628, leaving remainders 1, 2 and 3 respectively.

If a number leaves remainder 1 when it divides 1251, then it divides $1251 - 1 = 1250$ exactly. In the same way, it divides $9377 - 2 = 9375$ and $15628 - 3 = 15625$. So we want the largest common factor of 1250, 9375 and 15625, which is their HCF. Now ${1250 = 2 \times 5^4}$, $9375 = 3 \times 5^5$ and $15625 = 5^6$. The only common prime is 5, and its smallest power is $5^4$. So the number is 625. Taking the remainders away turned the question into an HCF question.

What is the smallest four-digit number that leaves a remainder of 5 when it is divided by 12, by 18 and by 30?

Take 5 away from such a number. What is left is divisible by 12, 18 and 30, so it is a common multiple of the three numbers, and so a multiple of their LCM. Since ${12 = 2^2 \times 3}$, $18 = 2 \times 3^2$ and $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$, the LCM is $2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 180$. The number we want is 5 more than a multiple of 180. Now $900 + 5 = 905$ has only three digits, and the next multiple of 180 is 1080. So the answer is $1080 + 5 = 1085$.
