From Fig. 2.1 you can see that the graph of $y = 2x + 3$ cuts the $x$-axis half-way between $x = -2$ and $x = -1$, that is, at the point $\left(-\frac{3}{2}, 0\right)$. You also know that the zero of $2x + 3$ is $-\frac{3}{2}$. So the zero of the polynomial $2x + 3$ is the $x$-coordinate of the point where the graph of $y = 2x + 3$ cuts the $x$-axis.

You can see why this must happen. A point lies on the $x$-axis exactly when its $y$-coordinate is 0. So the line $y = 2x + 3$ meets the $x$-axis at the point where $2x + 3 = 0$, that is, at the point whose $x$-coordinate is the zero of $2x + 3$.

In general, for a linear polynomial $ax + b$ with $a \neq 0$, the graph of $y = ax + b$ is a straight line that cuts the $x$-axis at exactly one point, namely $\left(-\frac{b}{a}, 0\right)$. So the linear polynomial $ax + b$ has exactly one zero: the $x$-coordinate of the point where the graph of $y = ax + b$ cuts the $x$-axis.
