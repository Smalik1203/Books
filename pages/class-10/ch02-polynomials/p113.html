9 $(\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha\beta = 49 - 20 = 29$. Option (a) adds $2\alpha\beta$, (c) leaves it out, and (d) takes away $\alpha\beta$ only once.
10 $\frac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha\beta} = \frac{-7}{12}$. Option (a) loses the sign of the sum, and (b) divides the product by the sum.
13 $-\frac{d}{a} = -\frac{6}{2} = -3$; the zeroes are 2, $-3$ and $\frac{1}{2}$. Option (a) forgets the minus sign, and (b) is $\frac{c}{a}$.
15 The sum is $-\frac{2}{k}$ and the product is $\frac{3k}{k} = 3$, so $-\frac{2}{k} = 3$. Option (a) loses the minus sign of the sum.
19 $x^3 - x = x(x - 1)(x + 1)$ has the zeroes 0, 1 and $-1$, so A is true. But $x^3$ has only the zero 0, so R is false: the answer is (c).
20 The product is $\frac{-6}{1} = -6$, not 6, so A is false while R is true: the answer is (d).

Three things from this section are worth keeping. An expression in the zeroes, such as $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$ or $\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}$, can often be written using only $\alpha + \beta$ and $\alpha\beta$, so the zeroes themselves are not needed. The minus sign belongs to the sum of the zeroes and not to their product. And the degree of a polynomial limits how many times its graph can meet the $x$-axis.
