Using What You Know

The chapter showed you what the zeroes of a polynomial mean on a graph, and how the sum and product of the zeroes come from the coefficients. What changes now is that a question may ask about the zeroes without asking you to find them.

The zeroes of $x^2 - 5x + 6$ are $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Find $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$.

From the coefficients, $\alpha + \beta = 5$ and $\alpha\beta = 6$. The identity $(\alpha + \beta)^2 = \alpha^2 + 2\alpha\beta + \beta^2$ gives $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha\beta = 25 - 12 = 13$. The zeroes are in fact 2 and 3, and $4 + 9 = 13$, but we did not need to find them.

One zero of $x^2 + kx - 12$ is 3. Find the other zero and the value of $k$.

The product of the zeroes is $\frac{-12}{1} = -12$, so the other zero is $-12 \div 3 = -4$. The sum of the zeroes is $3 + (-4) = -1$, and it is also $-\frac{k}{1} = -k$. So $k = 1$. Check: $3^2 + 1 \times 3 - 12 = 0$.

Riya says the graph of a cubic polynomial could cut the $x$-axis at four points. Is she right?

Each point where the graph of $y = p(x)$ cuts the $x$-axis gives a zero of $p(x)$. A cubic polynomial has at most three zeroes, so its graph meets the $x$-axis at no more than three points. So Riya is not right. A graph that cuts the $x$-axis at four points belongs to a polynomial of degree 4 or more.

Find a quadratic polynomial whose zeroes are $1 + \sqrt{5}$ and $1 - \sqrt{5}$.

We do not need to factorise anything. The sum of the zeroes is $(1 + \sqrt{5}) + (1 - \sqrt{5}) = 2$, and the product is $(1 + \sqrt{5})(1 - \sqrt{5}) = 1 - 5 = -4$. So one such polynomial is ${x^2 - 2x - 4}$.
