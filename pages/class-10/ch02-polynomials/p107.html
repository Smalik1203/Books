B2 $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha\beta = 49 - 20 = 29$: (b). Option (a) adds $2\alpha\beta$ instead of taking it away, and (c) forgets it.
B3 $\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta} = \frac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha\beta} = \frac{-5}{6}$: (d). Option (a) loses the sign of the sum, and (b) divides the product by the sum.
B4 The product of the zeroes is $\frac{c}{a} = 1$, so $c = a$: (c). Options (a), (b) and (d) do not follow from the product being 1.
C1 The sum is 6 and the product is $9 - 2 = 7$, so the polynomial is $x^2 - 6x + 7$: (b). Option (a) has the sign of the sum wrong, (c) the sign of the product, and (d) adds where it should subtract.
C2 $\alpha\beta\gamma = -\frac{d}{a} = -\frac{6}{2} = -3$: (c). The zeroes are 2, $-3$ and $\frac{1}{2}$. Option (a) forgets the minus sign, and (b) is $\frac{c}{a}$.
C3 $p(x) = 0$ only when $x = 1$ or $x = -2$, so there are two zeroes: (b). The factor $x - 1$ appears twice but gives the same zero, and the graph only touches the axis there.
C4 The sum is $-\frac{2}{k}$ and the product is $\frac{3k}{k} = 3$, so $-\frac{2}{k} = 3$ and $k = -\frac{2}{3}$: (d). Option (a) loses the minus sign of the sum.

Three things from this section are worth keeping. An expression in the zeroes, such as $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$ or $\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}$, can often be written using only $\alpha + \beta$ and $\alpha\beta$, so the zeroes themselves are not needed. The minus sign belongs to the sum of the zeroes and not to their product. And the degree of a polynomial limits how many times its graph can meet the $x$-axis.
