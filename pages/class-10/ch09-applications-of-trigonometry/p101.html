Using What You Know

The chapter showed you how to find a height or a distance: draw a right triangle, then choose a ratio. What changes now is that a question may give you no length at all, or may be solved more quickly by looking at the angles first.

The shadow of a vertical pole on level ground is $\sqrt{3}$ times as long as the pole. Find the angle of elevation of the Sun.

Let the pole be $h$ m tall, so its shadow is ${h\sqrt{3}}$ m long. The pole, its shadow and the line from the tip of the shadow to the top of the pole make a right triangle, and the Sun's angle of elevation $\theta$ is at the tip of the shadow. So ${\tan\theta = \frac{h}{h\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}}$, and from Table 8.1, ${\theta = 30^\circ}$. The height $h$ cancelled out: the angle depends only on the ratio of the two lengths, not on the lengths themselves. In the same way, a pole whose shadow is exactly as long as the pole gives ${\tan\theta = 1}$, and so an angle of $45^\circ$.

Look again at Example 5. The angles of elevation of the top A of the tower from D and from C are $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$, and ${DC = 40}$ m. Find the height of the tower without writing any equation in $x$.

In $\triangle ADC$, the angle ACB is an exterior angle, so it is equal to the sum of the two interior opposite angles: ${\angle ACB = \angle ADC + \angle DAC}$. That is, ${60^\circ = 30^\circ + \angle DAC}$, so ${\angle DAC = 30^\circ}$. The triangle ADC has two equal angles, at D and at A, so the sides opposite them are equal: ${CA = CD = 40}$ m. Now the right triangle ABC gives ${AB = CA \sin 60^\circ = 40 \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 20\sqrt{3}}$ m, the answer of Example 5. Whenever the second angle of elevation is twice the first, this triangle is isosceles, and the distance walked is equal to the second line of sight.

From the top of a building 14 m high, the angle of depression of a car on the road is $30^\circ$. What is the angle of elevation of the top of the building, seen from the car? How far is the car from the building?

The horizontal line through the top of the building and the road are parallel, and the line of sight from the top to the car is a transversal to them. So the angle of depression at the top and the angle of elevation at the car are alternate angles, and they are equal: both are $30^\circ$. This is what Example 6 used. If the car is $d$ m from the foot of the building, then ${\tan 30^\circ = \frac{14}{d}}$, so ${d = 14\sqrt{3}}$, about 24.25 m. An angle of depression can always be moved to the object, where it becomes an angle of elevation inside the right triangle.
