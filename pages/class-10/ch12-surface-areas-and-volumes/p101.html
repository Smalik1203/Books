Using What You Know

The chapter gave you two rules: add the surfaces that can still be seen, and add the volumes. What changes now is that a question may give the whole solid and ask about one part, change its size, or ask which way an area moves when a piece is cut away. The answers often need no value of $\pi$ at all.

A cube of edge 2 cm is placed on the middle of the top face of a cube of edge 4 cm. Find the surface area of the new solid.

The small cube hides 4 cm2 of the top of the large cube, and its own bottom face, also 4 cm2, is hidden. So the surface area is ${6 \times 16 + 6 \times 4 - 2 \times 4 = 96 + 24 - 8 = 112}$ cm2. You can see it more quickly from above: the top of the solid still shows 16 cm2, just as the large cube did. So the area is the large cube's 96 cm2 plus the four side faces of the small cube, ${4 \times 4 = 16}$ cm2, which again gives 112 cm2.

A cone, a hemisphere and a cylinder all have radius $r$, and the cone and the cylinder both have height $r$. Compare their volumes.

The cone has volume ${\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 \times r = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^3}$, the hemisphere ${\frac{2}{3}\pi r^3}$ and the cylinder ${\pi r^2 \times r = \pi r^3}$. So their volumes are in the ratio ${1 : 2 : 3}$. The cone and the hemisphere together make exactly the cylinder. This is why, in Example 7 of the chapter, the space left inside the cylinder was equal to the volume of the toy.

A solid is a cylinder of radius 3 cm and height 10 cm, with a cone of radius 3 cm on top. The volume of the whole solid is $96\pi$ cm3. How tall is the cone?

Volumes add, so the cone holds whatever the cylinder does not. The cylinder holds ${\pi \times 3^2 \times 10 = 90\pi}$, so the cone holds ${96\pi - 90\pi = 6\pi}$. A cone of radius 3 cm and height $h$ holds ${\frac{1}{3}\pi \times 3^2 \times h = 3\pi h}$. So ${3\pi h = 6\pi}$, and ${h = 2}$ cm. The $\pi$ on both sides cancelled, so no value of $\pi$ was needed.
