Quadratic Equations

4.1Introduction

In Chapter 2 you studied different kinds of polynomials. One kind was the quadratic polynomial $ax^2 + bx + c$, where $a \neq 0$. When we set such a polynomial equal to zero, we get a quadratic equation. Quadratic equations come up in many real-life situations.

For example, suppose a charity trust decides to build a prayer hall. The floor of the hall is to have an area of 300 square metres, and its length is to be one metre more than twice its breadth. What should the length and breadth of the hall be?

Let the breadth of the hall be $x$ metres. Then its length is $(2x + 1)$ metres. Fig. 4.1 shows this information in a picture.

area of the hall $= (2x + 1) \times x = 2x^2 + x$ square metres

$2x^2 + x = 300$   (given)

$2x^2 + x - 300 = 0$

So the breadth of the hall must satisfy the equation $2x^2 + x - 300 = 0$, which is a quadratic equation.

Many people believe that the Babylonians were the first to solve quadratic equations. For instance, they knew how to find two positive numbers with a given sum and a given product, and this problem is the same as solving the equation $x^2 - px + q = 0$ for a suitable $p$ and $q$. The Greek mathematician Euclid found lengths by a method of drawing, and in today’s language those lengths are solutions of quadratic equations.

Solving quadratic equations in their general form is often credited to ancient Indian mathematicians. Brahmagupta (598–665 CE) gave a formula for solving a quadratic equation of the form $ax^2 + bx = c$. Later, Sridharacharya (1025 CE) found the formula now called the quadratic formula, as quoted by Bhaskara II, by a method called completing the square. The Arab mathematician Al-Khwarizmi (about 800 CE) also studied quadratic equations of different kinds, and Abraham bar Hiyya Ha-Nasi gave full solutions of different quadratic equations in his book Liber embadorum, published in Europe in 1145 CE.
