Check that ${P(W) + P(B) + P(R) = 1}$.

Example 9

Harpreet tosses two different coins at the same time, say a ₹1 coin and a ₹2 coin. What is the probability that she gets at least one head?

Write H for head and T for tail. When the two coins are tossed together, the possible outcomes are (H, H), (H, T), (T, H) and (T, T), and they are all equally likely. Here (H, H) means a head on the first coin (the ₹1 coin) and a head on the second coin (the ₹2 coin); (H, T) means a head on the first coin and a tail on the second, and so on. The outcomes favourable to the event E, at least one head, are (H, H), (H, T) and (T, H). (Why?) So

$P(E) = \dfrac{3}{4}$

that is, the probability that Harpreet gets at least one head is $\frac{3}{4}$.

Note. You can also find P(E) from its complement. The event $\overline{E}$ is no head, which has one outcome, (T, T). So ${P(E) = 1 - P(\overline{E}) = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}}$.

In every example so far, the number of possible outcomes was finite. Check this now if you did not notice it.

In many experiments the outcome can be any number between two given numbers, or any point inside a circle or a rectangle. Can you count all the possible outcomes then? No: there are infinitely many numbers between two numbers, and infinitely many points inside a circle. So the definition of probability you have learnt cannot be used as it stands. What can be done? Look at the next example.
