Example 9

Harpreet tosses two different coins at the same time, say a ₹1 coin and a ₹2 coin. What is the probability that she gets at least one head?

Solution.

Step 1 H is head and T is tail; (H, H), (H, T), (T, H), (T, T) are equally likely
Step 2 (H, H) means a head on the first coin (the ₹1 coin) and on the second (the ₹2 coin); (H, T) is a head on the first and a tail on the second
Step 3 for E, at least one head, (H, H), (H, T), (T, H) are favourable each has a head
Answer $P(E) = \dfrac{3}{4}$, so the probability of at least one head is $\frac{3}{4}$

Note. You can also find P(E) from its complement. The event $\overline{E}$ is no head, which has one outcome, (T, T). So ${P(E) = 1 - P(\overline{E}) = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}}$.

In every example so far, the number of possible outcomes was finite. Check this now if you did not notice it.

In many experiments the outcome can be any number between two given numbers, or any point inside a circle or a rectangle. Can you count all the possible outcomes then? No: there are infinitely many numbers between two numbers, and infinitely many points inside a circle. So the definition of probability you have learnt cannot be used as it stands. What can be done? Look at the next example.

Example 10

In a game of musical chairs, the person playing the music is told to stop it at any moment within 2 minutes of starting. What is the probability that the music stops within the first half-minute?

This example and the next are not needed for the examination.

Solution.

Step 1 the outcomes are the numbers between 0 and 2, the line from 0 to 2 (Fig. 14.1)
Step 2 let E be the event the music stops within the first half-minute; the outcomes favourable to E are the points of the number line from 0 to $\frac{1}{2}$
Step 3 the length from 0 to 2 is 2, and the length from 0 to $\frac{1}{2}$ is $\frac{1}{2}$; so of the total length 2, a length of $\frac{1}{2}$ is favourable to E all outcomes equally likely
Answer $P(E) = \dfrac{\text{length favourable to } E}{\text{total length in which the outcomes can lie}} = \dfrac{\frac{1}{2}}{2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$
