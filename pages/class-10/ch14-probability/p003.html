Remark 2. In Example 1, $P(E) + P(F) = 1$, and in Example 2, $P(Y) + P(R) + P(B) = 1$. The probabilities of all the elementary events of an experiment always add up to 1.

A note on history. Probability theory began in the sixteenth century, when the Italian physician and mathematician J. Cardan wrote the first book on it, The Book on Games of Chance. Since then it has drawn the attention of great mathematicians: James Bernoulli (1654–1705), A. de Moivre (1667–1754) and Laplace all added a great deal to it. Laplace's Théorie Analytique des Probabilités (1812) is thought of as the greatest contribution by one person to the subject. Today probability is used widely, in biology, economics, genetics, physics, sociology and many other fields.

Example 3

A die is thrown once. What is the probability of getting (i) a number greater than 4? (ii) a number less than or equal to 4?

Solution.

Step 1 there are six possible outcomes, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6
Step 2 (i) let E be getting a number greater than 4; 5 and 6 are favourable to E
Step 3 $P(E) = P(\text{number greater than } 4) = \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}$
Step 4 (ii) let F be getting a number less than or equal to 4; of the 6 outcomes, 1, 2, 3 and 4 are favourable
Step 5 $P(F) = \dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3}$
Answer (i) $\dfrac{1}{3}$   (ii) $\dfrac{2}{3}$

Are E and F elementary events? No: E has 2 outcomes and F has 4.

Look again at Example 1, where E is getting a head and F is getting a tail, and at Example 3, where E is getting a number greater than 4 and F is getting a number less than or equal to 4.

${P(E) + P(F) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1}$   (1)

${P(E) + P(F) = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{3} = 1}$   (2)

Getting a number that is not greater than 4 is the same as getting a number less than or equal to 4. So in both (1) and (2), F is the event not E. We write the event not E as $\overline{E}$. So ${P(E) + P(\overline{E}) = 1}$, which gives ${P(\overline{E}) = 1 - P(E)}$.
