Empirical probability can be found for any event of an experiment that can be repeated many times. But repeating an experiment has limits: in many situations it is very costly, or cannot be done at all. It worked well for tossing coins and throwing dice. But you cannot launch a satellite again and again to find the empirical probability that a launch fails, or wait for earthquakes to find the empirical probability that a tall building falls in one.

In experiments where we are ready to make some assumptions, we need not repeat the experiment: the assumptions let us work out the exact, or theoretical, probability directly. One such assumption is that the outcomes are equally likely, which holds in many experiments, such as tossing a coin or throwing a die. It leads to the following definition.

Theoretical probability

The theoretical probability, also called the classical probability, of an event E is written P(E) and defined as

$$P(E) = \dfrac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{number of all possible outcomes of the experiment}}$$

where the outcomes of the experiment are assumed to be equally likely.

From here on, probability means theoretical probability. This definition was given by the French mathematician Pierre Simon Laplace (1749–1827) in 1795.

Let us find the probabilities of some events of experiments whose outcomes are equally likely.

Example 1

A coin is tossed once. Find the probability of getting a head, and the probability of getting a tail.

When a coin is tossed once there are two possible outcomes, head (H) and tail (T). Let E be the event getting a head. One outcome is favourable to E. So

$P(E) = P(\text{head}) = \dfrac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{number of all possible outcomes}} = \dfrac{1}{2}$

In the same way, if F is the event getting a tail, then ${P(F) = P(\text{tail}) = \frac{1}{2}}$. (Why?)
