Theoretical probability

The theoretical probability, also called the classical probability, of an event E is written P(E) and defined as

$$P(E) = \dfrac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{number of all possible outcomes of the experiment}}$$

where the outcomes of the experiment are assumed to be equally likely.

From here on, probability means theoretical probability. This definition was given by the French mathematician Pierre Simon Laplace (1749–1827) in 1795.

Let us find the probabilities of some events of experiments whose outcomes are equally likely.

Example 1

A coin is tossed once. Find the probability of getting a head, and the probability of getting a tail.

Solution.

Step 1 there are two possible outcomes, head (H) and tail (T)
Step 2 let E be the event getting a head; one outcome is favourable to E
Step 3 $P(E) = P(\text{head}) = \dfrac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to } E}{\text{number of all possible outcomes}} = \dfrac{1}{2}$
Step 4 if F is the event getting a tail, then ${P(F) = P(\text{tail}) = \frac{1}{2}}$ one outcome of two
Answer $P(\text{head}) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $P(\text{tail}) = \frac{1}{2}$
Example 2

A bag contains a red, a blue and a yellow ball, all of the same size. Kritika takes out a ball without looking. What is the probability that it is (i) yellow? (ii) red? (iii) blue?

Solution.

Step 1 each ball is equally likely to be taken out, so there are 3 possible outcomes Kritika does not look
Step 2 let Y, B and R be the events the ball is yellow, the ball is blue and the ball is red
Step 3 (i) one outcome is favourable to Y, so $P(Y) = \dfrac{1}{3}$
Step 4 (ii) in the same way, $P(R) = \dfrac{1}{3}$   and   (iii) $P(B) = \dfrac{1}{3}$
Answer each colour has probability $\dfrac{1}{3}$

Remark 1. An event that consists of only one outcome of an experiment is called an elementary event. In Example 1 the events E and F are elementary events, and in Example 2 so are Y, B and R.
