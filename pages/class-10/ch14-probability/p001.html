Probability

The American astronomer R. S. Woodward once described the theory of probabilities, together with the theory of errors, as a large body of mathematics of great interest and of great practical use. In this chapter you will meet the first part of that theory.

14.1Probability — A Theoretical Approach

Look at this situation: a coin is tossed at random.

When we speak of a coin, we assume that it is fair: it is symmetrical, so there is no reason for it to land on one side more often than on the other. We also call such a coin unbiased. A random toss means that the coin falls freely, with nothing to push it one way or the other.

We know in advance that the coin can land in only two ways, head up or tail up. (We ignore the chance that it lands on its edge, which could happen if it falls on sand, for example.) It is reasonable to assume that a head is as likely as a tail. We say that the outcomes head and tail are equally likely.

Here is another example. Suppose a die is thrown once. In this book a die always means a fair die. The possible outcomes are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, and each number has the same chance of showing. So the six outcomes of throwing a die are equally likely.

Are the outcomes of every experiment equally likely? Suppose a bag holds 4 red balls and 1 blue ball, and you take out a ball without looking. Are the outcomes a red ball and a blue ball equally likely? There are 4 red balls and only 1 blue ball, so you are more likely to get a red ball. These two outcomes are not equally likely. But each of the five balls is as likely to be drawn as any other. So not every experiment has equally likely outcomes. From now on in this chapter, however, we assume that the outcomes of every experiment are equally likely.

In Class 9 you defined the experimental, or empirical, probability of an event E as

$$P(E) = \dfrac{\text{number of trials in which the event happened}}{\text{total number of trials}}$$
