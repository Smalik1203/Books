Theorem 10.1

The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.

Proof. We are given a circle with centre O and a tangent XY to the circle at a point P. We need to prove that OP is perpendicular to XY. Take a point Q on XY other than P, and join OQ (Fig. 10.5).

The point Q must lie outside the circle. (Why? If Q were inside the circle, XY would be a secant and not a tangent.) So OQ is longer than the radius OP, that is,

${OQ \gt OP}$

This holds for every point Q of XY other than P. So OP is the shortest of all the distances from O to the points of XY. Draw the perpendicular OM from O to XY. If M were a point other than P, then OP would be the hypotenuse of the right triangle OMP, and so longer than OM, which cannot be. So M is P, and OP is perpendicular to XY.

Remark 1. From this theorem, there is one and only one tangent at any point of a circle: it is the line through the point that is perpendicular to the radius there.

Remark 2. The line containing the radius through the point of contact is sometimes called the normal to the circle at that point.
