A tangent and a secant

A tangent to a circle is a special case of a secant: the case in which the two end points of its chord coincide.

Activity 2. On a sheet of paper, draw a circle and a secant PQ of the circle. Draw several lines parallel to the secant, on both sides of it. You will find that the chords cut off by these lines get shorter as the lines move away from PQ: the two points where a line meets the circle come closer together [Fig. 10.3 (ii)]. On each side of PQ, the chord at last shrinks to a single point. These are the positions P′Q′ and P″Q″ in Fig. 10.3 (ii), and they are the tangents to the circle that are parallel to PQ. This also shows that there cannot be more than two tangents parallel to a given secant. Try it again with a secant that passes through the centre of the circle.

This activity shows again what you saw in Activity 1: a tangent is a secant whose chord has shrunk to a point, with its two end points coinciding.

The common point of a tangent and the circle is called the point of contact (the point A in Fig. 10.1 (iii)), and we say that the tangent touches the circle at that point.

Now look around you. Have you seen a bicycle or a cart moving? Look at its wheels. The spokes of a wheel lie along its radii. Where the wheel meets the ground, do you see a tangent? (See Fig. 10.4.) The wheel moves along a line that is a tangent to the circle of the wheel. Notice also that in every position, the radius through the point of contact with the ground seems to be at right angles to the ground. We shall now prove this property of the tangent.
