Join OT, and let it meet PQ at the point R. The triangle TPQ is isosceles, since ${TP = TQ}$, and TO bisects $\angle PTQ$ (Remark 2 after Theorem 10.2). In an isosceles triangle, the bisector of the angle between the equal sides is perpendicular to the base and bisects it. You proved this fact in Class 9, using congruent triangles. So ${OT \perp PQ}$, and ${PR = RQ = 4}$ cm. Also

${OR = \sqrt{OP^2 - PR^2} = \sqrt{5^2 - 4^2} = 3}$ cm

Now ${\angle TPR + \angle RPO = 90^\circ}$, by Theorem 10.1, and ${\angle TPR + \angle PTR = 90^\circ}$, because these are the two acute angles of the right triangle TRP. So ${\angle RPO = \angle PTR}$, and the right triangle TRP is similar to the right triangle PRO, by AA similarity, since the angles at R are both right angles. This gives

$\dfrac{TP}{PO} = \dfrac{RP}{RO}$,   that is,   $\dfrac{TP}{5} = \dfrac{4}{3}$,   so   $TP = \dfrac{20}{3}$ cm

Note. TP can also be found with the Pythagoras theorem. This takes longer, but needs no similar triangles. Let ${TP = x}$ and ${TR = y}$. Then

${x^2 = y^2 + 16}$   (right triangle PRT)   (1)

${x^2 + 5^2 = (y + 3)^2}$   (right triangle OPT)   (2)

subtracting (1) from (2):   ${25 = 6y - 7}$,   so   $y = \dfrac{32}{6} = \dfrac{16}{3}$

$x^2 = \left(\dfrac{16}{3}\right)^2 + 16 = \dfrac{16(16 + 9)}{9} = \dfrac{16 \times 25}{9}$   [from (1)],   so   $x = \dfrac{20}{3}$
