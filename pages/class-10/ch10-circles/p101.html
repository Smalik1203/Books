Using What You Know

The chapter gave you two facts about tangents: the radius to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent, and the two tangents from an outside point are equal. What changes now is that you have to find where to use them, and often draw the radius or the line to the centre yourself.

A point P is 13 cm from the centre O of a circle of radius 5 cm. The tangents from P touch the circle at A and B. Find the length of the chord AB.

OA is perpendicular to PA, so ${PA = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 12}$ cm, and PB is 12 cm too. The quadrilateral OAPB is made of the two right triangles OAP and OBP, each of area ${\frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 12 = 30}$ cm2, so its area is 60 cm2. Its diagonals OP and AB are perpendicular, because OP bisects the angle APB of the isosceles triangle APB. So its area is also ${\frac{1}{2} \times OP \times AB = \frac{13}{2}AB}$. Then ${\frac{13}{2}AB = 60}$, and ${AB = \frac{120}{13}}$ cm. Working out one area in two ways gave a length that is hard to reach directly.

A circle touches the sides AB, BC and CA of a triangle ABC, where ${AB = 12}$ cm, ${BC = 8}$ cm and ${CA = 10}$ cm. Find the lengths of the tangents from A, B and C to the circle.

Let the tangents from A, B and C be $a$, $b$ and $c$ cm long. Each side is made of two tangents, one from each of its ends, so ${a + b = 12}$, ${b + c = 8}$ and ${c + a = 10}$. Adding the three equations gives ${2(a + b + c) = 30}$, so ${a + b + c = 15}$. Then ${a = 15 - 8 = 7}$, ${b = 15 - 10 = 5}$ and ${c = 15 - 12 = 3}$. Each length is half the perimeter less the opposite side. The radius of the circle was never needed.

A quadrilateral ABCD has ${AB = 5}$ cm, ${BC = 7}$ cm, ${CD = 9}$ cm and ${DA = 6}$ cm. Can a circle touch all four of its sides?

Suppose it could. By Question 8 of Exercise Set 10.2, a quadrilateral circumscribing a circle has ${AB + CD = AD + BC}$. Here ${AB + CD = 5 + 9 = 14}$ cm, but ${AD + BC = 6 + 7 = 13}$ cm. So the answer is no.
