10.2Tangent to a Circle

In the previous section you saw that a tangent to a circle is a line that meets the circle in only one point. The word tangent comes from the Latin word tangere, which means to touch. It was brought into mathematics by the Danish mathematician Thomas Fincke in 1583.

Does a circle have a tangent at each of its points? Try these activities to find out.

Activity 1. Take a circular wire, and fix a straight wire AB to it at a point P so that AB can turn about P in the plane of the circle. Lay the two wires on a table and slowly turn AB about P, as in Fig. 10.3.

In most positions, the straight wire meets the circular wire at P and at one more point, such as Q1 or Q2 on one side, or R1 or R2 on the other. In just one position, A′B′, it meets the circle at P only. So a tangent to the circle exists at P. Turn the wire a little either way from A′B′, and it meets the circle at a second point again. So there is only one tangent at a point of the circle.

As AB turns towards the position A′B′, its second common point with the circle, say Q1, moves closer and closer to P, until in the position A′B′ it coincides with P. The same happens when AB is turned the other way: the point R1 comes closer to P and at last coincides with it. So we see the following.

A tangent and a secant

A tangent to a circle is a special case of a secant: the case in which the two end points of its chord coincide.
