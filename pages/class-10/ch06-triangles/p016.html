Then ${\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{BC}{EF} = \frac{CA}{FD}}$, each equal to $\frac{2}{3}$. Now measure $\angle A$, $\angle B$, $\angle C$, $\angle D$, $\angle E$ and $\angle F$. You will find that $\angle A = \angle D$, $\angle B = \angle E$ and $\angle C = \angle F$, so the corresponding angles of the two triangles are equal. Repeat this with several pairs of triangles whose sides are in the same ratio. Each time, their corresponding angles are equal. This is because of the following criterion.

Theorem 6.4

If in two triangles the sides of one triangle are proportional to (that is, in the same ratio as) the sides of the other triangle, then their corresponding angles are equal, and so the two triangles are similar.

This criterion is called the SSS (Side–Side–Side) similarity criterion for two triangles.

It can be proved by taking two triangles ABC and DEF such that ${\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{BC}{EF} = \frac{CA}{FD}}$, with this ratio less than 1 (Fig. 6.26). Mark P on DE and Q on DF such that DP = AB and DQ = AC, and join PQ.

$\dfrac{DP}{PE} = \dfrac{DQ}{QF}$ and $PQ \parallel EF$   (How?)

so $\angle P = \angle E$ and $\angle Q = \angle F$

so $\dfrac{DP}{DE} = \dfrac{DQ}{DF} = \dfrac{PQ}{EF}$

so $\dfrac{DP}{DE} = \dfrac{DQ}{DF} = \dfrac{BC}{EF}$   (Why?)

so $BC = PQ$   (Why?)

so ${\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DPQ}$   (Why?)
