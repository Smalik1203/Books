6.4Criteria for Similarity of Triangles

In the previous section we said that two triangles are similar if (i) their corresponding angles are equal and (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio. That is, in $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle DEF$, if

(i) $\angle A = \angle D$, $\angle B = \angle E$, $\angle C = \angle F$ and

(ii) $\dfrac{AB}{DE} = \dfrac{BC}{EF} = \dfrac{CA}{FD}$

then the two triangles are similar (Fig. 6.22).

Here A corresponds to D, B to E and C to F. We write the similarity of these two triangles as ${\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF}$ and read it as "triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF". The symbol $\sim$ stands for "is similar to". In Class IX you used the symbol $\cong$ for "is congruent to".

As with congruence, a similarity must be written with the vertices in the correct correspondence. For the triangles of Fig. 6.22, we cannot write ${\triangle ABC \sim \triangle EDF}$ or ${\triangle ABC \sim \triangle FED}$, but we can write ${\triangle BAC \sim \triangle EDF}$.

Now a natural question arises. To check whether two triangles ABC and DEF are similar, must we always check all three equal angles, $\angle A = \angle D$, $\angle B = \angle E$ and $\angle C = \angle F$, and all the equal ratios of sides, ${\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{BC}{EF} = \frac{CA}{FD}}$? In Class IX you found criteria for congruence that use only three pairs of corresponding parts of two triangles. Let us look for criteria for similarity that also use fewer than all six pairs.
