Here A corresponds to D, B to E and C to F. We write the similarity of these two triangles as ${\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF}$ and read it as "triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF". The symbol $\sim$ stands for "is similar to". In Class IX you used the symbol $\cong$ for "is congruent to".

As with congruence, a similarity must be written with the vertices in the correct correspondence. For the triangles of Fig. 6.22, we cannot write ${\triangle ABC \sim \triangle EDF}$ or ${\triangle ABC \sim \triangle FED}$, but we can write ${\triangle BAC \sim \triangle EDF}$.

Now a natural question arises. To check whether two triangles ABC and DEF are similar, must we always check all three equal angles, $\angle A = \angle D$, $\angle B = \angle E$ and $\angle C = \angle F$, and all the equal ratios of sides, ${\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{BC}{EF} = \frac{CA}{FD}}$? In Class IX you found criteria for congruence that use only three pairs of corresponding parts of two triangles. Let us look for criteria for similarity that also use fewer than all six pairs. Try this activity.

Activity 4. Draw two line segments BC and EF of different lengths, say 3 cm and 5 cm. At B and C, construct angles PBC and QCB of, say, $60^\circ$ and $40^\circ$. At E and F, construct angles REF and SFE of $60^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ (Fig. 6.23). Use a ruler for BC and EF, and a protractor for the angles. Keep the two drawings, because you will measure them. Label every point.

Let the rays BP and CQ meet at A, and the rays ER and FS meet at D. In the triangles ABC and DEF, $\angle B = \angle E$, $\angle C = \angle F$ and $\angle A = \angle D$, so their corresponding angles are equal. What about their corresponding sides? Here $\frac{BC}{EF} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6$. Now measure AB, DE, CA and FD. You will find that $\frac{AB}{DE}$ and $\frac{CA}{FD}$ are also equal to 0.6, or nearly 0.6 if there is some error in measuring. So ${\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{BC}{EF} = \frac{CA}{FD}}$. You can repeat the activity with several pairs of triangles whose corresponding angles are equal. Each time, you will find that their corresponding sides are in the same ratio. This leads to the following criterion. (Measure as carefully as you can, and compare your ratios with a classmate’s. Small differences come only from errors in measuring.)

Theorem 6.3

If in two triangles corresponding angles are equal, then their corresponding sides are in the same ratio, and so the two triangles are similar.

This criterion is called the AAA (Angle–Angle–Angle) criterion of similarity of two triangles. Its name lists the three pairs of equal parts that it uses, and all three are angles.
