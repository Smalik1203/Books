Remark. Recall that for two polygons, neither condition (equal corresponding angles, or corresponding sides in the same ratio) is enough on its own for similarity. But Theorems 6.3 and 6.4 show that for two triangles you need not check both conditions, because each one gives the other.

Now recall the criteria for congruence of triangles from Class IX. The SSS similarity criterion matches the SSS congruence criterion. This suggests looking for a similarity criterion that matches the SAS congruence criterion. Try this activity.

Activity 6. Draw two triangles ABC and DEF with AB = 2 cm, $\angle A = 50^\circ$, AC = 4 cm, DE = 3 cm, $\angle D = 50^\circ$ and DF = 6 cm (Fig. 6.27).

Here ${\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{AC}{DF}}$, each equal to $\frac{2}{3}$, and $\angle A$, between the sides AB and AC, is equal to $\angle D$, between the sides DE and DF. That is, one angle of one triangle is equal to one angle of the other, and the sides that include these angles are in the same ratio. Now measure ${\angle B, \angle C, \angle E}$ and $\angle F$. You will find that $\angle B = \angle E$ and $\angle C = \angle F$. So all three pairs of angles are equal, and by the AAA criterion the triangles ABC and DEF are similar. Repeat this with several pairs of such triangles, and each time the triangles are similar. This is because of the following criterion.

Theorem 6.5

If one angle of a triangle is equal to one angle of the other triangle and the sides including these angles are proportional, then the two triangles are similar.

This criterion is called the SAS (Side–Angle–Side) similarity criterion for two triangles.

As before, it can be proved by taking two triangles ABC and DEF such that ${\frac{AB}{DE} = \frac{AC}{DF}}$, with this ratio less than 1, and $\angle A = \angle D$ (Fig. 6.28). Mark P on DE and Q on DF such that DP = AB and DQ = AC, and join PQ.
