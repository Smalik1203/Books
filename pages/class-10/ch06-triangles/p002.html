Can a circle and a square be similar? Can a triangle and a square be similar? You can answer these questions just by looking at the shapes in Fig. 6.1, without measuring anything: these figures are not similar. (Why?)

What about the two quadrilaterals ABCD and PQRS in Fig. 6.2? Are they similar? They look similar, but we cannot be sure just by looking. We need a definition of similarity, and rules based on it, to decide whether two figures are similar. To find one, look at the photographs in Fig. 6.3.

You will say at once that they are photographs of the same monument, the Taj Mahal, printed in different sizes. Are the three photographs similar? Yes, they are.

Now think of two photographs of the same size of the same person, one taken at the age of 10 and the other at the age of 40. Are these photographs similar? They are the same size, but they certainly do not have the same shape. So they are not similar.

What does a photographer do to print photographs of different sizes from the same film? You may have seen stamp-size, passport-size and postcard-size photographs. Suppose a photograph is taken on film 35 mm in size and then enlarged to 45 mm (or 55 mm). Then every line segment in the bigger photograph is $\frac{45}{35}$ (or $\frac{55}{35}$) of the matching segment in the smaller one. That is, every line segment of the smaller photograph is enlarged in the ratio 35 : 45 (or 35 : 55), and every line segment of the bigger photograph is reduced in the ratio 45 : 35 (or 55 : 35). Also, the angle between any two line segments in one photograph is always equal to the angle between the matching segments in the other. This is what similarity means, in particular for two polygons.
