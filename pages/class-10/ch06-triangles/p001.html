Triangles

6.1Introduction

You know triangles and their properties from earlier classes, and in Class IX you studied congruence of triangles in detail. Two figures are congruent if they have the same shape and the same size. In this chapter you will study figures that have the same shape but not necessarily the same size, called similar figures, especially similar triangles.

How do you think the height of a mountain such as Mount Everest, or the distance to a far-away object such as the Moon, has been found? Not with a measuring tape. These heights and distances are found by indirect measurement, which uses the idea of similar figures. You will see this in Example 7 and in Question 15 of Exercise Set 6.3, and again in Chapters 8 and 9 of this book.

6.2Similar Figures

In Class IX you saw that all circles with the same radius are congruent, all squares with the same side length are congruent, and all equilateral triangles with the same side length are congruent.

Now look at the circles in Fig. 6.1 (i). Are they congruent? They do not all have the same radius, so they are not all congruent to one another. But they all have the same shape. We say that they are similar. Two similar figures have the same shape, but not necessarily the same size. So all circles are similar. What about the squares in Fig. 6.1 (ii), or the equilateral triangles in Fig. 6.1 (iii)? In the same way, all squares are similar, and all equilateral triangles are similar.

So all congruent figures are similar, but similar figures need not be congruent.

Can a circle and a square be similar? Can a triangle and a square be similar? You can answer these questions just by looking at the shapes in Fig. 6.1, without measuring anything: these figures are not similar. (Why?)
