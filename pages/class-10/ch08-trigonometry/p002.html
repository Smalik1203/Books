In each of these situations, one length is easy to measure and another is not. In all of them, the distances or heights can be found with methods from a branch of mathematics called trigonometry. The word comes from the Greek words trigonon (a triangle, a figure with three angles) and metron (measure). Trigonometry is the study of the relationships between the sides and the angles of a triangle. The earliest known work on it was recorded in Egypt and Babylon, and early astronomers used it to find the distances of the stars and the planets from the Earth. Even today, many advanced methods in engineering and the physical sciences are based on it. The German teacher J. F. Herbart wrote that nothing holds the middle position of mathematics as fully as trigonometry does. You will use it again in Chapter 9, to find heights and distances.

In this chapter, we study some ratios of the sides of a right triangle with respect to its acute angles, called the trigonometric ratios of the angle. We keep to acute angles only, although these ratios can be extended to other angles too. We also define the trigonometric ratios for angles of $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$. We work out the trigonometric ratios for some particular angles, and prove some identities involving these ratios, called trigonometric identities. With these ratios, you will be able to find a side of a right triangle from one side and one angle.
