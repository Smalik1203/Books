From history. The idea of the sine, in the way we use it today, first appears in the Aryabhatiyam, the work of Aryabhata (476–550 CE), written around 500 CE. Aryabhata used the word ardha-jya for the half-chord, and it was shortened in time to jya or jiva. When the Aryabhatiyam was translated into Arabic, the word jiva was kept as it was. When the Arabic version was translated into Latin, jiva became sinus, which means curve. Soon sinus, or sine, was in common use across Europe. Edmund Gunter (1581–1626), an English professor of astronomy, was the first to use the short form sin.

The words cosine and tangent came much later. The cosine arose from the need to find the sine of the complementary angle, and Aryabhata called it kotijya. The name cosinus came from Edmund Gunter, and in 1674 the English mathematician Sir Jonas Moore first used the short form cos.

Remark. The symbol $\sin A$ is short for "the sine of the angle A". It is not the product of "sin" and A: "sin" on its own, apart from A, has no meaning. In the same way, $\cos A$ is not the product of "cos" and A, and the same holds for the other trigonometric ratios.

Now take a point P on the hypotenuse AC, or a point Q on AC extended, of the right triangle ABC. Draw PM perpendicular to AB, and QN perpendicular to AB extended (Fig. 8.6). How do the trigonometric ratios of $\angle A$ in $\triangle PAM$ compare with those of $\angle A$ in $\triangle CAB$, or with those of $\angle A$ in $\triangle QAN$?

To answer this, first look at the triangles. Is $\triangle PAM$ similar to $\triangle CAB$? Recall the AA similarity criterion from Chapter 6. Using it, you can see that the triangles PAM and CAB are similar. So, by the property of similar triangles, their corresponding sides are proportional, and we have

$$\dfrac{AM}{AB} = \dfrac{AP}{AC} = \dfrac{MP}{BC}$$

From this we get ${\frac{MP}{AP} = \frac{BC}{AC} = \sin A}$. In the same way, ${\frac{AM}{AP} = \frac{AB}{AC} = \cos A}$, ${\frac{MP}{AM} = \frac{BC}{AB} = \tan A}$, and so on.

This shows that the trigonometric ratios of angle A in $\triangle PAM$ are the same as those of angle A in $\triangle CAB$. In the same way, you should check that the value of $\sin A$, and of the other trigonometric ratios, stays the same in $\triangle QAN$ too.
