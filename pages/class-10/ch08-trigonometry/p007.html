Example 2

If $\angle B$ and $\angle Q$ are acute angles such that ${\sin B = \sin Q}$, prove that ${\angle B = \angle Q}$. Use two right triangles, one containing each angle.

Solution.

Step 1 Right triangles ABC and PQR, right-angled at C and at R, with ${\sin B = \sin Q}$ (Fig. 8.9) one for each angle
Step 2 $\sin B = \dfrac{AC}{AB}$   and   $\sin Q = \dfrac{PR}{PQ}$ the definition
Step 3 $\dfrac{AC}{AB} = \dfrac{PR}{PQ}$, so $\dfrac{AC}{PR} = \dfrac{AB}{PQ} = k$, say   (1) the sines are equal
Step 4 ${BC = \sqrt{AB^2 - AC^2}}$   and   ${QR = \sqrt{PQ^2 - PR^2}}$ the Pythagoras theorem
Step 5 $\dfrac{BC}{QR} = \dfrac{\sqrt{AB^2 - AC^2}}{\sqrt{PQ^2 - PR^2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{k^2PQ^2 - k^2PR^2}}{\sqrt{PQ^2 - PR^2}}$ putting in (1)
Step 6 $= \dfrac{k\sqrt{PQ^2 - PR^2}}{\sqrt{PQ^2 - PR^2}} = k$   (2)
Step 7 $\dfrac{AC}{PR} = \dfrac{AB}{PQ} = \dfrac{BC}{QR}$ from (1) and (2)
Step 8 ${\triangle ACB \sim \triangle PRQ}$ by Theorem 6.4
Answer So ${\angle B = \angle Q}$, as corresponding angles of similar triangles

Recall that Theorem 6.4 of Chapter 6 is the SSS similarity criterion: triangles whose sides are in the same ratio are similar. This shows that an acute angle is fixed by the value of its sine: two different acute angles cannot have the same sine. So once the sine of an acute angle is known, the angle itself is known.
