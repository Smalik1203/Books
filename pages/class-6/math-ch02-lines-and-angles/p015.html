Three measures to remember

A full turn is $360^\circ$. A straight angle is $180^\circ$. A right angle is $90^\circ$.

Why 360?

Nobody knows for sure why a full turn was divided into 360 parts. The idea is very old. The Rigveda, one of the oldest texts in the world, speaks of a wheel with 360 spokes. Many old calendars, in India, Persia, Babylonia and Egypt, had 360 days in a year. Babylonian mathematicians counted in 60s, and they often used 60 and 360.

There is also a useful reason. 360 is the smallest number that can be divided exactly by every number from 1 to 10 except 7. So you can cut a circle into 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 or 10 equal parts, and each part is still a whole number of degrees. 360 can also be divided exactly by 12, the number of months in a year, and by 24, the number of hours in a day.

The protractor

How do we measure other angles in degrees? We use a tool called a protractor. A protractor is either a full circle marked with 360 equal parts, or a half circle marked with 180 equal parts.

A protractor is made of clear plastic, so you can see the angle through it. Its straight edge is called the base. The middle of the base is marked with a short line. This point is the centre of the protractor, and it is where the vertex of an angle must go.

Fig. 2.33 shows a half-circle protractor with no numbers on it. Its straight edge is a straight angle, divided into 180 units of $1^\circ$. You can see only the outer ends of the dividing lines. Starting from the right end of the straight edge, there is a long mark every $10^\circ$. Halfway between two long marks there is a medium mark, at every $5^\circ$.
