One example is enough to show that a rule like this is wrong. Take $30^\circ$. It is acute. Double it and you get $60^\circ$, which is still acute. So Meena is not right. Double $50^\circ$ and you get $100^\circ$, which is obtuse, and double $45^\circ$ and you get $90^\circ$, a right angle. The answer depends on the angle you start with.

What is the angle between the hands of a clock at 5 o’clock?

The 12 numbers divide the full turn into 12 equal parts. Each part is $360^\circ \div 12 = 30^\circ$. At 5 o’clock the minute hand points at 12 and the hour hand at 5, so there are 5 parts between them: $5 \times 30^\circ = 150^\circ$. Going the other way round, from 5 to 12, there are 7 parts, which is $210^\circ$, a reflex angle. The two hands make both angles, and $150^\circ + 210^\circ = 360^\circ$.

None of these questions needed a new rule. Each one needed you to see a turn you already knew inside it: a full turn, a straight angle or a right angle.

Problem 1

The smaller angle between the hands of a clock at 10 o’clock is

  1. $300^\circ$
  2. $100^\circ$
  3. $60^\circ$
  4. $120^\circ$
Solution

The dial is a full turn cut into 12 equal parts. Count the parts between the hands the short way, from 10 up to 12.

one part: $360^\circ \div 12 = 30^\circ$

from 10 to 12: 2 parts

$2 \times 30^\circ = 60^\circ$

The answer is $60^\circ$, option (c). Option (a) is the angle the long way round, from 12 to 10, which is a reflex angle and not the smaller one. Option (b) takes each number on the dial to be worth $10^\circ$. Option (d) counts 4 parts instead of 2.

You can check an answer like this by picturing the clock. At 10 o’clock the hour hand is two numbers before 12. A quarter of the dial is three numbers, so the smaller angle must be less than $90^\circ$. Of the four options, only $60^\circ$ is.

The same counting works at any hour. At 4 o’clock the hands are 4 parts apart, which is $120^\circ$. At 8 o’clock they are also 4 parts apart the short way, from 8 up to 12, so the smaller angle is again $120^\circ$.
