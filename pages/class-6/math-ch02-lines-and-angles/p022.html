Angle bisector

Folding an angle in half gives two equal angles. Finding half of an angle like this is called bisecting the angle. The line that bisects an angle is called the angle bisector of that angle.

To bisect an angle drawn on paper, fold the paper through the vertex so that one arm lies exactly on the other. The crease is the angle bisector. If $\angle AOB$ in Fig. 2.38 is $70^\circ$, each of the two angles it is split into is $35^\circ$.

Equal angles in a figure are often marked with matching curves, each with a small stroke across it. When two curves are marked the same way, you know the angles are equal without measuring them. Fig. 2.38 marks $\angle AOC$ and $\angle COB$ in this way.

Find the angle bisectors in your paper protractor. Then try to make other angles by folding, using angle bisectors. For example, fold a straight angle in half, and then fold one of the halves in half again.

Think and Reflect
  1. Fold a right angle in half, and then in half again. What is the measure of each of the smallest angles?
  2. Can you bisect a straight angle by folding? What is the measure of each half, and what kind of angle is it?
  3. By folding in half again and again, starting from a straight angle, which of these can you make: $45^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $135^\circ$? Why?
  4. How many times must you fold a straight angle in half to get an angle of $22.5^\circ$? Check your answer on your paper protractor.
