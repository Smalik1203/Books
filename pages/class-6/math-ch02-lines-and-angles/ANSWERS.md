## 2.1–2.4 Point, Line Segment, Line and Ray

### Think and Reflect (§2.1, Point)

**Yes, always.** A point has no size, so between any two points, however
close, there is room for another point, and then another between those.

### Think and Reflect (§2.2, Line segment)

Answers will vary: the edge of a ruler, the edge of the blackboard, a side
of a window frame or a desk, each with two end points.

### Think and Reflect (§2.3, Line)

1. A line goes on without end in both directions, so we can draw only a
   part of it; the arrowheads say that it carries on. A line segment stops
   at its two end points, so it has none.

### Think and Reflect (§2.4, Ray)

**Two**: one in each direction along the line.

## 2.5 Angle

### Think and Reflect (after naming an angle)

1. Answers will vary. Both names have the vertex in the middle, for example
   $\angle DBE$ and $\angle EBD$.
2. **No.** An angle is two rays with a *common* starting point. Two rays
   that start at different points do not make an angle.

### The size of an angle (Fig. 2.10)

In each case the vertex is the spine of the book and the arms are the page
edge that stays on the table and the edge of the cover. The angle in Case 2
is bigger than in Case 1; the drawn turns are **25°, 60°, 105°, 150°, 230°
and 330°**, so each case turns further than the one before.

   through $R$, with a curve at $S$ between them, and the angle named
   $\angle TSR$ or $\angle RST$.

   $\overleftrightarrow{AD}$, $\overleftrightarrow{BC}$,

   angles** can be named: at $A$, $\angle BAC$, $\angle CAD$, $\angle BAD$;
   and three each at $B$, $C$ and $D$ in the same way.

### Think and Reflect (after the key idea *Equal angles*)

1. Line segments are compared by placing one along the other with one end
   on the other's end; shapes are compared by placing one on top of the
   other, as when checking whether two cut-outs are the same.
2. If the vertices do not match, the arms of the tracing do not start at
   $O$. An arm can then cross the arm of $\angle AOB$ or lie beside it, and
   Raju may decide that equal angles are unequal, or the wrong one is
   bigger.

### Exercise Set 2.3

1. Answers will vary. A crease and a side of the paper make two angles on
   each side of the crease, and the two angles at one point together make a
   straight angle. A fold at right angles to a side makes two equal angles;
   any other fold makes one angle bigger and one smaller. The more slanting
   the crease, the smaller the smallest angle.

2. In Fig. 2.18, $\overrightarrow{OA}$ is at 90°, $\overrightarrow{OX}$ at
   60° and $\overrightarrow{OY}$ at 26° from the bottom ray:
   - (i) **$\angle AOB$** is bigger: $\angle AOB = 90°$ and $\angle XOY = 34°$;
   - (ii) **$\angle AOB$** is bigger: $\overrightarrow{OX}$ lies inside

   - (iii) **they are equal**: $B$ and $C$ lie on the same ray, so
     $\angle XOB$ and $\angle XOC$ are the same angle, 60°.

   $\angle AOY$ with $\angle XOA$ added, and $\angle AOB$ is $\angle AOY$

## 2.8 Special Types of Angles

### Think and Reflect (after the key idea *Right angles*)

1. **Four.** The two creases are perpendicular, so they make four right
   angles where they cross.
2. **No.** It is a right angle only if it is exactly half of a straight
   angle. Check it by folding a straight angle in half, or with the corner
   of a page.

### Exercise Set 2.4

1. Answers will vary. A rectangular window has **4 right angles** at its
   corners, and more where the bars of the frame cross. Right angles are
   also at the corners of the board, the door, a desk top and a book.

2. In the first grid of Fig. 2.25, $A$ is joined to $B$ two dots to its
   right. A straight angle at $A$ needs the line carried on to the left,
   through the dots on the same row: **3 ways**, to the 1st, 2nd or 3rd dot
   to the left of $A$. In the second grid, $B$ is two dots down and two
   right of $A$; the line carried on goes up and to the left, one dot up
   and one left at a time: **2 ways**.

3. A right angle at $A$ needs a line at right angles to $\overline{AB}$.
   In the first grid this is the column through $A$: **5 ways**, 2 dots
   above $A$ and 3 below. In the second grid it goes one dot up and one
   right, or one down and one left, at a time: **4 ways**, 2 each way. Each
   one divides the straight angle $\angle CAB$ of Fig. 2.26 into two equal
   parts.

4. (i) **4 right angles** at the point where the creases cross. Each is
   exactly a right angle because the second fold laid one part of the first
   crease exactly on the other part, so the two angles on each side are
   equal, and two equal angles that make a straight angle are right angles.
   (ii) Answers will vary. For example: *fold the sheet in any direction and
   press the crease flat; keep it folded; fold again so that the crease lies
   exactly on itself; press flat and open out.*

## 2.9 Measuring Angles

### Think and Reflect (Fig. 2.32)

One part in each circle: 1 part $360°$; 2 parts $360 \div 2 = 180$°;
3 parts $360 \div 3 = 120$°; 4 parts $360 \div 4 = 90$°; 5 parts
$360 \div 5 = 72$°; 6 parts $360 \div 6 = 60$°; 8 parts $360 \div 8 = 45$°;
9 parts $360 \div 9 = 40$°; 10 parts $360 \div 10 = 36$°; 12 parts
$360 \div 12 = 30$°. The marked curve in each circle is drawn at exactly that
angle.

Item 2: the circle of **9** parts has parts of 40°, since $360 \div 9 = 40$;
the circle of **10** parts has parts of 36°, since $360 \div 10 = 36$.

### Example 1 (worked in the book)

$\angle KAL = 30°$. In Fig. 2.33 the rays are drawn at 30° ($T$), 70° ($W$),
120° ($L$) and 150° ($K$) from the right end of the base.

**Think and Reflect (after Example 1).** 1. **One** medium mark, halfway
between them; two long marks next to each other are **10** units of 1°
apart. 2. Each long mark stands for 10 units and each medium mark for 5, so
one mark counts 10 or 5 units at once. Single units are still counted when an
arm falls between two marks, for example at 38°.

### Angle bisector (Fig. 2.38)

$\angle AOB$ is drawn at 70° and the crease at 35°, so each part is
$70 \div 2 = 35$ degrees.

**Think and Reflect (bisecting).** 1. $90 \div 2 = 45$, then
$45 \div 2 = 22.5$: each smallest angle is **22½°**. 2. **Yes.** Fold so that
one arm lies on the other; each half is $180 \div 2 = 90$, a **right angle**.
3. **45°** (fold 180° in half twice) and **135°** (three of those 45° parts,
