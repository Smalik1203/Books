Now move the circle to the second crane’s angle, $\angle XOY$. Put $O$ on the vertex again, and lay $OA$ along the arm $\overrightarrow{OX}$. The mark $B$ lies inside the second angle, before the arm $\overrightarrow{OY}$. So the second crane opens its beak wider.

Cut a circle from tracing paper and try this with any two angles you draw.

Making the arms of an angle longer does not make the angle bigger. Only the amount of turn between the arms decides its size.

2.7Making Rotating Arms

You can make a model of an angle that opens and closes. Take two paper straws and a paper clip. Push one end of each straw onto an arm of the clip. Your rotating arms are ready.

Make several rotating arms, each opened to a different angle. Arrange them from the smallest angle to the biggest. Use superimposition when two of them are close.

Passing through a slit

Collect several rotating arms with different angles. Do not change any of them during this activity. Trace one of them on a piece of cardboard and cut out the shape to make an angle-shaped slit.

Mix up all the rotating arms. Which of them will pass through the slit? To find out, place each one over the slit, as in Fig. 2.21.

If the angle of the arms is smaller or bigger than the angle of the slit, the arms will not go through. Only the arms whose angle is equal to the slit’s angle pass through it. The length of the arms does not matter, as long as they are shorter than the slit.

Here is a challenge a friend might give you: make this angle smaller. You might cut the straws to make the arms shorter. Is the angle any smaller now? No. The arms are shorter, but the turn between them is the same, so the angle has not changed.
