10.5A Pinch of History

Like fractions, integers, including zero and the negative numbers, were first thought of and used in Asia, thousands of years ago. Much later, they spread across the world.

The earliest known uses of negative numbers were in keeping accounts. One of the most important mathematics books of China is The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. It was finished by the first or second century CE. It showed positive and negative numbers with red and black rods, much as we used blue and orange tokens.

India also had a strong tradition of keeping accounts. Around 300 BCE, Kautilya wrote a great deal about credit and debit in his Arthaśāstra, and he knew that a balance could be negative. Negative numbers are used in accounts in many old Indian works. One of them is the Bakhshālī manuscript, from around 300 CE. In it, a negative number was shown by a special mark written after the number, not before it as we write today.

The first person to treat positive numbers, negative numbers and zero all as proper numbers was Brahmagupta, in his book Brāhmasphutasiddhānta, written in 628 CE. He gave clear rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and even dividing them. We still work with integers in the same way today. Here are his rules for addition and subtraction.

Brahmagupta had names for these numbers that came from money. He called a positive number dhana, which means wealth, and a negative number rina, which means a debt. Zero was shunya, the empty place.

Brahmagupta’s rules for addition

1. The sum of two positive numbers is positive: $2 + 3 = 5$.

2. The sum of two negative numbers is negative. Add the numbers without their signs, and put a minus sign in front: $(-2) + (-3) = -5$.

3. To add a positive number and a negative number, take the smaller number from the larger one, both without signs. Give the answer the sign of the larger one: $(-5) + 3 = -2$, $2 + (-3) = -1$ and $(-3) + 5 = 2$.

4. A number plus its inverse is zero: $2 + (-2) = 0$.

5. A number plus zero is the same number: $(-2) + 0 = -2$ and $0 + 0 = 0$.

Rule 3 makes sense with money. If you have ₹5 and owe ₹3, you are left with ₹2, so $5 + (-3) = 2$. If you have ₹3 and owe ₹5, you still owe ₹2, so $3 + (-5) = -2$. The larger amount decides whether you end with money or with a debt.
