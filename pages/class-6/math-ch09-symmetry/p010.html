9.2Rotational Symmetry

Look at the paper windmill in Fig. 9.21. It looks symmetrical, but it has no line of symmetry. However you fold it, the two halves do not fit exactly.

Now turn the windmill a quarter of the way round its centre. Each blade moves to where the next blade was, and the windmill looks exactly the same as before. We say that the windmill has rotational symmetry.

A turn always happens about a fixed point. For the windmill, it is the marked point in the middle. This fixed point is called the centre of rotation.

Turn the windmill by less than a quarter turn, and each blade stops part-way to the next blade’s place. So it does not look the same. A quarter turn, $90^\circ$, is the smallest turn that works.

Angle of symmetry

An angle through which a figure can be turned so that it looks exactly the same is called an angle of rotational symmetry. We usually call it an angle of symmetry, for short.

The windmill looks the same after a turn of $90^\circ$ (a quarter turn), $180^\circ$ (a half turn), $270^\circ$ (a three-quarter turn) and $360^\circ$ (a full turn). So its angles of symmetry are $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$, $270^\circ$ and $360^\circ$. The windmill has 4 angles of symmetry.

A full turn of $360^\circ$ brings every figure back to where it started. So $360^\circ$ is always an angle of symmetry.
