Summary
  1. A figure made of parts that repeat in a fixed pattern has symmetry. We say that it is symmetrical. A flower and a rangoli are symmetrical, but a cloud is not.
  2. A line of symmetry cuts a figure into two parts that fit exactly on each other when you fold along the line. The two parts are called mirror halves.
  3. A figure may have more than one line of symmetry. A square has 4: its two diagonals, a vertical line and a horizontal line. A rectangle that is not a square has only 2, because its diagonals are not lines of symmetry.
  4. A line of symmetry reflects the part on one side of it onto the part on the other side, as a mirror does. So a figure with a line of symmetry is said to have reflection symmetry.
  5. Ink blots, folded paper cut-outs and holes punched in folded paper are all symmetrical. Whatever happens on one side of the fold happens at the same place on the other side, so the fold is a line of symmetry.
  6. An angle of symmetry is an angle through which a figure can be turned so that it looks exactly the same. The fixed point it turns about is the centre of rotation. A full turn of $360^\circ$ is an angle of symmetry of every figure.
  7. A figure has rotational symmetry if it has an angle of symmetry more than $0^\circ$ and less than $360^\circ$. The number of its angles of symmetry is its order of rotational symmetry. A square has order 4: its angles of symmetry are $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$, $270^\circ$ and $360^\circ$.
  8. A figure with radial arms has rotational symmetry when its arms are equal and equally spaced. With 3 such arms, $360^\circ \div 3 = 120^\circ$ is the smallest angle of symmetry.
  9. A circle fits on itself after a turn through any angle, and every diameter of a circle is a line of symmetry.
  10. Some figures have a line of symmetry but no rotational symmetry. Others, like the paper windmill, have rotational symmetry but no line of symmetry. Some, like the square, have both.
