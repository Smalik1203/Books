The angles of symmetry are the multiples of the smallest one, up to $360^\circ$. Count how many 40s make 360: $360 \div 40 = 9$. So the figure has 9 angles of symmetry: $40^\circ$, $80^\circ$, $120^\circ$, and so on up to $360^\circ$. The number 100 is not a multiple of 40, because $40 \times 2 = 80$ and $40 \times 3 = 120$. So $100^\circ$ is not an angle of symmetry. We did not need to see the figure at all.

A figure is made of equal radial arms, spaced equally. For which of these numbers of arms is the smallest angle of symmetry a whole number of degrees: 7, 8, 9, 11, 12?

With equal arms spaced equally, the smallest angle of symmetry is $360^\circ$ shared out equally among the arms. This is a whole number only when the number of arms divides 360 exactly. $360 \div 8 = 45$, $360 \div 9 = 40$ and $360 \div 12 = 30$, so 8, 9 and 12 arms work. But $360 \div 7 = 51\frac{3}{7}$ and $360 \div 11 = 32\frac{8}{11}$, so 7 and 11 arms do not. The question about angles became a question about the factors of 360.

Look back at the game on a grid of 6 rows of 6 squares. How can the second player always win?

The second player can copy every move with a half turn about the centre of the grid. Whenever the first player draws a line, the second player draws the line that it lands on after a half turn. After each of the second player’s moves, the covered squares make a pattern with rotational symmetry of order 2.

Can the copied line always be drawn? The centre of a 6 by 6 grid is a corner where four squares meet, and the middle of any line in the game is the midpoint of a side shared by two squares. So no line lands on itself after a half turn, and no line can even meet its own copy. The squares for the copy were free before the first player moved, because the pattern was symmetrical. So the second player always has a move, and the first player is the one who runs out. Symmetry decided the game.

Draw a four-sided figure that has exactly 2 lines of symmetry. Does it also have rotational symmetry?

A rectangle that is not a square works. It folds onto itself along the line through the middles of its long sides, and along the line through the middles of its short sides, and along no other line. A rhombus that is not a square works too: its two lines of symmetry are its diagonals.

Now give each figure a half turn about its centre. The rectangle fits on itself, and so does the rhombus. Both have rotational symmetry of order 2. This is the same thing we saw with the letters H and X: two lines of symmetry at right angles bring a half turn with them.
