Symmetry

Look around you. Many things catch your eye because they are beautiful. Fig. 9.1 shows five of them, drawn as simple shapes.

The flower looks the same from many directions. The two wings of the butterfly match each other. In the rangoli, the four large points are all alike. If you turn the rangoli a quarter of the way round its centre, each large point lands where the next one was, and so does every other part.

In the pinwheel, look at the hexagon in the middle first. The same small triangle is stuck to each of its six sides. Now look at the cloud. Nothing in it repeats in a regular way.

In the first four pictures, a part of the figure repeats in a fixed pattern. We say that these figures are symmetrical, or have symmetry. The cloud is not symmetrical.

In this chapter you will meet two ways in which a figure can repeat. It can fold onto itself, like the two wings of the butterfly. Or it can turn onto itself, like the pinwheel. The flower does both.

Symmetry is all around us in India. Rangoli and kolam designs at doorways, the borders of saris, carved temple doors and the tiles on many floors are made of parts that repeat. Even a leaf, a festival lantern or the wheel of a bullock cart can show symmetry. Look for these patterns on your way to school.
