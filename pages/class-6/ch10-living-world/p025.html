Investigation 9 · A pond from a safe distance

Question: which visible features distinguish the stages you see? Prediction: sketch the features you expect in a tadpole and a froglet before viewing.

Procedure: with an adult, observe from a firm, safe edge, or use a teacher-provided pond video. Sketch only stages you can identify. Note body shape, legs, tail and the place where each animal is seen. Do not label an unknown water animal “tadpole” simply because it wriggles.

Record: use one entry for each sighting. Leave identity uncertain if the features are unclear.

Think it through

Read a sample record

In this invented picture record, eggs are seen on day 0, a tadpole on day 6, a froglet on day 60 and a tailless young frog on day 80. The observed interval from tadpole to froglet is 54 days; from froglet to tailless young frog it is 20 days. A young frog is not necessarily a reproducing adult. These are intervals between recorded sightings, not exact stage durations, because a change may have occurred between visits.

To estimate durations more closely, observe more frequently. Do not conclude that all frogs follow this timetable. Species, water temperature and food supply can change the timing.

The cycle in words and pictures

Make a six-frame strip: eggs, embryo in egg, tadpole, tadpole with hind legs, froglet and adult. Draw a final arrow from the adult to new eggs and label it “reproduction”. Write one original sentence under each frame. You can turn the sentences into a short poem, but check that every stage remains scientifically accurate.

Notice that the table groups embryos with eggs and leg-bearing tadpoles with tadpoles. Six drawings and four broad stages need not disagree. They can describe the same continuous development at different levels of detail.
