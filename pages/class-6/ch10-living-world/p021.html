One animal, changing form

The larva and pupa are not two different animals. They are successive forms in one mosquito’s development. In many common mosquitoes, both obtain air at the water surface. A larva is usually elongated; the pupa has a larger rounded front and a curled abdomen. A pupa is not a dead larva, even though its feeding and appearance change.

Four stages

Egg → larva → pupa → adult → eggs of the next generation. Major changes in body form during development are called metamorphosis.

Temperature, food, species and other conditions affect development and adult lifespan. There is no single number of days that describes every mosquito. Use dates from a particular observation record when answering a timing question.

How can we find the correct order?

A photograph showing larvae and pupae together tells us that both occur in the container. It does not, by itself, prove which becomes which. To establish a sequence, we need observations of change through time.

Investigation 8 · Reconstruct a sequence safely

Question: which evidence connects stages? Prediction: write the stage order you expect.

Procedure: use a teacher-provided video or dated picture series that follows individual mosquitoes through development. Record the starting shape, the later shape and the date for each tracked individual. Conclusion: if an elongated larva becomes a pupa, and a pupa later releases an adult, those transitions establish the order.

For a mixed group, repeatedly counting larvae and pupae is less certain: new eggs may hatch and some individuals may die. A continuous recording of a particular individual, or controlled observations supplied by a trained observer, gives stronger evidence.

Care while exploring. Do not rear mosquitoes or bring standing-water samples into the classroom. Use images and recordings. During a water-container inspection, observe from a safe place with an adult.
