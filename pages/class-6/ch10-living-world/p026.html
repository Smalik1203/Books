10.12Patterns across the living world

Plants and animals both grow, develop, use resources and reproduce. A life cycle connects one generation to the next. The stages differ, and some young organisms resemble adults much more closely than others do.

OrganismA useful sequenceA major change
Bean plantSeed → seedling → mature plant → flowers and pods → new seedsReproductive structures appear
MosquitoEgg → larva → pupa → adult → new eggsAquatic young become a flying adult
Pond frogEgg → tadpole → froglet → adult → new eggsTail and gills give way to an adult body with legs and lungs

Both mosquitoes and frogs have young stages in water, and both undergo marked changes in body form. A mosquito has a pupal stage; a frog does not. A froglet is a young frog with a changing tail, not a pupa. A frog is not simply a large tadpole, just as a winged mosquito is not simply a larger larva.

A chick already resembles a bird: it has a beak, legs and feathers, though its proportions and abilities change as it grows. Birds do not pass through larva and pupa stages. This comparison shows why “all animals change in exactly the same way” is not a useful rule.

Find the common idea

A bean produces seeds; a frog produces eggs. Explain how these very different structures connect generations. Then name one stage that belongs only to the mosquito sequence in the table.

The surroundings matter

Food availability, water, temperature and the condition of a habitat can affect how organisms grow and develop. Compare records within one species before drawing conclusions. A cooler week may slow insect development; a drying pond can threaten aquatic stages. Dates belong to observations made under particular conditions.

What continues?

The same individual does not travel around a life-cycle diagram forever. Individuals eventually die. Reproduction starts new individuals, continuing the living kind.
