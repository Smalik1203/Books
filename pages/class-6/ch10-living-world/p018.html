Investigation 7 · Keep a plant diary

Question: which changes connect seedling, flowers and new seeds? Prediction: sketch the order you expect before keeping the diary.

Procedure: grow a bean or another suitable quick-growing flowering plant in a pot with drainage. Ask a gardener which kind suits the season. Start a dated notebook record and continue for about three months, or longer if needed. Note the first root, first leaves, new branches, flower buds, open flowers and fruits.

For every new event, record a date, a short description and a labelled sketch. Count days from sowing rather than guessing the plant’s age. Observe which part remains after petals fade and whether a pod develops. If mature seeds form, save some and sow them under suitable conditions.

Conclusion: use dated entries to distinguish development of the parent from production of new seeds.

Many bean plants yellow and dry after completing reproduction, even if water remains available. Trees may flower and fruit repeatedly over many years. Do not turn one plant’s lifespan into a rule for all plants. A plant can also yellow from unsuitable conditions, so yellow leaves alone do not establish that its natural life has ended.

Reading a life diary

A diary records events as they occur, not as a timetable says they should occur. Light, temperature, water, variety and seed condition affect the pace. If no flowers appear by the final observation, write that honestly and continue if possible. A missing event is a result, not an empty space to fill with an imagined date.
