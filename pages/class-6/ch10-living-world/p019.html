Not every new plant begins with a seed

Seeds are one route to a new plant, but they are not the only one. A potato tuber can sprout from a bud, and a suitable stem cutting, such as money plant, can form roots. These are examples of reproduction from plant parts. A florist’s cut flower and a rooted cutting therefore should not be judged only by whether they are detached from a plant.

Think it through

A seed is missing from the story

Rohan grows a money plant from a stem piece with a node. He says, “It cannot have a life cycle because I never sowed a seed.” His conclusion is mistaken. The cutting can establish a new plant, grow and develop. A seed-to-seed diagram describes the bean example, not every possible route by which all plants reproduce.

To investigate a cutting, sketch the stem before roots appear and again after they develop. Label the node and the new roots. Keep a separate record for the original plant. The new cutting and its parent share a living origin, but their later growth can be followed as separate individuals.

From a living stem

Ask a gardener which stems root readily. Success with one plant does not mean that every detached twig can produce a new plant.

A small field study

During an adult-led garden visit, look for seedlings, mature plants, flowers and fruits. They may belong to different kinds of plants; do not arrange them as one life cycle unless you can identify them. Ask the gardener when one chosen plant is usually sown and when it normally flowers locally.

For a longer project, make a five-plant picture journal with names, dated observations and labelled stages. Distinguish what you observed from what the gardener told you. Leave flowers and seeds where they are unless you have permission to collect them. The journal can show both shared stages and different rates of development.
