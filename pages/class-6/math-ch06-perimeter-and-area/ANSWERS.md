## 6.1 Perimeter

### Exercise Set 6.1

1. (a) **5 cm**. Half the perimeter is $14 \div 2 = 7$ cm, which is length
   plus breadth, and $7 - 2 = 5$.
   (b) **5 cm**. $20 \div 4 = 5$.
   (c) **3 m**. $12 \div 2 = 6$ and $6 - 3 = 3$.

2. **4 cm**. The wire is the perimeter of the rectangle,
   $2 \times (5 + 3) = 16$ cm, and the square uses all of it:
   $16 \div 4 = 4$.

3. **21 cm**. $55 - 20 - 14 = 21$.

4. **₹21600**. The fence is $2 \times (150 + 120) = 540$ m, and
   $540 \times 40 = 21600$.

5. (a) **9 cm**, (b) **12 cm**, (c) **6 cm**. The string is the
   perimeter each time: $36 \div 4 = 9$, $36 \div 3 = 12$, $36 \div 6 = 6$.

   $3 \times 780 = 2340$.

   $2 \times (15 + 9) = 48$ m. They need the same fencing.

8. **560 m**. One round is $2 \times (80 + 60) = 280$ m, and
   $2 \times 280 = 560$.

9. length **10 cm**, breadth **5 cm**. Length and breadth add up to

### Straight units and diagonal units (Fig. 6.5)

**Toshi is right.** A diagonal unit crosses a square from corner to corner,
so it is longer than a straight unit. The perimeter is $6s + 3d$ units,
which is more than 9 straight units.

### Think and Reflect (after the key idea for an equilateral triangle)

1. All the sides of a square are equal and all its angles are equal, and
   the same is true of an equilateral triangle. Both are regular polygons,
   and the perimeter of each is its number of sides times one side.

2. Answers will vary. **Worked instance:** a square floor tile of side
   30 cm has perimeter $4 \times 30 = 120$ cm. **The rule:** the perimeter
   of a regular polygon is the number of sides times the length of one
   side.

### Exercise Set 6.4

4. (a) **28 sq m**. Cut the shape into strips, from the top:
   $2 \times 1 = 2$, $5 \times 2 = 10$, $7 \times 1 = 7$ and
   $3 \times 3 = 9$, and $2 + 10 + 7 + 9 = 28$. The two lengths the figure

### Why do we measure area in squares?

A circle of diameter 3 units covers **about 7** squares by the rules.
Answers will vary a little with where the circle is drawn.

### Think and Reflect (after Fig. 6.13)

1. Triangles and rectangles can both cover a space with no gaps if they are
   laid the right way round. **Reasons the square is the best unit:** all
   its sides are equal, so it can be laid in any direction; squares fit
   together in rows and columns with no gaps and no overlaps; a row and a
   column are easy to count; and a square of side 1 unit matches the unit
   of length.

### Exercise Set 6.7

Answers will vary. **Worked instances:** a classroom 8 m by 6 m has a floor
area of 48 sq m. A playground 60 m by 40 m has an area of 2400 sq m.

### Same area, different perimeters — Exercise Set 6.8

1. With whole-number sides, the rectangles of area 24 square units are:

   | rectangle | perimeter |
   |---|---|
   | 1 by 24 | 50 |
   | 2 by 12 | 28 |
   | 3 by 8 | 22 |
   | 4 by 6 | 20 |

   (a) the **1 by 24** rectangle has the greatest perimeter, 50 units.
   (b) the **4 by 6** rectangle has the least, 20 units.

2. For 32 sq cm:

   | rectangle | perimeter |
   |---|---|
   | 1 by 32 | 66 |
   | 2 by 16 | 36 |
   | 4 by 8 | 24 |

   The greatest perimeter is the 1 cm by 32 cm rectangle, 66 cm; the least
   is the 4 cm by 8 cm rectangle, 24 cm.

3. **Yes.** The long, thin rectangle with a side of 1 always has the
   greatest perimeter. The rectangle whose two sides are closest together
   has the least; when the area is a square number, that rectangle is a
   square. Examples: 24 and 32 above; for 36, the 1 by 36 rectangle has
   perimeter 74 and the 6 by 6 square has 24.

### Think and Reflect (after cutting a rectangle along a diagonal)

### Making it more or less — Exercise Set 6.10

Every figure of 9 squares starts with $9 \times 4 = 36$ sides, and each side
two squares share takes 2 away from the perimeter. A figure in one piece
shares at least 8 sides.

1. **12 units**, the 3 by 3 square, which shares 12 sides.

2. **20 units**, when only 8 sides are shared: a row of 9, or any figure
   with no 2 by 2 block in it.

3. Answers will vary. **Worked instance:** a row of 7 squares with 2 more
   squares under its first two. It shares 9 sides, and
   $36 - 18 = 18$.

4. **12:** only one figure, the 3 by 3 square. **20:** many — a row of 9,
   an L, a zigzag. **18:** many — any figure with exactly one 2 by 2 block
   and no other extra shared side. The reason is the rule above: the
   perimeter depends only on how many sides are shared.

### Fig. 6.18

**24 units**. The new square fills a corner space, so it touches the figure
along 2 of its sides. It adds 2 new sides and covers 2, and the perimeter
stays the same.

### Think and Reflect (after Fig. 6.18)

1. It depends on how many sides the new square shares with the figure.
   - **Shares 1 side:** it adds 3 sides and covers 1. The perimeter goes up
     by 2. Most places round the figure are like this.
   - **Shares 2 sides:** it adds 2 and covers 2. The perimeter stays the
     same, as in Fig. 6.18.
   - **Shares 3 sides:** it adds 1 and covers 3. The perimeter goes down by
     2. The figure has two such places, each a gap with a square on three
     sides of it.
