Problem 2

A rectangle is split into four smaller rectangles by one line across and one line down. The two top rectangles have areas 12 sq cm and 18 sq cm. The bottom-left rectangle has area 20 sq cm. The area of the bottom-right rectangle is

  1. 26 sq cm
  2. 30 sq cm
  3. 24 sq cm
  4. 36 sq cm
Solution

The two top rectangles have the same height. So the right one is as many times wider as its area is bigger: $18 \div 12 = 1\frac{1}{2}$ times. The two bottom rectangles have the same widths as the two top ones, and they share a height too.

Right width $= 1\frac{1}{2} \times$ left width

Bottom-right area $= 1\frac{1}{2} \times 20 = 30$ sq cm

The answer is (b). Option (a) adds 6, as 12 grew to 18, but areas grow by multiplying. Option (c) doubles 12, and option (d) doubles 18; neither uses the bottom row.

You can check the answer another way. Multiply the two areas on one diagonal, and then the two on the other: $12 \times 30 = 360$ and $18 \times 20 = 360$. In a rectangle cut by one line across and one line down, the two products always match, because each one is the same four side lengths multiplied together.

Problem 3

How many different rectangles with whole-number sides have an area of 36 square units? (A 4 by 9 rectangle and a 9 by 4 rectangle count as the same.)

  1. 4
  2. 5
  3. 9
  4. 10
Solution

List the pairs of whole numbers that multiply to 36, smaller number first.

$1 \times 36$, $2 \times 18$, $3 \times 12$, $4 \times 9$, $6 \times 6$

There are 5, option (b). A square is also a rectangle, so $6 \times 6$ counts. Option (a) leaves the square out. Option (c) counts each pair twice, once each way round, but $6 \times 6$ only once. Option (d) counts $6 \times 6$ twice as well.

The number of rectangles depends on how many ways the area can be written as one whole number times another. An area that is a prime number, such as 13 square units, gives only one rectangle, 1 by 13. An area that is a square number, such as 36, always has a square among its rectangles.
