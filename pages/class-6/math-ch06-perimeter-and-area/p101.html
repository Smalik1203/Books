Beyond the Book

The chapter gave you the perimeter and area of rectangles, squares and triangles. Every question below uses only those ideas. What is different is that no question tells you which idea to use. Try each question before you read what follows it.

A square and a rectangle have the same perimeter. The rectangle is 9 cm long and 5 cm wide. Which of the two has the bigger area?

First find the perimeter they share: $2 \times (9 + 5) = 28$ cm. The square has four equal sides, so each side is $28 \div 4 = 7$ cm, and its area is $7 \times 7 = 49$ sq cm. The rectangle’s area is $9 \times 5 = 45$ sq cm. So the square has the bigger area, even though the two perimeters are equal. The perimeter did not tell us the area. We had to find the sides first.

A square has an area of 64 sq cm. What is its perimeter?

The area does not give the perimeter straight away, but it gives the side. Which number times itself makes 64? It is 8, because $8 \times 8 = 64$. So each side is 8 cm, and the perimeter is $4 \times 8 = 32$ cm. We went back from the area to the side, and then forward to the perimeter.

The length of a rectangle is doubled, and its width stays the same. What happens to its area? What happens to its perimeter?

Try it with one rectangle, 5 cm by 3 cm. Its area is 15 sq cm and its perimeter is 16 cm. Double the length to get 10 cm by 3 cm. Now the area is 30 sq cm, which is double. But the perimeter is 26 cm, which is not double 16. The area is length times width, so doubling the length doubles it. The perimeter adds the two widths as well, and they did not change.

Riya says that every rectangle with an area of 36 sq cm has a perimeter of 24 cm. How can you show that she is wrong?

A square of side 6 cm has area 36 sq cm and perimeter 24 cm, so Riya’s claim is true for that one. Now take a rectangle 9 cm by 4 cm. Its area is also 36 sq cm, but its perimeter is $2 \times (9 + 4) = 26$ cm. One rectangle that does not fit is enough to show that the claim is wrong.
