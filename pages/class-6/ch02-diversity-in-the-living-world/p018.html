What Movement Tells Us

Animals move in many ways. They walk, run, hop, crawl, fly, swim and glide. Some do more than one, like a pigeon.

Sorting them this way shows us something interesting. Animals that are not alike at all may still move in the same way, using similar body parts. A housefly and a pigeon are very different creatures. But both have wings, and both fly. A fish and a dolphin look alike in water, even though a dolphin is not a fish.

That is a clue about something you will meet soon in this chapter. The way an animal is built has a lot to do with where it lives.

Movement is not the only way to group animals. We can also group them by what they eat, by where they live, or by what covers the body — scales, feathers, fur, or exposed skin. As with plants, the best grouping depends on the question we are asking.

Know a scientist

Janaki Ammal (1897–1984) was one of the first Indian women to work as a professional botanist. She studied sugarcane and worked on making plant varieties better. In 1952 she became Officer on Special Duty at the Botanical Survey of India and helped reorganise its work. In her eighties she joined the fight to save the forest at Silent Valley in Kerala. Her whole life was spent doing the two things this chapter is about — looking closely at plants, and finding order in what she saw.
