Investigate

You will need: old magazines or newspapers, scissors, glue, small cards.

1.

Cut out pictures of plants and animals. Paste one on each card. Try to make at least thirty cards in the class.

2.

Work in groups of five or six. Put all your cards together.

3.

Sort the pile into groups. You decide what the groups will be.

4.

Write down, in one line, the rule you used.

5.

Now mix all the cards again. Sort them a second time, using a completely different rule.

Compare both your sortings with another group's.

Did any two groups in the class use the same rule?

Safety: Use scissors carefully, with your teacher’s guidance.

A Grouping Needs a Clear Rule

Take one sorted group and check every card against its rule. Two different rules can both be useful, but a card can still be placed incorrectly.

Different groups may use size, food or surroundings. Check what the pictures actually show before using these features.

Some ways of grouping: Flowers · Stem · Food · Habitat

Choose a rule to suit your question, then apply it consistently. If a picture does not show the feature you need, mark it "not enough information". A picture without a visible flower does not prove that the plant never flowers.

A gardener comparing shade might group plants by height. Someone studying their flowers would need a different grouping. Explain what your rule helps you find out and what it leaves out.

Scientists also study groups whose members share several inherited features. Knowing one feature can then help us predict others. Later in this chapter, you will test such a connection.
