A habitat can be small: damp soil beneath a fallen log differs from the dry path beside it. Such differences help explain why nearby places can hold different living things.

Habitats can be sorted into two broad kinds.

Compare land and water habitats
Terrestrial habitatsAquatic habitats
  • Living things on land live in terrestrial habitats.
  • Forests, deserts, grasslands and mountains are terrestrial habitats. So is the ground under your feet.
  • Living things in water live in aquatic habitats.
  • Ponds, lakes, rivers and oceans are aquatic habitats.

Some animals use both land and water. Frogs belong to a group called amphibians; many amphibians spend part of their lives in each. But using both habitats does not make every animal an amphibian. A crocodile does so too, and it is a reptile.

When a Habitat Is Lost

Ask your parents, your grandparents, or an older neighbour one simple question. Which birds, insects or plants did they see often as children, but rarely see now?

Record what the person remembers and where they lived. Memory is a starting point for a question, not proof of a change in population. Look for dated records or photographs to compare.
