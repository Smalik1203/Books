Beyond the Book

The chapter showed you how to organise data with tally marks, and how to show it in pictographs and bar graphs. Every question below uses only those ideas. What is different is that no question tells you which idea to use. Try each question before you read on.

A row of a frequency table has the tally marks for 17. How many groups of five does it have, and how many marks are left over? What do the tally marks for 30 look like?

Each group of five has four marks, with a fifth drawn across them. Since $17 = 5 + 5 + 5 + 2$, there are 3 groups and 2 marks left over. For 30 no marks are left over, because $30 = 6 \times 5$. So there are 6 groups. Grouping in fives lets you count the groups quickly, and then add the last few marks.

In a pictograph, one symbol stands for 8 books. The row for Tuesday has 3 whole symbols and half a symbol. How many books is that? Could this pictograph show 27 books exactly?

Three whole symbols stand for $3 \times 8 = 24$ books. Half a symbol stands for half of 8, which is 4. So Tuesday’s row shows $24 + 4 = 28$ books. With whole and half symbols, this key can show 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, and so on, going up in fours. 27 is not in that list. Its row would need a piece of a symbol smaller than a half, and no reader could measure that.

A bar in a bar graph is 7 units tall and stands for 350 kites. What is the scale of the graph? How tall is the bar for 150 kites?

7 units stand for 350 kites. So 1 unit stands for $350 \div 7 = 50$ kites, and the scale is 1 unit of length for 50 kites. The bar for 150 kites is $150 \div 50 = 3$ units tall. You can find the scale from any bar whose number you know. Then the scale gives you every other bar.
