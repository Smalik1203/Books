First turn the bar into students: 7 units stand for $7 \times 5 = 35$ students. Then turn the students into symbols. 30 students make 3 whole symbols, and the other 5 students make half a symbol. So the row needs 3 and a half symbols. A bar graph and a pictograph can show the same data, and you go from one to the other through the numbers.

A shop sold 120 pencils on Monday, 260 on Tuesday, 340 on Wednesday and 90 on Thursday. Your graph paper is 20 squares tall. Can you use 1 square for 10 pencils? What scale would you choose?

With 1 square for 10 pencils, the bar for Wednesday needs $340 \div 10 = 34$ squares. That is taller than the paper. With 1 square for 20 pencils, the bars are 6, 13 and 17 squares tall, and the bar for Thursday is 4 and a half squares. The tallest bar is now 17 squares, so every bar fits. The largest value decides the scale. Find the largest value first, and then choose a scale that makes its bar fit.

Shop A sold 60 kites and Shop B sold 70 kites. In a bar graph of their sales, the numbers on the line going up start at 50, not at 0. How many times as tall as the bar for Shop A does the bar for Shop B look?

On this graph, the bar for Shop A rises only from 50 to 60, a height of 10. The bar for Shop B rises from 50 to 70, a height of 20. So Shop B’s bar looks twice as tall as Shop A’s. But Shop B sold only 10 more kites than Shop A, not twice as many. This is why the marks on the scale of a bar graph must start from 0. When they do not, a small difference looks like a big one.

A frequency table shows the goals a football team scored in its matches. It scored 0 goals in 3 matches, 1 goal in 5 matches, 2 goals in 4 matches and 3 goals in 2 matches. How many matches did the team play? How many goals did it score?

The number of matches is the frequency, so add the frequencies: $3 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 14$ matches. The goals need more care. The 5 matches with 1 goal give $1 \times 5 = 5$ goals, and the 4 matches with 2 goals give $2 \times 4 = 8$ goals. So the total is $0 + 5 + 8 + 6 = 19$ goals. This is the question Mayank got wrong about Bumrah’s wickets. Adding 0, 1, 2 and 3 gives only 6, because it forgets how many matches each number of goals happened in.

None of these questions needed a new idea. Each time, the answer came from asking what one mark, one symbol, one unit or one row stands for, and then counting with that.

A tally mark stands for one observation. A symbol stands for the number that the key gives. A unit of length stands for the number that the scale gives. A row of a frequency table stands for one value, repeated as many times as its frequency. When a graph or a table looks confusing, first find what each of its parts stands for.
