Summary
  1. Facts, numbers, measurements or observations that tell us something about the things they describe are called data. You can collect data by asking questions, by counting, by measuring or by watching.
  2. Data can be organised in a table using tally marks. The fifth mark is drawn across the first four, so the marks can be counted in fives.
  3. The number of times an item appears in the data is its frequency. A frequency table shows the frequency of every item. Putting data in ascending order helps you find its smallest and largest values.
  4. A pictograph shows data with pictures or symbols. Its key, or scale, says how many things one symbol stands for, and it must be given. With a key of 10, half a symbol stands for 5.
  5. A bar graph shows data with bars of the same width and equal gaps. The length or height of each bar gives its frequency, and the scale that turns length into number must be given.
  6. Choose the scale from the smallest and the largest values, so that the graph fits and is easy to read. The marks on the scale start from 0. With 1 unit for 10 runs, a score of 80 runs needs a bar $80 \div 10 = 8$ units tall.
  7. The colours, the pictures and the direction of the bars also decide how well a graph works. A height is best shown with bars that stand up, as in a column graph.
  8. A graph decorated with pictures and colour is called an infographic. Making a graph fancy can also make it mislead, so check every picture of data you draw or read.
  9. Reading pictographs and bar graphs carefully lets you understand data quickly, and draw conclusions from it.
