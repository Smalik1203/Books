## 8.2 Squares and Rectangles

### Think and Reflect (names of a square)

**$PQSR$** is not a name for square $PQRS$: it goes from $Q$ to $S$, and
those corners are not next to each other. $SPQR$, $RSPQ$ and $QRSP$ all follow
the corners in order.

### Think and Reflect (turned squares and rectangles)

1. **Yes.** Turning does not stretch or bend the square, so each angle is
   still $90^\circ$.
2. **Yes.** A square has its opposite sides equal (R1), because all its
   sides are equal, and all its angles are $90^\circ$ (R2).
3. **No.** A rectangle 6 cm long and 4 cm wide has all its angles
   $90^\circ$, but its sides are not all equal, so it is not a square.

### Exercise 8.2

1. *Answers will vary.* **What the drawing must show:** the rectangle of
   Fig. 8.9 with a square on each side, each square as long as the side it
   sits on and placed at its middle, so the four sit evenly round it.
2. **Only shape $A$ is a square.** Reading the corners off the dots:
   - $A$: every side goes 4 dots across and 4 dots up or down, and each
     turn is a right angle — a square;
   - $B$: every side goes 3 across and 4 up or down, so the sides are
     equal, but the corners are not right angles — not a square;
   - $C$: every side goes 5 across and 4 up or down — equal sides, no
     right angles — not a square;
   - $D$: two sides go 2 across and 1 down, two go 2 across and 4 down.
     The corners are right angles, but the sides are not all equal — a
     rectangle, not a square.
3. **Yes.** Two sides are equal when each goes the same number of dots
   across and the same number up or down, whichever way round. A corner is
   a right angle when the second side swaps the two numbers of the first
   and turns one of them the other way — 2 across and 1 down, then 1
   across and 2 up.
4. *Answers will vary.* **What the drawing must show:** three squares and
   three rectangles, each tilted, with every corner on a dot. **A worked
   instance:** the square with corners at $(0, 0)$, $(2, 1)$, $(1, 3)$ and
   $(-1, 2)$, counting dots across and up.
5. The first side goes 2 right and 1 down. The side at its end must go 1
   right and 2 up, or 1 left and 2 down.
   - **First square:** from the second dot go 1 right and 2 up; from the
     first dot go 1 right and 2 up. Join the four corners.
   - **Second square:** from each of the two dots go 1 left and 2 down.
   Counting from the first dot, the other corners are $(3, 1)$ and
   $(1, 2)$, or $(1, -3)$ and $(-1, -2)$.

## 8.3 Constructing Squares and Rectangles

### Think and Reflect (after the construction)

$PS$ must be 6 cm because **all the sides of a square are equal**, and
$PQ$ is 6 cm. In the drawing, **$RS$ measures 6 cm**, and **$\angle R$ and
$\angle S$ each measure $90^\circ$**.

### Exercise 8.3

1. **What the drawing must show:** $AB = 6$ cm, perpendiculars at $A$ and
   $B$, $AD = BC = 4$ cm marked with the compass, and $D$ joined to $C$.
   The check: $DC$ measures 6 cm and every angle measures $90^\circ$.
2. The same construction with 10 cm and 2 cm. $DC$ measures 10 cm and every
   angle $90^\circ$.
3. **No.** *Attempts will vary; every one ends the same way.* Draw the
   bottom side and a perpendicular at each end. The top side has to meet
   both uprights at right angles. It can do that only if the two uprights
   are the same height — a top side joining two different heights slopes,
   and a sloping side cannot be square to an upright line. With the two
   uprights equal, the top side runs level and is as long as the bottom
   side. So a 4-sided figure with four right angles always has its opposite
   sides equal: it is a rectangle.

## 8.4 An Exploration in Rectangles

### Think and Reflect (guesses)

*Guesses will vary.* $X$ and $Y$ are **closest** when they are level with
each other — the same distance from $A$ and from $B$ — and then
$XY = 7$ cm. They are **farthest** when they are at opposite corners: $X$ on
$A$ with $Y$ on $C$, or $X$ on $D$ with $Y$ on $B$. Then $XY$ is a diagonal,
about **8 cm 1 mm**.

### Table 8.17

| $X$ from $A$ | $Y$ from $B$ | $XY$ |
|---|---|---|
| 5 mm | 3 cm | about 7 cm 4 mm |
| 1 cm | 1 cm | 7 cm |
| 2 cm | 4 cm | about 7 cm 3 mm |

The shortest $XY$ is 7 cm, **the same as $AB$**. It happens whenever $X$
and $Y$ are the same distance from $A$ and $B$.

### Table 8.18, and the Think and Reflect after it

Every row gives **$XY = 7$ cm**.

1. In each row, **$XY$ is equal to $AB$**.
2. Each time, **$ABYX$ is a rectangle**.
3. The greatest distance between $X$ and $Y$ is **equal to $AC$**, and also
   **equal to $BD$** — about 8 cm 1 mm.

### Breaking rectangles

With $AF = 4$ cm, **$AC$ must be 8 cm**: the top side holds two squares,
each 4 cm wide.

### Think and Reflect (three squares)

1. **What the drawing must show:** a rectangle whose long side is three
   times its short side, with two lines across it at equal steps. **A
   worked instance:** short side 3 cm, long side 9 cm, lines at 3 cm and
   6 cm, each point carried across with the compass.
2. *Answers will vary.* A rectangle can be divided into two identical
   squares only if its long side is twice its short side, and into three
   only if it is three times. **A worked instance:** 5 cm by 3 cm cannot be
   divided into two identical squares; 6 cm by 3 cm cannot be divided into
   three.
3. **Twice** its width: each square is as wide as the rectangle, and two
   of them sit side by side.

   long side. **What the drawing must show:** the 8 cm by 4 cm rectangle
   and two lines across it, each 2 cm from a short side.
2. **Falling squares.** **What the drawing must show:** on the left, three
   4 cm squares, each one's top-right corner the next one's bottom-left
   corner, climbing to the right — the whole figure fits in a 12 cm square.
   On the right, squares of 7 cm, 5 cm and 3 cm climbing the same way — it
   fits in a 15 cm square.
3. **Shadings.** *Lengths will vary.* **What the drawing must show:** the
   big square, and every smaller shape in it constructed as a square —
   right angles by protractor and equal sides by compass — as in Fig. 8.24.
4. **A square with a hole.** The needle goes at the **centre of the
   square**, the point where its two diagonals cross. Draw the diagonals
   lightly to find it.
5. **A square with more holes.** Divide the square into four equal squares
   with two lines through its centre. Each circle has its needle at the

6. **A square with curves.** *Answers will vary.* All four needles go

   4 cm 5 mm outside the middle of its side.

## 8.5 Exploring Diagonals of Rectangles and Squares

### In the running text

- **The two diagonals are equal in length.**
- $g$ and $h$ are **not equal**, and $c$ and $d$ are **not equal**, unless
  the rectangle is a square.
- **The equal pairs** (Fig. 8.26): $a = d = e = h$ and $b = c = f = g$.
  The two angles at any one corner add up to $90^\circ$. In Fig. 8.26, the
  smaller angles are each about $27^\circ$ and the larger about $63^\circ$.

### Think and Reflect (splitting a corner equally)

Construct it as a **square**: a diagonal splits the opposite angles into
two equal parts exactly when all four sides are equal.

### Table 8.27

*Rows will vary* with the rectangles chosen. In every row, $a$, $d$, $e$
and $h$ are equal, $b$, $c$, $f$ and $g$ are equal, and $a + b = 90^\circ$.
**For a square, all eight angles are $45^\circ$.** **A worked instance:**
for a rectangle 8 cm by 4 cm, the diagonal makes about $27^\circ$ with the
long sides and $63^\circ$ with the short sides.

### Think and Reflect (rules)

1. *Wording will vary.* The rules: **the diagonals of a rectangle are
   equal**; **each diagonal splits a pair of opposite right angles into the
   same two parts**; **the two parts add up to $90^\circ$**; and **the parts
   are equal only when the rectangle is a square**.
2. *Answers will vary.* Measuring more rectangles makes a rule more
   believable but never proves it. **A worked instance** of a reason that
   covers every rectangle: turn the rectangle half a turn about its centre.
   It lands on itself, with $P$ on $R$ and $Q$ on $S$, so every angle at $P$
   lands on the matching angle at $R$ — which is why $c$ and $g$ must be
   equal, and $d$ and $h$.

### A rectangle from the angles at a diagonal

At Step 3, the other part of $\angle A$ is **$90^\circ - 60^\circ = 30^\circ$**.

### A rectangle from a side and a diagonal

The point where the circle crosses $l$ is **7 cm from $D$**, because it is on
the circle. $ABCD$ has both properties: **$AB$ measures 5 cm** and **$AD$
equals $BC$**, about 4 cm 9 mm, and every angle is $90^\circ$.

   which is 7 cm from $D$ at its nearest point, $C$. So the third corner

2. **The diagonal** is longer than each side, in every rectangle drawn.
