Using What You Know

The chapter gave you a ruler and a compass, and some ways to use them: circles, perpendiculars, lengths carried across, and points found where two arcs cross. Every question below uses only those ideas. What is different is that no question tells you which idea to use. Try each one before you read what follows it.

A rectangle 12 cm long and 4 cm wide is to be divided into identical squares that sit side by side in one row. How many squares are there, and how big is each one?

Each square must fill the width of the rectangle exactly, so each square has sides of 4 cm. Along the length, $12 \div 4 = 3$, so three squares of side 4 cm fit with nothing left over. A rectangle 10 cm long and 4 cm wide cannot be divided like this, because $10 \div 4$ is not a whole number. The width fixed the size of the squares, and after that there was only one thing left to check.

Two points $P$ and $Q$ are 6 cm apart. How many points are there that are 4 cm from $P$ and also 4 cm from $Q$?

All the points 4 cm from $P$ lie on a circle of radius 4 cm about $P$. All the points 4 cm from $Q$ lie on a circle of radius 4 cm about $Q$. The points you want are on both circles. Along $PQ$, each circle reaches 4 cm towards the other centre, and $4 + 4 = 8$ cm is more than the 6 cm gap, so the circles overlap and cross. Circles that cross do so at two points, one on each side of $PQ$. So there are 2 such points.

At 8 cm apart the reaches add up to exactly the gap, so the circles just touch, at the middle of $PQ$: only 1 point. At 10 cm apart there is none. Going from $P$ to $Q$ through such a point would take $4 + 4 = 8$ cm, and no way from $P$ to $Q$ is shorter than the straight 10 cm. Two arcs cross only when their radii add up to more than the distance between their centres. You never had to hunt for the points.

A rectangle is 10 cm long and 6 cm wide. A square with sides of 6 cm is drawn inside it, with the same centre. How far is each short side of the rectangle from the nearest side of the square?

The square is as wide as the rectangle, so it fills the width. Along the length, the square uses 6 cm of the 10 cm, which leaves $10 - 6 = 4$ cm. The two shapes have the same centre, so this 4 cm is shared equally between the two ends: 2 cm at each end. A rough diagram shows where the leftover length goes before you measure anything.
