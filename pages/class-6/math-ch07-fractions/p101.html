Using What You Know

The chapter gave you fractional units, the number line, mixed numbers, equivalent fractions and Brahmagupta’s method. Every question below uses only those ideas. What is different is that no question tells you which idea to use. Try each question before you read what follows it.

Which is greater, $\frac{5}{6}$ or $\frac{6}{7}$? Try to decide without finding a common denominator.

Look at what each fraction is missing. $\frac{5}{6}$ is $\frac{1}{6}$ short of a whole, and $\frac{6}{7}$ is $\frac{1}{7}$ short of a whole. Sharing among 7 gives a smaller piece than sharing among 6, so $\frac{1}{7} < \frac{1}{6}$. The fraction with the smaller gap is closer to 1, so $\frac{6}{7} > \frac{5}{6}$. You can check with the common denominator 42: $\frac{36}{42} > \frac{35}{42}$. We compared the missing pieces, not the fractions.

Rohan says that $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{2}{7}$. Without working out the sum, how can you tell that he is wrong?

When you add two amounts, the total is more than either amount. So the answer must be more than $\frac{1}{3}$. But $\frac{2}{7}$ is less than $\frac{1}{3}$: write both in twenty-firsts, and $\frac{6}{21} < \frac{7}{21}$. So Rohan’s answer cannot be right. He added the numerators and added the denominators. The correct sum is $\frac{3}{12} + \frac{4}{12} = \frac{7}{12}$.

A ribbon is $3\frac{1}{2}$ metres long. It is cut into pieces that are each $\frac{1}{4}$ metre long. How many pieces are there?

Count the ribbon in quarters. Each metre is 4 quarters, so 3 metres are 12 quarters. Half a metre is 2 more quarters. That makes $12 + 2 = 14$ quarters, so there are 14 pieces. Writing the length as $\frac{14}{4}$ says the same thing: 14 fractional units of $\frac{1}{4}$.

Find a fraction that lies halfway between $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$.

In sixths, the two fractions are $\frac{2}{6}$ and $\frac{3}{6}$. They sit next to each other, with no sixth between them. So use a smaller unit. In twelfths they are $\frac{4}{12}$ and $\frac{6}{12}$, and $\frac{5}{12}$ is exactly halfway. Cutting each unit into smaller equal parts always makes room for a fraction in between.
