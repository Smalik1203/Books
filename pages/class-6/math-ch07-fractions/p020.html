7.9A Pinch of History

In ancient India, a fraction was called bhinna in Sanskrit, which means broken. It was also called bhaga or ansha, which mean a part or a piece.

The way the whole world writes fractions today began in India. The Bakshali manuscript, from about 300 CE, writes one half as a 1 placed above a 2. This is very close to how we write it. Aryabhata (499 CE), Brahmagupta (628 CE), Sridharacharya (about 750 CE) and Mahaviracharya (about 850 CE) all wrote fractions this way. The line between the numerator and the denominator was added later, in the 12th century, by the Moroccan mathematician Al-Hassar. Over the next few hundred years, this way of writing spread to Europe and then everywhere.

The ancient Egyptians and Babylonians mostly used fractional units. They wrote other fractions as sums of different units, such as $\frac{19}{24} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{8}$. These sums are now called Egyptian fractions.

Fractions with any numerator, together with rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing them, were first used in India. The Sulba-sutras show that people knew rules for working with fractions even in Vedic times. Brahmagupta set them out in full, in the form we still use. They reached Europe through the Arabs, and were in common use there by the 17th century.

A puzzle with fractional units

Equal units make 1 easily, as in $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 1$. Can you make 1 from units that are all different?

Two different units will not work. The largest fractional unit is $\frac{1}{2}$, and $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1$. To make the two units different, one $\frac{1}{2}$ must be swapped for a smaller unit, and then the sum is less than 1.

Try three units. Start from $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = 1$. One $\frac{1}{3}$ must get bigger, and the only bigger unit is $\frac{1}{2}$. What is left is $1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$, which is $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4}$. Again one $\frac{1}{4}$ must get bigger, and the only choice other than $\frac{1}{2}$ is $\frac{1}{3}$.
