Think It Through

Does it stop along the same line as before? Try it four or five times. Before you push it a sixth time, say out loud where it will stop.

Let the magnet turn without touching the table, floor or stand. A twisted thread can pull it away from its resting direction. If the result changes between trials, check the suspension and remove nearby magnetic objects before deciding that the magnet behaves differently.

Repeated observations are useful because one trial can be disturbed. Draw the line after each trial in your notebook and compare your records.

Always the Same Line

Compare your repeated trials. Does the same end return to the same direction? A sticking support or a nearby magnet can change the result.

Now find out what that line is. If you know where the sun rises, you know roughly where east is. Face east and stretch out your arms. North is on your left. South is on your right.

Away from nearby magnets and iron objects, it settles along the magnetic north-south line. This is usually close to geographic north-south, but not exactly the same.

One end always turns towards the north. That end is the North pole of the magnet. The other end always turns towards the south. That is the South pole. Now the names make sense. They are named after the direction each end looks for.

But why does this happen at all?

How It Works

Earth has a magnetic field. It exerts a turning effect, called torque, on a freely suspended magnet. The magnet rotates into alignment with the local field; it is not being carried towards the North Pole. Earth does not contain a giant bar magnet.
