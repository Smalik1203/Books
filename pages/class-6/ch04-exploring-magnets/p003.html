A magnet’s shape gives a clue to its use. A long bar is easy to hold and turn. A ring can slide over a rod. Shape alone, however, does not tell you how strong a magnet is. Compare what the magnets can actually do.

4.1 Magnetic and Non-magnetic Materials

Bring a magnet near a steel pin and the pin jumps to it. Bring the same magnet near a rubber eraser and nothing happens.

So a magnet does not pull everything. It pulls only some things. Which ones?

Investigate

You will need: a magnet, and many small objects made of different materials — a pencil, an eraser, a steel paper clip, a plastic scale, an iron nail, a glass marble, a coin, a piece of cloth, a key, a rubber band, a piece of wood, a sharpener.

1.

Look at each object. Write down what material it is made of.

2.

Now predict. Before you test anything, write down whether you think it will stick to the magnet. Write Yes or No for every object.

3.

Only after all your predictions are written, bring the magnet near each object, one by one.

4.

Write down what really happened.

Safety: Keep small magnets and objects away from young children. Never put a magnet in your mouth.

Think It Through

Compare the two columns. Where were you right? Where were you wrong? Look only at the objects that stuck. What do those objects have in common?

Test a part of the object whose material you know. A pencil may have a metal ferrule, and a sharpener may have a steel blade inside a plastic body. If only one part sticks, record that part rather than calling the whole object magnetic.
