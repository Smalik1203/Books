3.5Pretty Palindromic Patterns

Look at these numbers: 66, 848, 575, 797 and 1111. What do they have in common? Read each one from left to right, and then from right to left. You get the same number both ways. A number like this is called a palindrome, or a palindromic number.

Palindromes

A number is a palindrome if it reads the same from left to right and from right to left. 575 and 1111 are palindromes, but 578 is not.

Palindromes from 1, 2 and 3

The numbers 121, 313 and 222 are 3-digit palindromes made from the digits 1, 2 and 3. A digit may be used more than once, and a palindrome does not have to use all three digits. But 123 is not a palindrome, because it reads 321 from right to left.

Write down every 3-digit palindrome that uses only the digits 1, 2 and 3. How can you be sure that you have found them all? You could start with the palindromes that begin with 1, then those that begin with 2.

Reverse and add

Here is a way to make palindromes. Start with a 2-digit number. Reverse its digits, and add the two numbers. If the answer is a palindrome, stop. If it is not, reverse the answer and add again. Keep going until you reach a palindrome.

Example 4

Start with 34, 29, 48 and 76. Reverse and add until you reach a palindrome.

Solution.

Step 1 $34 + 43 = 77$ a palindrome
Step 2 $29 + 92 = 121$ a palindrome
Step 3 $48 + 84 = 132$, then $132 + 231 = 363$ one step more
Step 4 $76 + 67 = 143$, then $143 + 341 = 484$ one step more
Answer 34 and 29 reach a palindrome in one step. 48 and 76 need two steps.

Try the same steps with other 2-digit numbers. You will find that some numbers need many more steps than these.
