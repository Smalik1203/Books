Why do the Kaprekar steps need a number whose digits are not all the same? Try the steps on 5555.

With 5555, the largest and the smallest numbers you can make are both 5555. So $C = 5555 - 5555 = 0$, and every round after that gives 0 again. You never reach 6174. With any other 4-digit number, A is bigger than B, so C is never 0.

Which starting number from 1 to 9 takes the most steps to reach 1 under the Collatz rule?

Work out each sequence and count the steps. 8 takes 3 steps and 6 takes 8 steps, but 7 takes 16. Then look at 9. Its sequence goes 9, 28, 14, 7, and from 7 it follows the same 16 steps as before. So 9 takes $3 + 16 = 19$ steps, the most of all. Once a sequence reaches a number you have already worked out, you do not need to do those steps again.

Which starting numbers reach 1 in exactly 5 steps under the Collatz rule?

Work backwards from 1. The only number that gives 1 is 2, the only one that gives 2 is 4, and the only one that gives 4 is 8, because 4 could also come from 1, and 1 is already the end. 8 comes only from 16. But 16 comes from 32, and also from 5, because $3 \times 5 + 1 = 16$. So both 32 and 5 take exactly 5 steps. Going backwards showed us 5, which you might never find by trying numbers forwards.

Two players take turns adding 1 or 2 to a total that starts at 0. The first player to reach 10 wins. You go second, and the first player says 2. What should you say, and why?

Work backwards from 10. If you say 7, the other player can only reach 8 or 9, and then you reach 10. In the same way, saying 4 lets you reach 7, and saying 1 lets you reach 4. So the winning numbers are 1, 4, 7 and 10, each 3 more than the one before. The first player has said 2, so say 4, by adding 2. Whatever the other player adds next, 1 or 2, you add the number that makes 3 in that turn. That brings you to 7, and then to 10. The key is the number 3, which is 1 more than the most a player can add.

None of these questions needed a new rule. Each needed you to see which idea from the chapter was hiding inside it. When a question looks new, ask what you already know that could help. Often it is one plain fact, such as what a neighbour is or what a palindrome must look like.
