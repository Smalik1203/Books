## 5.1 Common Multiples and Common Factors

### Think and Reflect (after Exercise 5.1)

**Meena's game.** The pair was 4 and one other number. Nobody ever said
*vada* alone, so every multiple of the other number was also a multiple of
4 — which means the other number is itself a multiple of 4.

Of 2, 3, 5, 8 and 10, only **8** works. With 2 the players would say vada at
2, 6, 10, …; with 3 at 3, 6, 9, …; with 5 at 5, 10, 15, …; with 10 at 10,
30, 50, …. With 8 every multiple of 8 is a multiple of 4, so 8 is never said
alone — it is always idli-vada.

### Exercise 5.1

The game is played with 3 and 5, so idli-vada is said on the common
multiples of 3 and 5, which are the multiples of 15.

1. The 10th idli-vada is at $10 \times 15 = 150$.

2. From 1 to 90:
   - **idli** (multiples of 3): $90 \div 3 = 30$ times;
   - **vada** (multiples of 5): $90 \div 5 = 18$ times;
   - **idli-vada** (multiples of 15): $90 \div 15 = 6$ times.

3. Up to 900 each count is ten times as large: **300**, **180** and **60**.
   Nothing about the game changes — only how far it is played.

4. Fig. 5.1 puts the multiples of 3 in one circle and the multiples of 5 in
   the other, so the overlap holds exactly the numbers said as idli-vada.
   **What the drawing must show,** for a game played up to 60: the left
   circle holds 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 21, 24, 27, 33, 36, 39, 42, 48, 51, 54, 57;
   the right circle holds 5, 10, 20, 25, 35, 40, 50, 55; and the overlap
   holds **15, 30, 45, 60**.

**The activity that follows** (other pairs, up to 60). The overlap in each
case holds the common multiples:

| pair | first common multiple | in the overlap, up to 60 |
|---|---|---|
| 2 and 5 | 10 | 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 |
| 3 and 7 | 21 | 21, 42 |
| 4 and 6 | 12 | 12, 24, 36, 48, 60 |

For 2 and 5, and for 3 and 7, the first common multiple is the product,
because each pair is co-prime. For 4 and 6 it is 12, not 24, because they
share the factor 2. This is the observation Section 5.3 returns to.

## 5.2 Prime Numbers

### Think and Reflect (after the figs)

1. From 21 to 30 the primes are **23 and 29** — **2** of them.
2. The rest are composite: 21, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30 — **8** of them.

### Think and Reflect (after the Sieve)

1. Each step removes the **multiples of the number just circled**, other
   than that number itself. Step 2 removes the multiples of 2, step 3 the
   multiples of 3, and so on. Every number removed has the circled number as
   a factor, so it has more than two factors and is composite.

2. Every number left at the end is prime because **anything composite would
   have been crossed out**. A composite number has a factor other than 1 and
   itself, and its smallest such factor is a prime. That prime is circled at
   some point, and the number is crossed out as one of its multiples. So
   nothing composite can survive.

## 5.3 Co-prime Numbers

### Think and Reflect (which pairs are safe)

A pair is safe when its only common factor is 1.

- (i) 15 and 39: **not safe.** 3 is a common factor.
- (ii) 4 and 15: **safe.** $4 = 2 \times 2$ and $15 = 3 \times 5$.
- (iii) 18 and 29: **safe.** 29 is prime and is not a factor of 18.
- (iv) 20 and 55: **not safe.** 5 is a common factor.

### Example 1 (worked in the book)

15 and 39 are not co-prime; 4 and 9 are co-prime.

### Think and Reflect (after Example 1)

1. Co-prime or not:
   - (a) 18 and 35: **co-prime.** $18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3$,
     $35 = 5 \times 7$.
   - (b) 15 and 37: **co-prime.** 37 is prime and is not a factor of 15.
   - (c) 30 and 415: **not co-prime.** Both are multiples of 5.
   - (d) 17 and 69: **co-prime.** $69 = 3 \times 23$, and 17 is not among
     its factors.
   - (e) 81 and 18: **not co-prime.** $81 = 3^4$ and
     $18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3$ share 3; their largest common factor is 9.

2. The first common multiple is the **product** exactly when the two
   numbers are **co-prime**. *Answers will vary:* 3 and 5 are co-prime and
   their first common multiple is $3 \times 5 = 15$; 4 and 6 are not, and
   their first common multiple is 12, which is less than $4 \times 6 = 24$.
   When the numbers share a factor, that factor is counted once instead of
   twice, so the first common multiple falls short of the product.

### Think and Reflect (thread art)

The thread reaches every peg exactly when the number of pegs and the
thread-gap are **co-prime**.

- (a) 15 pegs, gap 10: **no.** They share 5, and the thread reaches only
  3 pegs.
- (b) 10 pegs, gap 7: **yes.** 10 and 7 are co-prime.
- (c) 14 pegs, gap 6: **no.** They share 2, and the thread reaches 7 pegs.
- (d) 8 pegs, gap 3: **yes.** 8 and 3 are co-prime.

**What the drawing must show:** in (b) and (d) a single closed path passing
through every peg; in (a) a triangle using 3 of the 15 pegs, and in (c) a
seven-pointed figure using 7 of the 14.

In Fig. 5.7 the same rule holds: 12 pegs with a gap of 4 reaches 3 pegs,
13 with a gap of 3 reaches all 13, 16 with a gap of 6 reaches 8, and 24 with
a gap of 6 reaches 4.

## 5.4 Prime Factorisation

### Think and Reflect (splitting 60 and 48)

1. Starting from $60 = 6 \times 10$: $6 = 2 \times 3$ and $10 = 2 \times 5$,
   so $60 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5$. Starting from
   $60 = 4 \times 15$: $4 = 2 \times 2$ and $15 = 3 \times 5$, so
   $60 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5$ again. **Yes — the same primes both
   times**, two 2s, one 3 and one 5.

2. Guna will end with **four 2s**, the same as Anshu. The prime
   factorisation of 48 does not depend on where the splitting starts:

## 5.5 Divisibility Tests

### Think and Reflect (10, 5 and 2)

All three statements are **true**.

- A number is divisible by 10 exactly when it ends in 0, because 10 is the
  base of our numerals: every number is a whole number of tens plus its
  units digit.
- Divisible by 5 exactly when it ends in 0 or 5, for the same reason — every
  multiple of 10 is a multiple of 5, and the units digit must add a multiple
  of 5.
- Divisible by 2 exactly when it ends in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8, since every
  multiple of 10 is even and the units digit must be even too.

### Think and Reflect (divisibility by 4)

All three statements are **true**, and the second and third together are
what makes the test work.

- 100 is a multiple of 4, so every multiple of 100 is too. A number is its
  hundreds part plus the number made by its last two digits, and the
  hundreds part is always a multiple of 4. So only the last two digits
  decide it — both ways round.

### Think and Reflect (divisibility by 8)

All three statements are **true**, for the same reason one step further:
1000 is a multiple of 8, so every multiple of 1000 is too, and only the
number made by the last three digits decides.

## 5.6 Fun with Numbers

### Think and Reflect (special numbers)

*Answers will vary, and more than one reason is right for each number.* The
question asks for at least one reason per box that uses an idea from this
chapter — being prime, being composite, being a multiple of something, or
having a particular factor.

**A worked instance,** for the four numbers the chapter itself discusses —
9, 16, 25 and 43:

- 9 is the only multiple of 3, and the only single-digit number;
- 16 is the only even number, and the only multiple of 4;
- 25 is the only multiple of 5;
- 43 is the only prime, the only number that is not a square, and the only
  one greater than 40.

A teacher marking thirty different answers should accept any reason that is
true of exactly one number in the box and false of the other three.

### Think and Reflect (the prime puzzles)

Each puzzle has exactly one solution.

**A** — rows 105, 20, 30; columns 28, 125, 18:

$$\begin{matrix} 7 & 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \end{matrix}$$

**B** — rows 8, 105, 70; columns 30, 70, 28:

$$\begin{matrix} 2 & 2 & 2 \\ 3 & 5 & 7 \\ 5 & 7 & 2 \end{matrix}$$

**C** — rows 63, 27, 190; columns 45, 42, 171:

$$\begin{matrix} 3 & 7 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 & 3 \\ 5 & 2 & 19 \end{matrix}$$

**D** — rows 343, 66, 44; columns 28, 154, 231:

$$\begin{matrix} 7 & 7 & 7 \\ 2 & 11 & 3 \\ 2 & 2 & 11 \end{matrix}$$

**Where to start, and why.** Take the row or column whose number has the
fewest prime factors, because that leaves the fewest choices.

- In **A**, the middle column is 125, and $125 = 5 \times 5 \times 5$, so
  all three squares in it are 5. Then the middle row is
  $20 = 2 \times 5 \times 2$, so its other two squares are 2 and 2.
- In **B**, the top row is $8 = 2 \times 2 \times 2$, so all three squares
  in it are 2.
- In **C**, the middle row is $27 = 3 \times 3 \times 3$, so all three are
  3; and 19 is prime, so the 190 row and the 171 column must meet at 19.
- In **D**, the top row is $343 = 7 \times 7 \times 7$, so all three are 7.

Multiplying along every row and every column is the check.
