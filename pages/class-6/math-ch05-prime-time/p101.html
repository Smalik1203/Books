Using What You Know

The chapter gave you common multiples and common factors, primes, prime factorisation, co-prime numbers and quick tests for divisibility. Every question below uses only those ideas. What is different is that no question tells you which idea to use. Try each question before you read what follows it.

The idli-vada game is played with 6 and 8, from 1 to 100. How many times is idli-vada said?

Idli-vada is said on the common multiples of 6 and 8. You might start from $6 \times 8 = 48$, but check the smaller numbers first. The multiples of 8 are 8, 16, 24, … and 24 is already a multiple of 6. So the first common multiple is 24, and the common multiples up to 100 are the multiples of 24: 24, 48, 72 and 96. Idli-vada is said 4 times. Counting only the multiples of 48 would give 2, and would miss 24 and 72. This happened because 6 and 8 are not co-prime: they share the factor 2.

Lakshmi has between 40 and 50 laddoos. She can pack all of them in boxes of 4, with none left over. She can also pack all of them in boxes of 6, with none left over. How many laddoos does she have?

The number of laddoos is a common multiple of 4 and 6. Start with the fewer choices. Between 40 and 50, the multiples of 4 are 44 and 48. Now check each one for 6. 44 is not a multiple of 6, but $48 = 6 \times 8$. So Lakshmi has 48 laddoos. We did not need to list the multiples of 6 at all. 42 is a multiple of 6, but it was never a choice, because it is not a multiple of 4.

Ravi says that 391 is a prime, because it is not divisible by 2, 3 or 5. Is he right?

Ravi has shown only that 2, 3 and 5 are not factors of 391. A composite number can still have larger prime factors. So keep trying the next primes. Dividing 391 by 7 leaves 6, by 11 leaves 6, and by 13 leaves 1. Dividing by 17 leaves nothing: $391 = 17 \times 23$. So 391 is composite, and Ravi is wrong. One factor was enough to show that his claim was false.
