What this chapter showed
  1. A common multiple of two numbers is a multiple of both of them. A common factor of two numbers is a factor of both of them.
  2. If a number is divisible by another number, the second number is a factor of the first. For example, 4 is a factor of 12, because $12 \div 4 = 3$.
  3. A prime number has exactly two factors, 1 and itself, like 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11. A composite number has more than two factors, like 4, 6, 8 and 9. The number 1 is neither prime nor composite.
  4. The Sieve of Eratosthenes finds the primes by crossing out the multiples of each prime in turn.
  5. Every number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes. This is its prime factorisation. For example, $84 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 7$. A number has only one prime factorisation, apart from the order of the primes.
  6. Two numbers are co-prime if their only common factor is 1. To check, find their prime factorisations. If no prime appears in both, they are co-prime. Otherwise, they are not.
  7. One number is a factor of another if its prime factorisation is included in the prime factorisation of the other.
  8. Looking at the last digits gives quick tests for divisibility by 10, 5 and 2, by 4 (the last two digits) and by 8 (the last three digits).

When you split a number into factors, circle each prime as soon as you reach it. When every branch ends in a circle, the circled primes are the whole prime factorisation.
