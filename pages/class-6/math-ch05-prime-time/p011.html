Prime factorisation

Writing a number as a product of primes is called its prime factorisation. The primes in it are the number’s prime factors.

The prime factorisation of 56 is $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 7$, and the prime factors of 56 are 2 and 7.

Every number greater than 1 has a prime factorisation. The method is always the same: keep splitting the composite numbers into factors, until only primes are left. A prime such as 7 cannot be split at all, so its prime factorisation is just 7. The number 1 has no prime factorisation, because no prime divides it.

A number can often be split in more than one way. For example, splitting 63 in different ways gave us $3 \times 3 \times 7$ and also $3 \times 7 \times 3$. Are these different? Not really. Both use the same primes, 3 and 7. And in both, 3 appears twice and 7 appears once.

Fig. 5.9 splits 36 in four different ways. In all four, the primes at the ends are two 2s and two 3s. Multiply them back to check that you get 36 each time.

Only one prime factorisation

However you split a number, you always reach the same prime factorisation. The primes may come in a different order, but the same primes appear, the same number of times.
