5.4Prime Factorisation

Checking whether two numbers are co-prime

The teacher asks the class, “Are 56 and 63 co-prime?” Anshu and Guna know that if the two numbers have a common factor other than 1, they are not co-prime. They start to check.

Anshu: I can write $56 = 14 \times 4$ and $63 = 21 \times 3$. So 14 and 4 are factors of 56, and 21 and 3 are factors of 63. There are no common factors, so the numbers are co-prime.

Guna: Wait. I can also write $56 = 7 \times 8$ and $63 = 9 \times 7$. So 7 is a factor of both numbers. They are not co-prime.

Guna is right, because 7 is a common factor. So where did Anshu go wrong? Writing $56 = 14 \times 4$ shows that 14 and 4 are factors of 56. It does not show all the factors of 56. The same is true for 63.

Try another pair: 80 and 63. Each of them can be split into two factors in many ways.

$80 = 40 \times 2 = 20 \times 4 = 10 \times 8 = 16 \times 5 = \ ???$

$63 = 9 \times 7 = 3 \times 21 = \ ???$

The ??? says that there may be more ways. Take $80 = 16 \times 5$ and $63 = 9 \times 7$. These show no common factor. Can we say that 80 and 63 are co-prime? Anshu’s mistake shows that we cannot, because some other way of splitting might show a common factor. We need a method that checks every possibility.

Prime factorisation

Take the number 56. It is composite, because $56 = 4 \times 14$. So 4 and 14 are both factors of 56. Now take one of them, say 14. It is composite too: $14 = 2 \times 7$. So $56 = 4 \times 2 \times 7$. And 4 is composite: $4 = 2 \times 2$. So

$56 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 7$

Every factor in this line is 2 or 7, and both are primes. A prime cannot be split into smaller factors, so we stop. Fig. 5.8 draws the same steps as a tree, with the primes at the ends circled.
