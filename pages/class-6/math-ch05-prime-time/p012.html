Does the order matter?

Fig. 5.10 shows 30 small cubes built into a block. Use it to explain why $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$, whichever order you multiply 2, 3 and 5 in.

The block can be counted in different ways. It is 2 layers of 15 cubes, so $30 = 2 \times 15 = 2 \times (3 \times 5)$. It is also 5 slices of 6 cubes, so $30 = 5 \times 6 = 5 \times (2 \times 3)$. Every way counts the same cubes, so every order gives 30.

Numbers can be multiplied in any order, and the answer stays the same. That is why two 2s and two 3s give 36, whichever order they are multiplied in. In a later class you will study this under the names commutativity and associativity of multiplication.

So the order does not matter. We usually write the primes from the smallest to the largest. For example, $225 = 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$ and $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$.

Prime factorisation of a product

To find a prime factorisation, we first write the number as a product of two factors, such as $72 = 12 \times 6$. Then we find the prime factorisation of each factor: $12 = 2 \times 2 \times 3$ and $6 = 2 \times 3$. Putting the two together gives the prime factorisation of 72.

$72 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 2 \times 3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3$

Multiply the primes to check that you get 72 back. How many times does each prime appear in the prime factorisation of 72? Compare this with how many times it appears in the factorisations of 12 and 6 put together.

Example 2

Find the prime factorisation of 180, starting from $180 = 18 \times 10$.

$18 = 2 \times 3 \times 3$ and $10 = 2 \times 5$

$180 = 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 2 \times 5 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5$

Check by multiplying back: $2 \times 2 = 4$, then $4 \times 9 = 36$, then $36 \times 5 = 180$. If you had started from $180 = 12 \times 15$ instead, you would reach the same five primes.
