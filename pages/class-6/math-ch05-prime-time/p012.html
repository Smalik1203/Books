Only one prime factorisation

However you split a number, you always reach the same prime factorisation. The primes may come in a different order, but the same primes appear, the same number of times.

Does the order matter?

Fig. 5.10 shows 30 small cubes built into a block. Use it to explain why $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$, whichever order you multiply 2, 3 and 5 in.

The block can be counted in different ways. It is 2 layers of 15 cubes, so $30 = 2 \times 15 = 2 \times (3 \times 5)$. It is also 5 slices of 6 cubes, so $30 = 5 \times 6 = 5 \times (2 \times 3)$. Every way counts the same cubes, so every order gives 30.

Numbers can be multiplied in any order, and the answer stays the same. That is why two 2s and two 3s give 36, whichever order they are multiplied in. In a later class you will study this under the names commutativity and associativity of multiplication.

So the order does not matter. We usually write the primes from the smallest to the largest. For example, $225 = 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$ and $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$.
