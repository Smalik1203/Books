Beyond the Book

The chapter showed you some number sequences, how to draw them, and how two sequences can be connected. Every question below uses only those ideas. What is different is that no question tells you which idea to use. Try each question before you read what follows it.

Without adding the numbers one by one, find $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 + 13 + 15$.

These are odd numbers, starting from 1. Count them: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15. There are 8. The L-shapes of Fig. 1.6 put the first 8 odd numbers together into a square of 8 rows of 8 dots, so the sum is $8 \times 8 = 64$. Notice that we did not add anything. We only counted how many odd numbers there were.

Riya says that the sum of the first 12 odd numbers is 124. Without adding them, how can you tell that she has made a mistake?

Adding the odd numbers from 1 always gives a square number. But 124 is not a square number: $11 \times 11 = 121$ and $12 \times 12 = 144$, and nothing in between is a square. So 124 cannot be the answer, whatever the correct answer is. (It is $12 \times 12 = 144$.) One fact about the answer was enough to show that her answer was wrong.

What is $1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1$?

The counting numbers go up to 7 and then back down. Adding up and then down gives a square number, and here the square has 7 rows. So the sum is $7 \times 7 = 49$. The biggest number in the middle of the sum tells you the size of the square.

What comes next: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, …? Draw the numbers as dots to find out.

Try drawing each number as a rectangle of dots. 2 is 1 row of 2 dots. 6 is 2 rows of 3. 12 is 3 rows of 4. 20 is 4 rows of 5, and 30 is 5 rows of 6. Each rectangle is one row taller and one dot wider than the one before, so the next is 6 rows of 7, which is 42.
