1.4Sequences That Are Connected

Sometimes two different number sequences are connected in a way you would not expect. A connection like this is exciting, because it shows that two sequences you thought were separate are really telling the same story. Here is an example. Start adding the odd numbers, one at a time:

$1 = 1$

$1 + 3 = 4$

$1 + 3 + 5 = 9$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 = 36$

The answers are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36. These are the square numbers. Will this keep happening, however many odd numbers you add? And if it does, why?

Think about it before you read on. Here is a clue: a square number can be drawn as a square of dots. Can you split a square of dots into groups of 1, 3, 5, 7, … dots? Draw a square of 16 dots and try it.

A picture that explains it

Try it yourself first, with buttons, seeds or small stones, before you look at the picture. Fig. 1.6 shows one way. Start with 1 dot in a corner. Around it, add an L-shape of 3 dots, and you have a square of 4 dots. Around that, add an L-shape of 5 dots, and you have a square of 9. Go on with 7, then 9, then 11. Each L-shape has 2 more dots than the one inside it, so the L-shapes use the odd numbers in order, and after each one the dots make a square.
