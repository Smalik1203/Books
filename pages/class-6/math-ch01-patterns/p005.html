1.4Sequences That Are Connected

Sometimes two different number sequences are connected in a way you would not expect. A connection like this is exciting, because it shows that two sequences you thought were separate are really telling the same story. Here is an example. Start adding the odd numbers, one at a time:

$1 = 1$

$1 + 3 = 4$

$1 + 3 + 5 = 9$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 = 36$

The answers are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36. These are the square numbers. Will this keep happening, however many odd numbers you add? And if it does, why?

Think about it before you read on. Here is a clue: a square number can be drawn as a square of dots. Can you split a square of dots into groups of 1, 3, 5, 7, … dots?

A picture that explains it

Fig. 1.5 shows one way. Start with 1 dot in a corner. Around it, add an L-shape of 3 dots. Around that, add an L-shape of 5 dots, then 7, then 9, then 11. Each L-shape has 2 more dots than the one inside it, so the L-shapes use the odd numbers in order.

The whole picture is a square with 6 rows of 6 dots, which is 36 dots. So

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 = 36$

You can draw the same picture for a square of any size. Each new L-shape makes the next square. That is why adding the odd numbers from 1 always gives a square number, and why the pattern never stops.

Odd numbers and squares

Adding the odd numbers in order, starting from 1, always gives a square number.
