Summary
  1. Mathematics looks for patterns, and for the reasons why those patterns happen. Patterns are all around us: in nature, in our homes and schools, and in the movement of the sun, the moon and the stars.
  2. A list of numbers made by following a rule is called a number sequence. The part of mathematics that studies patterns in whole numbers is called number theory.
  3. Some important number sequences are the counting numbers, the odd numbers, the even numbers, the triangular numbers (1, 3, 6, 10, …), the square numbers (1, 4, 9, 16, …), the cube numbers (1, 8, 27, 64, …), the Virahānka numbers (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, …) and the powers of 2 and of 3.
  4. Drawing a sequence as a picture, such as a triangle or a square of dots, often shows why it follows its rule.
  5. Sequences are connected to each other. Adding the odd numbers in order from 1 gives the square numbers: $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$. Adding a triangular number to the next one also gives a square number: $6 + 10 = 16$.
  6. A sum that goes up to a number and back down to 1 is that number times itself. For example, $1 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 9$, which is $3 \times 3$.
  7. Shapes can make sequences too: regular polygons, complete graphs, stacked squares, stacked triangles and the Koch snowflake.
  8. Counting the parts of these shapes gives number sequences we already know. The sides of regular polygons give the counting numbers from 3, the lines of complete graphs give the triangular numbers, and stacked squares and stacked triangles both give the square numbers.
