Think It Through

Write about a problem you worked out for yourself recently. It does not have to be a science problem. List the steps you took, in order. Was there a point where you had to change your first guess?

Everyone Does This

Scientists follow the scientific method for a living. But nobody owns it, and it is used every day by people who would never call themselves scientists.

A cook lifts the lid off the idli batter in the morning and finds it flat instead of risen. She asks why. Was the kitchen too cold overnight? Was the batter ground too thin? She will not solve it by worrying — she will change one thing tomorrow and watch what happens.

A tailor's machine starts skipping stitches. He does not replace the whole machine. He checks the needle first, then the thread tension, then the bobbin — one at a time, because changing everything at once would tell him nothing.

An electrician is called about a tubelight that flickers for a long time before it steadies. Is it the tube, the starter, or the wiring? Each of those can be tested separately.

None of them are in a laboratory. All of them are doing exactly what step three and step four describe: guess, then test — and crucially, test one thing at a time, so that the result actually means something.

A useful record is specific. Describe which part of a puddle’s edge moved and when. A sketch can preserve details that memory loses. Keep what you observed separate from your explanation of why it happened.
