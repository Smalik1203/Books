3. Guess a possible answer.

4. Test the guess, through an experiment or through further careful observation.

5. Examine the result and decide whether it actually answers your question.

That list looks tidier than real life ever is. Very often you finish step five only to discover you are back at step three with a fresh guess. Sometimes the test throws up something you were not even looking for, and that becomes a better question than the one you started with.

This is one useful route through an investigation, not a compulsory order for every kind of science. What matters is that the guess comes before the test, and that you accept the result even when it is not the one you wanted.

An observation describes what you notice: the clock hands have not moved. An explanation suggests a reason: the cell may be used up. Keeping these separate helps you test your idea honestly.

For a fair comparison, change one thing while keeping other conditions similar. If you replace the cell and free the hands together, you will not know which change mattered. Repeating a check can help you spot a result that happened only by chance.
