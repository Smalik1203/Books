$$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \;=\; 2ab + c^2$$

Take $2ab$ off both sides — the same to both sides, as always — and what is left is the theorem.

The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem

In a right-angled triangle with short sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, $\ a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

The square on the hypotenuse is the two squares on the other sides together. It is named for Baudhāyana, who stated it around 800 BCE, and for Pythagoras, who stated it in Greece some three centuries later.

Read the picture rather than the letters and the theorem is a statement about paper: cut up the two smaller squares, and the pieces will cover the larger one exactly. The algebra is only a way of saying that without scissors.

2.6Finding a Side You Cannot Reach

The theorem is chiefly used backwards. It relates three lengths, so knowing any two of them gives the third — and the third is very often the one you cannot get a tape measure to.

One thing has to be settled before any arithmetic: which side is the hypotenuse. It is the one opposite the right angle, and it is always the longest of the three. If you know it, you subtract; if you are looking for it, you add.

Example 1

A right-angled triangle has short sides $9$ cm and $12$ cm. Find its hypotenuse.

Solution.

Step 1 $c^2 = 9^2 + 12^2 = 81 + 144 = 225$ the theorem
Step 2 $c = \sqrt{225} = 15$ cm take the square root
Answer The hypotenuse is $15$ cm.

A check costs a second: $15$ is longer than both $9$ and $12$, as the hypotenuse must be, and shorter than $9 + 12$, as any side of a triangle must be.
