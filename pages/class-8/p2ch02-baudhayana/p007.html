The shape left in the middle is a square. Its four sides are all hypotenuses, so they are equal; and its corners are right angles, because at every point where two triangles meet along an edge, the two angles they contribute are the triangle's two acute angles, which add to $90^\circ$ — leaving $90^\circ$ for the corner between them.

Now count the area of the big square twice.

as one square $(a+b)^2$
as its pieces $4 \times \tfrac12 ab + c^2 = 2ab + c^2$

The two counts are of the same paper, so they are equal. And Chapter 4 of Part I cut a rectangle in two to establish what $(a+b)^2$ comes to:

$$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \;=\; 2ab + c^2$$

Take $2ab$ off both sides — the same to both sides, as always — and what is left is the theorem.

The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem

In a right-angled triangle with short sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, $\ a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.

The square on the hypotenuse is the two squares on the other sides together. It is named for Baudhāyana, who stated it around 800 BCE, and for Pythagoras, who stated it in Greece some three centuries later.
