2.5Two Different Squares

So far we have joined a square to a copy of itself. Baudhāyana next asks a harder question. Given two squares of different sizes, can we build one square whose area is the two areas together? His answer is the rule this chapter is named after.

Śulbasūtra 1.12

A cord stretched along the diagonal of a rectangle makes a square equal to the squares made by its length and its breadth together.

In other words: take the two side lengths, and make a right-angled triangle with them as its two short sides. The square on the hypotenuse of that triangle has the same area as the two squares on the short sides together.

The counts work for $3$, $4$ and $5$. That is a good sign, but it proves nothing about other triangles. Why should it be true for every right-angled triangle?
