Some powers were settled early. Fermat himself left a proof for fourth powers, and Leonhard Euler gave one for cubes in 1770. But proving the claim one power at a time could never finish, because there are endlessly many powers. A proof had to cover every power together, the small ones and the large ones.

Fermat's Last Theorem
  • For $n$ bigger than $2$, $x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no solution in counting numbers.
  • Fermat stated it in the 1630s, and Andrew Wiles proved it in 1994.

Think and Reflect
  1. A square can be split into two squares in endlessly many ways, but a cube cannot be split into two cubes at all. One example of $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$ would have settled Fermat's question. Why did three hundred years of not finding one settle nothing?
  2. Check that $3^3 + 4^3 + 5^3 = 6^3$. Does this go against Fermat's claim? Give a reason.

Wiles's proof is more than a hundred pages long. It uses mathematics from the twentieth century. So very few people now believe that Fermat really had a proof. He was a careful mathematician and was not often wrong. But he wrote the note for himself, in a margin. An argument that convinces its writer late at night is not always a correct argument.

Still, his note was not wasted. For three centuries, people who tried to settle his claim built new mathematics on the way, and many of the tools Wiles used came from that work. A hard question can be useful long before anyone answers it.

The two ends of this chapter teach us something. Baudhāyana's rule was proved in a paragraph, by cutting a square into four triangles, and it has stood for twenty-eight centuries. Fermat's question looked like the same kind of question, and it took more than three hundred years. We cannot tell which questions are easy just by looking at them. Here the difference is a picture. Two squares can be cut up and laid over a third square, and there is no such picture for cubes. That is one reason why two questions that look so alike turned out so different.
