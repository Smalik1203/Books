Example 4

The diagonals of a rhombus are $24$ and $70$ units long. Find the length of a side.

Part I's Chapter 4 established that the diagonals of a rhombus cut each other in half and cross at right angles. So each side of the rhombus is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose short sides are half of each diagonal.

Solution.

Step 1 half-diagonals $12$ and $35$ halve each diagonal
Step 2 $s^2 = 12^2 + 35^2 = 144 + 1225 = 1369$ the theorem
Step 3 $s = \sqrt{1369} = 37$ take the square root
Answer Each side of the rhombus is $37$ units.

All four sides come to $37$, which they had to: the four right triangles have the same two short sides.

2.6.1 Is this triangle right-angled?

The theorem also runs the other way, and that is the half of it the altar builders relied on. If the three sides of a triangle happen to make $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ true, then the angle opposite the longest side is a right angle. So a triangle can be tested for squareness with nothing but a tape measure and some arithmetic.

$13$, $84$, $85$ $169 + 7056 = 7225 = 85^2$ — right-angled
$9$, $12$, $16$ $81 + 144 = 225$, but $16^2 = 256$ — not right-angled

The second is a near miss on purpose: $9$, $12$, $15$ would have been a right angle, and lengthening the longest side by one centimetre opens the corner past $90^\circ$. It is the same test a carpenter uses on a door frame — measure across the two diagonals, and if they do not agree the frame is not square.

2.6.2 Using it twice: the diagonal of a box

A hall is $4$ m by $3$ m on the floor and $12$ m to the ceiling. How far is it from one bottom corner to the opposite top corner — the longest straight line that fits inside the room?

The floor diagonal is found first. It is then one short side of a second right triangle whose other short side is the height, and whose hypotenuse is the line we want.

across the floor $d^2 = 4^2 + 3^2 = 25$
then up $D^2 = d^2 + 12^2 = 25 + 144 = 169$, so $D = 13$ m

The floor diagonal itself was never needed, only its square — which is why the whole thing collapses into one line, $\ D^2 = l^2 + b^2 + h^2$. Two Baudhāyana triples went by on the way: $(3,4,5)$ across the floor and $(5,12,13)$ up to the corner.
