## 2.1 Introduction

### Think and Reflect

1. $6 \times 6 = 36$ cm². The first square has area 9 cm², and
   $36 \div 9 = 4$: **four** copies fit.
2. Side 2 cm gives 4 cm²; side 3 cm gives 9 cm². $9$ is **more than double**
   $4$ (it is $2.25$ times as large).

## 2.2 The Square on the Diagonal

The question *Why should that be?* is answered in the running text that
follows it: the tilted square is four copies of a triangle, and two copies
make the first square.

### Think and Reflect

1. **50 cm²** and **100 cm²**: each square has twice the area of the one
   before it.
2. **Upright.** The squares turn upright, tilted, upright, tilted, upright,
   so every odd-numbered square is upright.

## 2.3 Halving a Square

### Think and Reflect

1. Each join halves the area, so the last square is **a quarter** of the first.
2. Folding lays each corner exactly on a quarter of the inner square, with no
   gap and no overlap. That is true of every square, whatever its size; a
   drawing only shows one square, and only as well as it was measured.

### Exercise Set 2.1

1. Square on the diagonal: $2 \times 50 = 100$ cm². Midpoint square:
   $50 \div 2 = 25$ cm².
2. **6, 12, 24, 48** cm².
3. $5 \times 5 = 25$, so the area is multiplied by **25**. A third of the side
   multiplies the area by $\tfrac13 \times \tfrac13 = \tfrac19$.
4. The square on the diagonal of a square of side 6 cm has area
   $2 \times 36 = 72$ cm²; a square of side 8 cm has area 64 cm². **The square
   on the diagonal is larger, by 8 cm².**
5. $400 \div 2 = 200$ cm²; then $200 \div 2 = 100$ cm².
6. The areas are 16 cm², $2 \times 16 = 32$ cm² and $2 \times 32 = 64$ cm². The
   sides of the first and third are 4 cm and 8 cm. **The middle square** has
   side $\sqrt{32}$ cm, which is not a whole number: $5^2 = 25 < 32 < 36 = 6^2$.

## 2.4 How Long Is the Diagonal?

### Think and Reflect

1. $1.7^2 = 2.89$ and $1.8^2 = 3.24$, so $1.7 < \sqrt{3} < 1.8$.
2. $7^2 = 49 < 50 < 64 = 8^2$, so the diagonal lies between **7 cm and 8 cm**.

## 2.5 Two Different Squares

### Think and Reflect

1. $6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2$, so the hypotenuse is 10.
2. $a^2 + a^2 = c^2$, so $c^2 = 2a^2$: the square on the diagonal of a square
   has **twice its area** (§2.2).

## 2.6 Finding a Side You Cannot Reach

Worked examples: Example 2 **34 cm**; Example 3 **28 m**; Example 4 the wall
is **12 m** and the ladder 13 m; Example 5 **29 cm**.

### Think and Reflect

1. **No.** The ladder is the hypotenuse, the longest side, so the height it
   reaches is less than 10 m whenever its foot is away from the wall.
2. Each diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose short sides are
   the length and the breadth, so both equal $\sqrt{l^2 + b^2}$.

## 2.7 Triples

### Think and Reflect

1. **No.** $13^2 + 14^2 = 169 + 196 = 365$, but $15^2 = 225$.
2. $25 - 1 = 24$, $2 \times 5 \times 1 = 10$, $25 + 1 = 26$: $(24, 10, 26)$.
   It is **not primitive**: it is 2 times $(12, 5, 13)$.

### Exercise Set 2.3

1. (a) $324 + 576 = 900 = 30^2$: **a triple**. (b) $36 + 49 = 85$, but
   $9^2 = 81$: **not a triple**. (c) $784 + 2025 = 2809 = 53^2$: **a triple**.
   (d) $196 + 2304 = 2500 = 50^2$: **a triple**.
2. **(15, 20, 25), (7, 24, 25), (10, 24, 26), (20, 21, 29), (18, 24, 30).**
   The primitive ones are **(7, 24, 25)** and **(20, 21, 29)**.
3. $m = 8$, $n = 1$: **(63, 16, 65)**; $3969 + 256 = 4225 = 65^2$.
   $m = 7$, $n = 4$: **(33, 56, 65)**; $1089 + 3136 = 4225 = 65^2$.
4. $(5a)^2 + (5b)^2 = 25(a^2 + b^2) = 25c^2 = (5c)^2$.
5. If $a$ and $b$ are both odd, $a^2$ and $b^2$ are both odd, and two odd
   numbers add to an even number. So $c^2$ is even, and then $c$ is even. So
   the three cannot all be odd.

## 2.8 One Question, Three Hundred Years

### Think and Reflect

1. One example would have been enough to show that such numbers exist. But
   there are endlessly many numbers to try, and a search only ever checks some
   of them. Not finding an example says nothing about the numbers nobody
   tried, so only a proof could settle it.
2. $27 + 64 + 125 = 216 = 6^3$. **No**: Fermat's claim is about the sum of
   *two* cubes, and this is a sum of three.
