The builders had a use for them. Tie knots in a cord to mark twelve equal lengths. Peg it out on the ground as a triangle with sides $3$, $4$ and $5$. By the second half of the theorem, the angle between the sides of $3$ and $4$ is a right angle. This is how the altars got square corners.

One entry in the list is not like the others. Divide $(15, 36, 39)$ by $3$ and you get $(5, 12, 13)$, which is already on the list. It is the same triangle, made three times as large.

So if $(a, b, c)$ is a triple, so is $(ka, kb, kc)$ for any counting number $k$:

work it out $(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2a^2 + k^2b^2 = k^2(a^2 + b^2)$
and $a^2+b^2 = c^2$ $= k^2c^2 = (kc)^2$

So $(3,4,5)$ alone gives $(6,8,10)$, $(9,12,15)$, $(30,40,50)$ and as many more as you like. There is no last Baudhāyana triple. A triple whose three numbers have no common factor other than 1 is called primitive. Five of the six triples in the list are primitive. The sixth is $(5,12,13)$ enlarged.

Where do the primitive triples come from? Take any two counting numbers $m$ and $n$, with $m$ the larger, and build three numbers from them.

A machine for making triples

For every choice of $m > n$, the numbers $m^2 - n^2$, $\ 2mn$ and $\ m^2 + n^2$ make a Baudhāyana triple. With $m=2, n=1$ it gives $(3, 4, 5)$; with $m=3, n=2$, $\ (5, 12, 13)$; with $m=4, n=1$, $\ (15, 8, 17)$; with $m=4, n=3$, $\ (7, 24, 25)$; and with $m=6, n=1$, $\ (35, 12, 37)$.

That is every primitive triple in the list, from one rule. We can check the rule with the identities from Part I:

$$(m^2-n^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 = m^4 - 2m^2n^2 + n^4 + 4m^2n^2 = m^4 + 2m^2n^2 + n^4 = (m^2+n^2)^2.$$
