Summary
  1. About 800 BCE, Baudhāyana's Śulbasūtra gave the geometry for building fire altars. One of its questions is how to make a square of twice the area of a given square.
  2. Doubling the side of a square does not double its area. It makes the area four times as large.
  3. The square built on the diagonal of a square has twice its area. Joining the midpoints of the sides of a square makes a square of half its area. A square of area 50 cm² has a midpoint square of 25 cm².
  4. In a right-angled triangle, the side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse. It is always the longest side.
  5. The diagonal of a square of side 1 is $\sqrt{2}$. It can be trapped between decimals by squaring: $1.41 < \sqrt{2} < 1.42$. Its decimal never ends or repeats.
  6. The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem: in a right-angled triangle with short sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. It is proved by fitting four copies of the triangle into a square of side $a + b$.
  7. Knowing two sides of a right-angled triangle gives the third. Add the squares to find the hypotenuse, and subtract to find a short side: short sides 16 and 30 give a hypotenuse of 34.
  8. The theorem also works the other way. If $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, the angle opposite $c$ is a right angle.
  9. Three whole numbers with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ form a Baudhāyana triple, such as $(3, 4, 5)$. Multiplying a triple by any counting number gives another triple. A triple with no common factor other than 1 is primitive. The numbers $m^2 - n^2$, $2mn$ and $m^2 + n^2$ always make a triple.
  10. Fermat claimed that $x^n + y^n = z^n$ has no solution in counting numbers when $n$ is bigger than 2. Andrew Wiles proved this in 1994.
