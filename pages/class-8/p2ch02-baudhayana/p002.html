2.2The Square on the Diagonal

Draw a square and one of its diagonals. Then build a new square that stands on that diagonal. The new square is tilted. The claim is that its area is exactly twice the area of the first square. Why should that be?

Extend two sides of the first square: the bottom side and the right-hand side. These two lines are the diagonals of the new square, and they cross at a corner of the old one.

Each of the four triangles has two short sides equal to a side of the first square, with a right angle between them. So the four triangles are copies of one another. Each one is half of the first square, because the diagonal of the first square cuts it into two of them.

the tilted square four of the same triangles

Twice as many pieces of the same size make twice the area. This proves Baudhāyana's rule. Notice that nothing was measured. The argument counts pieces, and counting is exact, while measuring is never quite exact.
