2.2The Square on the Diagonal

Draw a square, draw one of its diagonals, and then build a new square standing on that diagonal. The new square is tilted, and the claim is that it holds exactly twice the area of the one you started with. Why should that be?

Extend two sides of the original square — the bottom one and the right-hand one — and something helpful happens. Those two lines are the diagonals of the new square, and they cross at a corner of the old one.

Every one of those four triangles has the same two short sides, each a side of the original square, and a right angle between them. So they are all copies of one another, and each is half the original square, because the original square's own diagonal cuts it into two of them.

the tilted square four of the same triangles

Twice as many pieces of the same size is twice the area, and Baudhāyana's rule is proved. Nothing was measured to prove it, which is worth noticing: the argument counts pieces, and counting is exact where measuring never quite is.

And once you can double a square you can double it again, and again. Each new square stands on the diagonal of the last, turning a half-turn of the compass each time and holding twice the area.
