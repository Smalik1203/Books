Beyond the Book

Three things came out of this chapter and nothing below needs a fourth. The square on the diagonal of a square is twice it. In any right-angled triangle $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, which finds a third length from two, and run backwards tests whether a corner is square at all. And whole-number triples like $(3,4,5)$ are rare enough to be worth recognising on sight.

What most of the questions below have in common is that they do not mention a right angle, and in most of them you have to make one before there is anything to calculate. Try each before reading what follows it.

A square has an area of $200$ cm2. The midpoints of its four sides are joined to make a smaller square. How long is that smaller square's side?

Joining the midpoints of a square's sides halves the area, which Section 2.3 settled with the four folded corners. So there is no need to find the first square's side at all.

inner area $200 \div 2 = 100$ cm2
inner side $\sqrt{100} = 10$ cm

Going the other way round — finding the outer side as $\sqrt{200}$ and then working with it — gives the same answer through a number that does not come out exactly. The tidy route was to halve the area first.

That is worth generalising. Areas in this chapter behave themselves and lengths often do not, so a question about lengths is frequently easier answered by working in areas for as long as possible and taking the square root once, at the end.

A rectangle measures $11$ cm by $60$ cm. Find its diagonal, and then the area of a square built on that diagonal.

A rectangle's diagonal cuts it into two right-angled triangles whose short sides are the rectangle's own sides.

diagonal $d^2 = 11^2 + 60^2 = 121 + 3600 = 3721$, so $d = 61$ cm
square on it $61^2 = 3721$ cm2
