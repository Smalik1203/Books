Using What You Know
The chapter's ideas, in questions that do not say which one to use.

Three things came out of this chapter and nothing below needs a fourth. The square on the diagonal of a square is twice it. In any right-angled triangle $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, which finds a third length from two, and run backwards tests whether a corner is square at all. And whole-number triples like $(3,4,5)$ are rare enough to be worth recognising on sight.

What the questions below have in common is that none of them mentions a right angle, and in most of them you have to make one before there is anything to calculate. Try each before turning the page.

A square has an area of $200$ cm2. The midpoints of its four sides are joined to make a smaller square. How long is that smaller square's side?

A rectangle measures $8$ cm by $15$ cm. Find its diagonal, and then the area of a square built on that diagonal.

A triangle has sides $12$, $16$ and $20$. Find its area.

A ladder $25$ m long leans against a wall and reaches $24$ m up it. Its foot slips out a further $8$ m. How far down the wall has the top come?

Start with a square of area $1$ and build on its diagonal, then on the new square's diagonal, and so on, ten times over. What is the area of the last square, and how long is its side?
