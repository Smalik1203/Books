2.8One Question, Three Hundred Years

There are endlessly many whole numbers whose squares add up to a square. In the 1630s, Pierre de Fermat, a French lawyer who studied mathematics, was reading a book about these triples. He asked the next question: can it be done with cubes? Are there counting numbers with $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$? What about fourth powers, or hundredth powers?

He decided that the answer was no. He claimed that

$$x^n + y^n = z^n$$

has no solution in counting numbers when $n$ is bigger than $2$. In the margin of his book he wrote that he had a wonderful proof of this, but that the margin was too small to hold it.

Nobody ever found his proof. Mathematicians tried for more than three hundred years and failed. The claim came to be called Fermat's Last Theorem. For most of that time it was not really a theorem. It was a guess that nobody could prove or disprove.

In 1963, a ten-year-old boy in England named Andrew Wiles read about it in a library book. He decided that he would prove it. He did prove it, in 1994, at the age of forty-one. He used mathematics that did not exist when he was ten.
