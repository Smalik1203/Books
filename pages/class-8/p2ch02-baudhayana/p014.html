There is a lesson in the two ends of this chapter. Baudhāyana's rule was proved in a paragraph by cutting a square into four triangles, and it has stood for twenty-eight centuries. Fermat's looked like the same kind of question and took three hundred years. Which questions are easy is not something you can tell by looking at them — and the difference here is a picture: two squares can be cut up and laid over a third, and there is no such picture for cubes.

What this chapter established
  1. Areas do not follow lengths. Doubling the side of a square gives four times the area; halving the side gives a quarter.
  2. The square built on the diagonal of a square has twice its area. Its own diagonals cut it into four triangles, and two of them make the original square.
  3. Read backwards, that picture halves a square: join the midpoints of the four sides, and the four corners fold in to fill the middle exactly.
  4. The diagonal of a square of side $1$ is $\sqrt{2}$, since the square on it has area $2$. Squaring traps that number as narrowly as you like — $1.414 < \sqrt2 < 1.415$ — and the decimal never ends.
  5. The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem: with short sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, $\ a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. Four copies of the triangle in a square of side $a+b$ prove it, because counting that square two ways gives $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 2ab + c^2$.
  6. The hypotenuse is opposite the right angle and is always the longest side. Add the two squares to find it, subtract to find either of the others — and the theorem run backwards tests whether a triangle is right-angled at all.
  7. Three whole numbers with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ are a Baudhāyana triple. Any multiple of a triple is a triple, so there are endlessly many, and $m^2-n^2$, $2mn$, $m^2+n^2$ makes one for every $m > n$.
  8. The same question about cubes has no answers at all. Fermat claimed that in the 1630s and it took until 1994 to prove.
