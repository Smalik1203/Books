work it out $(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2a^2 + k^2b^2 = k^2(a^2 + b^2)$
and $a^2+b^2 = c^2$ $= k^2c^2 = (kc)^2$

So $(3,4,5)$ alone gives $(6,8,10)$, $(9,12,15)$, $(30,40,50)$ and as many more as you like: there is no last Baudhāyana triple. A triple that is not a magnification of a smaller one — one whose three numbers share no common factor — is called primitive. Five of Baudhāyana's six are primitive, and the sixth is $(5,12,13)$ in disguise.

Where do the primitive ones come from? Take any two whole numbers $m$ and $n$ with $m$ the larger, and build three numbers from them.

A machine for making triples

$m^2 - n^2$, $\ 2mn$, $\ m^2 + n^2$ is a Baudhāyana triple for every choice of $m > n$. With $m=2, n=1$ it makes $(3, 4, 5)$; with $m=3, n=2$, $\ (5, 12, 13)$; with $m=4, n=1$, $\ (15, 8, 17)$; with $m=4, n=3$, $\ (7, 24, 25)$; and with $m=6, n=1$, $\ (35, 12, 37)$.

That is every primitive triple on Baudhāyana's list, out of one rule. And the rule can be checked in three lines with the identities from Part I:

$$(m^2-n^2)^2 + (2mn)^2 = m^4 - 2m^2n^2 + n^4 + 4m^2n^2 = m^4 + 2m^2n^2 + n^4 = (m^2+n^2)^2.$$
