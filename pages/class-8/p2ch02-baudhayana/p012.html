2.8One Question, Three Hundred Years

There are endlessly many whole numbers whose squares add to a square. In the 1630s the French lawyer and mathematician Pierre de Fermat, reading a book about exactly these triples, asked the next question along: could it be done with cubes? Are there whole numbers with $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$? With fourth powers? With hundredth?

He decided the answer was no — that

$$x^n + y^n = z^n$$

has no solution in whole numbers at all once $n$ is bigger than $2$. In the margin of his book he wrote that he had found a truly marvellous proof of it, and that the margin was too narrow to hold it.

Nobody ever found that proof. Mathematicians tried for three hundred years and failed, and the claim came to be called Fermat's Last Theorem. For most of that time it was not a theorem at all. It was a guess that nobody could settle either way.

In 1963 a ten-year-old in England named Andrew Wiles read about it in a library book, and decided he would be the one to prove it. He proved it in 1994, at forty-one, using mathematics that had not existed when he was ten.

Squares can be split into two squares in endlessly many ways, and cubes — it turns out — cannot be split into two cubes at all.

A single example of $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$ would have settled Fermat's question for ever. Why did three hundred years of not finding one settle nothing?

Wiles's proof runs to more than a hundred pages and rests on mathematics invented in the twentieth century, which is why hardly anyone now believes Fermat had a proof of his own. He was a careful mathematician and was rarely wrong, but the note was written in a margin for nobody but himself, and an argument that convinces its author late at night is not always an argument.

There is a lesson in the two ends of this chapter. Baudhāyana's rule was proved in a paragraph by cutting a square into four triangles, and it has stood for twenty-eight centuries. Fermat's looked like the same kind of question and took three hundred years. Which questions are easy is not something you can tell by looking at them. The difference here is a picture: two squares can be cut up and laid over a third, and there is no such picture for cubes.
