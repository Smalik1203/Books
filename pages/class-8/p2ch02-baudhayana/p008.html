Here is an argument that settles it. Take a right-angled triangle with short sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$. Draw a square of side $a + b$. Place four copies of the triangle in its corners, each turned through a right angle from the last.

The shape in the middle is a square. Its four sides are hypotenuses, so they are equal. At each of its corners, the two acute angles of the triangle add up to $90^\circ$, which leaves $90^\circ$ for the corner. Now find the area of the big square in two ways.

as one square $(a+b)^2$
as its pieces $4 \times \tfrac12 ab + c^2 = 2ab + c^2$

Both are the area of the same square, so they are equal. In Chapter 6 of Part I we found what $(a+b)^2$ is by cutting a square into four pieces:

$$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \;=\; 2ab + c^2$$

Take $2ab$ away from both sides. What is left is the theorem.

The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem
  • In a right-angled triangle, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, where $c$ is the hypotenuse.
  • The square on the hypotenuse has the area of the other two squares together.

The theorem is named after Baudhāyana, who stated it about 800 BCE, and after Pythagoras, who stated it in Greece about three hundred years later.
