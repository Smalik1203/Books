2.7Triples

Most right-angled triangles have an awkward side. Two sides of $1$ give a hypotenuse of $\sqrt{2}$; two sides of $1$ and $2$ give $\sqrt5$. So the triangles whose three sides are all whole numbers are worth collecting, and the Śulbasūtra collects them.

$(3, 4, 5)$ $9 + 16 = 25$
$(5, 12, 13)$ $25 + 144 = 169$
$(8, 15, 17)$ $64 + 225 = 289$
$(7, 24, 25)$ $49 + 576 = 625$
$(12, 35, 37)$ $144 + 1225 = 1369$
$(15, 36, 39)$ $225 + 1296 = 1521$

Three whole numbers with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ are called a Baudhāyana triple, and also a Pythagorean triple.

They were not collected out of curiosity. A cord knotted into twelve equal lengths and pegged out as $3$, $4$ and $5$ makes a right angle on the ground — no instrument, no drawing, and no doubt about it, because the theorem says the angle between the $3$ and the $4$ can only be a right angle if the third side comes to $5$. That is how the altars were squared.

Look down the list again and one entry is not like the others. Divide $(15, 36, 39)$ by $3$ and it becomes $(5, 12, 13)$, which is already there. It is the same triangle drawn three times as large.
