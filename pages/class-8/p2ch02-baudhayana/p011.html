2.6.1 Is this triangle right-angled?

The theorem also works the other way, and the altar builders used this half of it. If the three sides of a triangle make $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ true, then the angle opposite the longest side is a right angle. So we can test a corner with a measuring tape and some arithmetic.

$13$, $84$, $85$ $169 + 7056 = 7225 = 85^2$ — right-angled
$10$, $24$, $27$ $100 + 576 = 676$, but $27^2 = 729$ — not right-angled

The second one is a near miss. Sides $10$, $24$ and $26$ would make a right angle. Making the longest side one unit longer opens the corner wider than $90^\circ$. A carpenter tests a door frame in the same spirit: she measures the two diagonals, and if they are not equal, the frame is not square.

2.6.2 Using it twice: the diagonal of a box

A room is $12$ m long, $9$ m wide and $8$ m high. How far is it from one bottom corner to the opposite top corner? This is the longest straight line that fits inside the room.

First find the diagonal of the floor. It is one short side of a second right triangle. The other short side is the height, and the hypotenuse is the line we want.

across the floor $d^2 = 12^2 + 9^2 = 225$
then up $D^2 = d^2 + 8^2 = 225 + 64 = 289$, so $D = 17$ m

We never needed the floor diagonal itself, only its square. So the whole working fits in one line: $\ D^2 = l^2 + b^2 + h^2$. On the way we met two sets of whole numbers that fit the theorem, $(9, 12, 15)$ and $(8, 15, 17)$. Section 2.7 gives such sets a name.

Using the theorem
  • To find the hypotenuse, add the two squares. To find a short side, subtract.
  • If $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, the angle opposite $c$ is a right angle.
