Two poles, $12$ m and $7$ m tall, stand upright $12$ m apart on level ground. How far apart are their tops?

Nothing in the question is a right-angled triangle until you draw one. Run a horizontal line from the top of the shorter pole across to the taller one: the line between the tops is now a hypotenuse.

across $12$ m, the distance between the poles
up $12 - 7 = 5$ m, the difference in height
so $144 + 25 = 169$, and the tops are $13$ m apart

The heights themselves never mattered, only the difference between them. Two poles of $107$ m and $102$ m would give the same answer.

A rope hangs from the top of a flagpole, and $2$ m of it lies on the ground. Pulled tight, its end touches the ground $10$ m from the foot of the pole. How tall is the pole?

The rope does not change length, so pulled tight it is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose short sides are the pole and the $10$ m along the ground. Call the height $h$; the rope is $h + 2$.

the theorem $h^2 + 10^2 = (h+2)^2$
expand $h^2 + 100 = h^2 + 4h + 4$
take $h^2$ off $100 = 4h + 4$, so $h = 24$

The pole is $24$ m tall and the rope $26$ m long, and $(10, 24, 26)$ is $(5, 12, 13)$ doubled. The unknown was squared on both sides, as the depth of the lake was in Section 2.6, and once the two squares were taken off what was left was an equation of the kind solved in Class 7.

A square field has an area of one hectare — $10{,}000$ m2. Walking along the diagonal instead of round two sides saves how many metres, and what percentage of the walk is that?

A hectare is $10{,}000$ m2, so the field is $100$ m square. Two sides come to $200$ m; the diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle with two sides of $100$.

diagonal $d^2 = 100^2 + 100^2 = 20{,}000$, so $d \approx 141.4$ m
saving $200 - 141.4 = 58.6$ m
as a percentage $\dfrac{58.6}{200} \times 100 \approx 29\%$

The diagonal of any square is $\sqrt2$ times its side, so cutting the corner off a square always saves the same share of the walk — about $29\%$ — whether the field is a hectare or a hundred. That is why the worn path across a lawn is always the diagonal.
