Compare that with $1.4142135\ldots$: he is right to five decimal places, which is an error of about two parts in a million. On an altar ten metres across, that is a fifth of a hair's breadth.

2.5Two Different Squares

Everything so far has combined a square with a copy of itself. Baudhāyana asks the harder question next: given two squares of different sizes, can you build a single square whose area is the two of them together? His answer is the sentence this chapter is named after.

Śulbasūtra 1.12

The area of the square produced by the diagonal is the sum of the areas of the squares produced by the two sides.

Unpacked: take the two side lengths, and make a right-angled triangle with them as its two short sides. Then the square on that triangle's hypotenuse — the diagonal of the rectangle those two sides make — is exactly the two squares together.

That the counts come out right for $3$, $4$ and $5$ is encouraging and proves nothing. Why should it be true for every pair of sides?

Here is an argument that settles it, and it needs one square, four copies of one triangle, and an identity you already have.

Take the right-angled triangle with short sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$. Draw a square of side $a + b$, and lay four copies of the triangle into its corners, each turned a quarter-turn from the last.
