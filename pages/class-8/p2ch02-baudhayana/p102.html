diagonal $d^2 = 11^2 + 60^2 = 121 + 3600 = 3721$, so $d = 61$ cm
square on it $61^2 = 3721$ cm2

The second answer was already written down in the first line, because $d^2$ is the area of the square on $d$. That is the theorem's real shape: it is about areas, and the lengths are what we take out of it afterwards.

A triangle has sides $12$, $16$ and $20$. Find its area.

An area needs a base and a perpendicular height, and this triangle offers neither — unless it happens to be right-angled. Test it.

test $12^2 + 16^2 = 144 + 256 = 400 = 20^2$
so area $= \tfrac12 \times 12 \times 16 = 96$

It is $(3,4,5)$ multiplied by $4$, which is worth spotting: the two short sides are the base and the height of each other, so no extra line has to be drawn.

A ladder $25$ m long leans against a wall and reaches $24$ m up it. Its foot slips out a further $8$ m. How far down the wall has the top come?

The ladder does not change length, so both positions are right-angled triangles with the same hypotenuse. Find where the foot started.

foot at first $25^2 - 24^2 = 625 - 576 = 49$, so $7$ m out
foot after $7 + 8 = 15$ m out
new height $625 - 225 = 400$, so $20$ m up

The top has come down $4$ m. Notice that the foot moved $8$ m and the top moved half that: a ladder that is nearly upright loses very little height when its foot slips, and one that is nearly flat loses a great deal. The two movements are not equal and never are, because the theorem ties the squares of the two distances together and not the distances themselves.

Start with a square of area $1$ and build on its diagonal, then on the new square's diagonal, and so on, ten times over. What is the area of the last square, and how long is its side?

Each step doubles the area, so after ten steps the area has been multiplied by $2$ ten times over.

area $2^{10} = 1024$
side $\sqrt{1024} = 32$

The side came out a whole number because $1024$ happens to be a perfect square.
