Set B, 3. Option (d), $169$, stops one step early: it is $D^2$, not $D$. The theorem always hands back a square first, and forgetting to take the root is the commonest way to lose a mark on a question you understood.

Set B, 9. Option (c), $100$ m, is the diagonal itself rather than the saving. The question asked what walking the diagonal saves, which is $140 - 100$. Reading the last line of a question again before writing the answer costs five seconds.

Set B, 10. Option (c), $9$, is what happens to the areas. Trebling every side treble the hypotenuse — that is exactly the $(ka, kb, kc)$ rule from Section 2.7 — while the squares on the sides grow nine times. Both facts are true and they answer different questions.

Set C, 1. Option (d) is the careful-looking answer and it is wrong. A triangle's area does need a height, but $8$, $15$, $17$ satisfies $a^2+b^2=c^2$, so the two short sides are perpendicular and each is the other's height. Testing for a right angle is worth doing before deciding a question cannot be answered.

Set C, 7. Options (a) and (b) each generalise from one example — $(3,4,5)$ has an odd $c$, $(6,8,10)$ an even one. One example never settles an always, and two examples that disagree settle it at once.

What to carry forward

Find the right angle before you find anything else. Most of these questions arrive without one drawn. A pole and its shadow, two walls of a room, the diagonals of a rhombus, a horizontal line between the tops of two poles — the triangle is usually one line away, and until it is on the page there is nothing to calculate.

The theorem is about squares, not lengths. It hands you $c^2$, and the length comes afterwards. That is why areas in these questions often come out exactly while lengths do not, and why the tidy route to a half-area square is to halve the area rather than to chase the side.

Recognise the small triples. $(3,4,5)$, $(5,12,13)$, $(8,15,17)$, $(7,24,25)$ and their multiples turn up constantly, and spotting $(12,16,20)$ as four times the first saves the arithmetic entirely. When the numbers are not one of these, expect a root that does not come out — and leave it as $\sqrt{\,\cdot\,}$ rather than rounding early.
