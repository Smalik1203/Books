3.5A Ratio on a Map

Look in the corner of any map and you will find a ratio, usually with a $1$ on the left and something enormous on the right: $1 : 60{,}00{,}000$. It is called the representative fraction, and it says that one unit of anything on the paper stands for sixty lakh of the same unit on the ground.

One centimetre on that map is $60{,}00{,}000$ cm across the country. Divide by $100$ for metres and by another $1{,}000$ for kilometres, and one centimetre turns out to be $60$ km.

Example 7

On a map with a representative fraction of $1 : 60{,}00{,}000$, two towns are $5.5$ cm apart. How far apart are they on the ground? On a second map they are $22$ cm apart — what is that map's scale?

first map $5.5 \times 60 = 330$ km
second map $22$ cm is $330$ km, so $1$ cm is $15$ km
as a ratio $1 : 15{,}00{,}000$

The second map is four times the first, and the number in its ratio is a quarter the size. A smaller second number means a larger map — which is worth reading twice, and is the first hint of what Section 3.7 is about.

A map's ratio has no units, and that is deliberate: it holds whether you measure the paper in centimetres or in inches, since both sides of the ratio are then in the same unit. It also measures distance as the crow flies, never along the road — a road that winds through hills can be half as long again as the line on the map.
