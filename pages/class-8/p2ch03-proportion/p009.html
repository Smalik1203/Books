Drawing it takes a compass and a protractor and no further arithmetic. Draw a circle, draw one radius, and measure each angle from the last radius in turn.

Notice what the picture shows and what it hides. It shows shares — A took a little under a third of the circle, and A and B together took more than half. It does not show how many students there were: the same chart would serve a class of four hundred with the same proportions, which is the whole point of a ratio and the reason a pie chart is nearly always labelled with its total.

Percentages get there by the same route. Grade A is $\tfrac{12}{40} = 30\%$ of the class, and $30\%$ of $360^\circ$ is $108^\circ$. Chapter 1's arithmetic and this chapter's are the same arithmetic with a different whole underneath: a hundred there, three hundred and sixty here.

Exercise Set 3.4
  1. Three hundred and sixty people were asked which season they liked best. Ninety said summer, $120$ said the rains, and the rest said winter. Find the three angles and draw the pie chart.
  1. Draw a pie chart for these viewing figures: entertainment $50\%$, sport $25\%$, news $15\%$, information $10\%$.
  1. A pie chart of a family's monthly spending gives food a slice of $126^\circ$. What percentage of the spending is that? If the family spends ₹$28{,}000$ a month, how much goes on food?
  1. A survey of $60$ students found $25$ walk to school, $20$ come by bus, $9$ cycle and the rest are driven. Find the four angles, and check that they add to $360^\circ$.
  1. Two pie charts, one of a class of $30$ and one of a class of $50$, have identical slices. What can you say about the two classes, and what can you not?
