1. A tap fills a tank in $6$ hours and a second fills it in $3$ hours. How long do the two take together? Check that your answer is less than $3$ hours.
  1. A car covers a journey in $4$ hours at $60$ km/h. The driver wants to do it in $3$ hours. At what speed must she travel? Is the relationship between the two direct or inverse, and how do you know?
What this chapter established
  1. A ratio records a relation between quantities and not their amounts, which is why two batters of different sizes can be the same recipe.
  2. $a : b$ and $c : d$ are proportional when $ad = bc$, or equally when $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{c}{d}$. Knowing three of the four numbers gives the fourth.
  3. Quantities are in direct proportion when their quotient never changes. Multiply one by any factor and the other follows by the same factor.
  4. A ratio may have any number of terms, and two such ratios are proportional when every matching pair has the same quotient. Finding the value of one part is what turns a ratio into amounts.
  5. To divide $x$ in the ratio $a : b : c$, add the terms and take those fractions of $x$. The shares must add back to $x$.
  6. A map's representative fraction is a ratio with no units — $1 : 60{,}00{,}000$ means one centimetre stands for $60$ km. The bigger that second number, the smaller the map.
  7. A pie chart divides $360^\circ$ in the ratio of the parts. It shows shares and hides totals, which is why one should always be labelled with how many it counts.
  8. Quantities are in inverse proportion when their product never changes: $xy = k$, so $x_1y_1 = x_2y_2$. Multiply one by $n$ and the other is multiplied by $\tfrac1n$.
  9. Which kind a pair of quantities is cannot be read off the numbers. It comes from the situation — more workers move more bricks, and more workers take fewer days.
  10. In questions about people working together, add the rates and never the times.
