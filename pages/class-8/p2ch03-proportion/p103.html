Through the ground and back to paper

Two maps of the same place have nothing directly to do with each other. What joins them is the ground, so go out to it and come back.

on the ground $6 \times 50{,}000 = 3{,}00{,}000$ cm, that is $3$ km
on the second map $3{,}00{,}000 \div 2{,}00{,}000 = 1.5$ cm

The second map's number is four times the first's, and the distance on the paper came out a quarter as long. That is an inverse proportion hiding inside two direct ones.

A slice as a fraction of the circle

The slice is $150$ out of $360$ degrees, and the same fraction of the people. No decision about direct or inverse is needed here — a pie chart is a ratio drawn with a protractor.

the fraction $\dfrac{150}{360} = \dfrac{5}{12}$
of the people $\dfrac{5}{12} \times 720 = 300$

Reducing $\tfrac{150}{360}$ before multiplying turned an awkward sum into $60 \times 5$. It is the same saving that reducing the ratio made in Section 3.6. The percentage is worth noting too: $\tfrac{5}{12}$ is about $42\%$, so a slice a little larger than a right angle and a half has taken not far off half the survey.

Two rates, one journey

Two direct proportions in a row: kilometres to litres, then litres to rupees. Neither is inverse, and running them in order is easier than trying to make one rule of the pair.

petrol needed $240 \div 15 = 16$ litres
its cost $16 \times 105 = $ ₹$1{,}680$

The middle quantity, litres, is not asked for and cannot be skipped. Notice also that a car doing $30$ km to the litre would halve the bill — kilometres per litre and rupees per kilometre are inversely proportional, though neither sentence in the question said so. A question can contain a proportion it does not mention, and this one contains three: distance to litres, litres to rupees, and — hidden behind both — efficiency to cost.
