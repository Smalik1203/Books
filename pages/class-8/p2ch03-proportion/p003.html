One warning before we go on. That last answer assumed the workers do not get in each other's way, and real ones eventually do. A proportional relationship is a model of a situation, and it is worth asking whether the situation deserves it.

3.3More Than Two Terms

Viswanath grinds a spice mix from eight spoons of coriander seed, four red chillies, two spoons of toor dal and one spoon of fenugreek. Written as a ratio that is

$$\text{coriander} : \text{chilli} : \text{dal} : \text{fenugreek} \ = \ 8 : 4 : 2 : 1.$$

A ratio may have as many terms as there are ingredients. It still records relations and not amounts, and it is still proportional to another ratio when every term has been multiplied by the same factor.

Puneet has only two chillies. That is half of Viswanath's four, so everything else must be halved too: four spoons of coriander, one of dal, half a spoon of fenugreek. His mix is $4 : 2 : 1 : 0.5$, and the two mixes are proportional — written $8 : 4 : 2 : 1 :: 4 : 2 : 1 : 0.5$.

In general, if $a : b : c : d$ and $p : q : r : s$ are proportional then every matching pair has the same quotient:

$$\frac{a}{p} = \frac{b}{q} = \frac{c}{r} = \frac{d}{s}.$$

With more than two terms there is no cross-multiplication to fall back on, so this is the test: work out the four quotients and see whether they agree. If even one of them differs, the mixes are not the same mix — and in a spice powder or a concrete beam that difference is the whole point.
