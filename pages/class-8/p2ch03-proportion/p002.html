The quotient is what holds still

Two quantities are in direct proportion when one is always the same multiple of the other. Double one and the other doubles; multiply one by any factor at all and the other follows by the same factor. What never changes is the quotient $\ y \div x$, and that fixed number is worth a name — it is the constant of the relationship.

For the batter, the constant is $2$: rice is always twice the dal, whether the pot holds three cups or thirty. It is the same constant that makes a photograph enlarge without distorting, and its absence is what makes a photograph stretched in one direction look wrong.

Example 1

Are $9 : 15$ and $21 : 35$ proportional?

cross-multiply $9 \times 35 = 315$ and $15 \times 21 = 315$
so yes — both reduce to $3 : 5$

Neither ratio was in its simplest form, and the test never needed them to be. That is its advantage: it works on the numbers as given.

The fourth number in a proportion can be found the same way. If three of $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ are known, the equation $ad = bc$ has one unknown in it and gives it up at once. Older books call this the rule of three, and it is the most used piece of arithmetic in this chapter.

Example 2

Five workers can shift $4{,}500$ bricks in a day. How many workers are needed to shift $18{,}000$ bricks in a day?

as a proportion $4{,}500 : 18{,}000 \ :: \ 5 : x$
cross-multiply $4{,}500\,x = 18{,}000 \times 5$
so $x = \dfrac{90{,}000}{4{,}500} = 20$ workers

Four times the bricks, four times the workers. Setting it out as a proportion is worth the extra line, because it is where the thinking happens — once the four numbers are in the right places the arithmetic cannot go wrong.
