Summary
  1. A ratio compares quantities. It records how many times one is of another, not how much there is of each, so it has no units. Sherbet mixed $2 : 8$ and $3 : 12$ tastes the same, because both are $1 : 4$.
  2. Two ratios $a : b$ and $c : d$ are proportional, written $a : b :: c : d$, when $a \times d = b \times c$. If three of the four numbers are known, this finds the fourth: the rule of three.
  3. Two quantities are in direct proportion when their quotient $y \div x$ stays the same. This fixed number is the constant.
  4. A ratio may have more than two terms, such as $6 : 3 : 2 : 1$. Two such ratios are proportional when every pair of matching terms gives the same quotient. A ratio is reduced by dividing every term by a number that divides them all.
  5. To use a ratio, find the value of one part from any amount that is known. Each term times one part gives its amount.
  6. To divide a whole in a ratio, add the terms and divide the whole by the total to find one part. The shares must add back to the whole.
  7. The scale of a map, $1 : n$, means that $1$ unit on the map stands for $n$ of the same unit on the ground. A smaller $n$ gives a larger drawing.
  8. In a pie chart, each slice's angle is its fraction of the whole times $360^\circ$, and the angles add to $360^\circ$.
  9. Two quantities are in inverse proportion when their product $xy$ stays the same: one grows as the other shrinks. Speed and time over a fixed distance, and workers and days for a fixed job, are examples.
  10. When people or taps work together, add the work each does in one hour, and take away the work of a drain. Then turn the total back into a time. Rates add; times do not.
