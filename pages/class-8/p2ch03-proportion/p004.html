A ratio can also be reduced, exactly as a fraction can, by dividing every term by a factor they all share. $8 : 4 : 2 : 1$ has no such factor and is already in its simplest form; $12 : 10 : 8 : 6 : 4$ shares a factor of $2$ and becomes $6 : 5 : 4 : 3 : 2$. Reducing changes nothing about the relation and a great deal about how easy the arithmetic is, which is why Section 3.6 begins by doing it.

Example 3

A particular purple is mixed red : blue : white in the ratio $2 : 3 : 5$. Yasmin has $10$ litres of white paint and wants to use all of it. How much red and blue does she need, and how much purple will she end up with?

White is $5$ parts of the mix, and those five parts are $10$ litres. So one part is $2$ litres, and every other quantity follows.

red $2$ parts $= 4$ litres
blue $3$ parts $= 6$ litres
in all $4 + 6 + 10 = 20$ litres

Finding the value of one part is the move that unlocks nearly every question of this kind. It turns a ratio into a set of amounts, and it needs only one amount to be known.

Example 4

Concrete for a beam is mixed cement : sand : gravel in the ratio $1 : 1.5 : 3$. With three bags of cement, how many bags of concrete can be made?

one part $3$ bags, since cement is $1$ part
the mix $3 : 4.5 : 9$ bags
in all $3 + 4.5 + 9 = 16.5$ bags

A ratio's terms need not be whole numbers. $1 : 1.5 : 3$ is the same relation as $2 : 3 : 6$, and a builder who prefers whole bags would use the second.

Those two examples asked opposite questions and used the same move. One gave the amount of a single ingredient and asked for the others; the other gave one ingredient and asked for the total. Both were answered by working out what one part is worth, and then reading the ratio off as a list of amounts.

Which ingredient the question names does not matter. In the paint it was white, worth $5$ parts; in the concrete, cement, worth $1$. Either does: divide the named amount by the number of parts it stands for, and the ratio does the rest.
