What Stays the Same

3.1Introduction

Idli batter is rice and urad dal ground together — two cups of rice to one cup of dal, in one common version. Viswanath grinds six cups of rice with three of dal. Puneet grinds four with two. Cooked the same way, do the two batters taste the same?

They do, and the reason is worth saying carefully. Viswanath has made half again as much batter as Puneet, so nothing about the two mixtures is equal in amount. What is equal is the relation between the two ingredients: twice as much rice as dal, in both bowls. A ratio records that relation and nothing else, which is why it is written $2 : 1$ and not in cups.

This chapter is about quantities that change together, and in every one of them something refuses to change while they do. In a recipe, a map or a paint mix, it is the ratio that holds still. In the last section it is something else — and finding out what tells you which kind of relationship you are looking at.

3.2When Two Ratios Agree

Viswanath's batter is $6 : 3$ and Puneet's is $4 : 2$. Both reduce to $2 : 1$, which settles that particular case, but reducing is not always the quickest test and it is not always possible in whole numbers. There is a check that always works.

Two ratios $a : b$ and $c : d$ describe the same relation — they are proportional — exactly when

$$a \times d = b \times c, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}.$$

The two forms say one thing twice. Multiplying across the two ratios is quick with awkward numbers; comparing the two quotients says why it works. For the batter, $6 \times 2 = 12$ and $3 \times 4 = 12$, so the ratios agree; and $\tfrac63 = \tfrac42 = 2$, which is the sentence twice as much rice as dal written as a number.
