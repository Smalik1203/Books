Four readings of one clay tablet

Two wedges, a gap, three wedges. The wedges are counts; the gap is supposed to say how many places separate them, and it cannot be measured.

adjacent places $2 \times 60 + 3$$123$
one place skipped $2 \times 3600 + 0 \times 60 + 3$$7203$
or the three in the 60s $2 \times 3600 + 3 \times 60$$7380$
two places skipped $2 \times 60^3 + 3$$432\,003$

Four readings, and there is no fifth reason to prefer any of them — nothing in the numeral settles it. In practice a scribe's reader used context: a tablet about a month's grain is unlikely to be recording four hundred thousand of anything.

That is why a placeholder is not a nicety. Without it, a place value system does not represent numbers at all; it represents families of numbers, and leaves the reader to choose.

Why base one is not a base

Put $n = 1$ into everything the chapter established and watch it collapse.

The landmarks are meant to be $n^0, n^1, n^2, \ldots$ — but every power of one is one. So the landmarks are $1, 1, 1, 1, \ldots$, and the second place is worth exactly what the first place is worth. Position has stopped meaning anything.

And the symbol count fails too. A base-$n$ system needs $n$ symbols, $0$ up to $n-1$; a base-$1$ system would need one symbol, which would have to be $0$. Every numeral would then be a row of zeros, worth nothing at all.

The smallest base that works is $2$, with symbols $0$ and $1$, and it is the one every computer uses. What is left if you insist on base one is the tally system of § 3.2 — which is a perfectly good way of recording a number, and is not a place value system, and never was.

Both of those solutions were of the same kind, and neither could have been reached by calculating. The first showed that a numeral fails to name a single number; the second that a proposed system cannot exist. In each case the work was done by taking the definition seriously and following it until it broke — which is a manoeuvre you will use far more often than any of the arithmetic in this chapter.
