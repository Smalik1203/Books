It is worth asking why the placeholder was the hard part, since a mark for nothing sounds like the easiest thing in the world to invent. The difficulty is that it is a symbol for the absence of a quantity, and for most of history a numeral meant a quantity you could point at. Three sheep are three sheep; no sheep are not a kind of sheep. Writing a sign for them requires treating the empty column as a thing in its own right, and several civilisations got all the way to place value and stopped just short of that step.

India did not stop, and went one further: having written the sign, it treated what the sign stood for as a number. That is the move that changed mathematics, and it is why $0$ appears in the row of digits at the front of this chapter alongside $1$ to $9$, rather than as a piece of punctuation between them.

3.7.1 How the digits travelled

An idea this good does not stay put, and the route it took is worth following because it explains a piece of vocabulary that is still causing confusion.

by c. 800 CE the system reaches the Arab world
c. 825 al-Khwārizmī writes On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals
c. 830 al-Kindī, On the Use of the Hindu Numerals
by c. 1100 the numerals reach Europe and North Africa
c. 1200 Fibonacci argues the case to Europe

Europe took its time. Roman numerals were built into law, accounting and habit, and for three centuries people resisted the new digits — some cities banned them in ledgers, on the grounds that a $0$ could too easily be altered into a $6$ or a $9$. It was not until the seventeenth century that refusing them became impossible, because by then no serious science could be done without them.

Now the vocabulary. European scholars met the numerals through Arabic books and so called them Arabic numerals. The Arabic books themselves called them Hindu numerals, after the place they came from. The older name is the more accurate one, and textbooks now say Indian numerals or Hindu-Arabic numerals instead. The word Hindu here names a geography and a people, not a religion.

The shapes themselves drifted over the centuries, which is why the digits in a Brahmi inscription do not look much like the ones on this page. They are the same system nonetheless: what travelled was never the shapes but the ideas — ten symbols, a base of ten, position, and a zero.

Today the system is so familiar that it is easy to miss how clever it is. A child who writes $305$ is using an idea that took the world thousands of years to find, and that is exactly why it is so useful: nobody has to think about it.
