3.2Counting Without Numbers

Start before any of it. You look after the fishing boats of a small village. Every morning the boats go out to sea, and every evening you must know whether they have all come back. You cannot count them, because nobody has invented counting yet. There are no number names and no numerals.

You can still do it. Each morning, as a boat leaves, drop a shell into a pot. Each evening, as a boat returns, take one shell out. If the pot empties exactly, every boat is home. If a shell is left, a boat is still at sea.

Nothing there is a number, and yet the question got answered. What did the work is a one-to-one mapping — one shell for one boat, no shell used twice — and it answers the question without ever saying how many.

One for one

Two collections are the same size when their items can be paired one for one with nothing left over. This can be checked without knowing how many there are.

Think and Reflect

The next village also has fishing boats. Using only shells, and still no counting, how would you find out which village has more boats?

If the other village has more, how would you find how many more, without counting either set of boats?

The shells can be replaced by anything you have plenty of and can put in a fixed order. Three kinds of thing have been used, all over the world:

objects pebbles, sticks, knots in a cord, notches cut in a bone
names a fixed sequence of spoken words, said in order
written symbols marks made on clay, stone, bark or paper

Any of the three will do, if it meets two conditions. The items must come in a fixed order, so everybody counts them the same way. And there must be no last one, so you never run out. A sequence of that kind is a number system; its written symbols are its numerals.

Those two conditions are harder to satisfy together than they look, and the first three attempts each fail one of them.
