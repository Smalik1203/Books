The pebbles can be replaced by anything you have plenty of and can put in a fixed order. Three kinds of thing have been used, all over the world:

objects pebbles, sticks, knots in a cord, notches cut in a bone
names a fixed sequence of spoken words, said in order
written symbols marks made on clay, stone, bark or paper

Any of the three will do, if it meets two conditions. The items must come in a fixed order, so everybody counts them the same way. And there must be no last one, so you never run out. A sequence of that kind is a number system, and the written symbols in one are its numerals.

Those two conditions are harder to satisfy together than they look, and the first three attempts each fail one of them.

Sticks never run out, but they are unusable. A herd of three hundred needs three hundred sticks, and comparing two such piles means matching them one by one all over again. The system is correct and impossible.

The letters of an alphabet are easy to say, but they stop. Count $a, b, c, \ldots$ and you have twenty-six numbers and then nothing. You can patch it — carry on with aa, ab, and so on — but the patch is itself an idea worth having, and it is the same idea this whole chapter is about.

Written strokes never run out, and are unreadable. Twenty-nine notches cut in a bone is a perfectly good record of twenty-nine, and the oldest kind of number we have. The Lebombo bone, found in South Africa, carries twenty-nine notches and is perhaps forty thousand years old. But nobody can look at twenty-nine notches and see twenty-nine.

The gate mark in the lower row is the first real idea in this chapter, and it is a small one: gather the marks into groups. Nothing has been added and nothing taken away; the twenty-nine notches are still twenty-nine. But they can now be read rather than counted.
