The digit-sum rule, in any base

First, why the base-$10$ rule works at all. Every landmark is one more than a multiple of nine: $10 = 9 + 1$, $100 = 99 + 1$, $1000 = 999 + 1$. So when a number is split into landmarks, each landmark contributes its digit plus a multiple of nine — and the multiples of nine can all be thrown away.

$473$ $= 4(99+1) + 7(9+1) + 3 = (4 + 7 + 3) + \text{a multiple of } 9$

Nothing there was about ten in particular. It was about $9$ being one less than the base. So in base $7$ the same argument works for $6$:

in base 7 a number is divisible by $6$ exactly when its digits add to a multiple of $6$
check: $48$ $= 6 \times 7 + 6 = 66$ in base $7$, digits sum to $12$ $48 = 6 \times 8$

In general, in base $n$ the digit-sum rule tests for divisibility by $n - 1$. Which explains a fact you may have wondered about: the base-$10$ rule works for $9$ and also for $3$, because $3$ is a factor of $9$.

Which fractions come out exactly

A fraction terminates in base $10$ when it can be written with a denominator that is a power of ten — $\tfrac12 = \tfrac{5}{10}$, $\tfrac15 = \tfrac{2}{10}$ — and a power of ten has only the primes $2$ and $5$ in it. So the fractions that terminate are exactly those whose denominators are built from $2$s and $5$s, which is why $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac17$ go on for ever.

Base $12$ has the primes $2$ and $3$, so thirds join the club and fifths leave it.

$\tfrac12$$= \tfrac{6}{12}$, exact
$\tfrac13$$= \tfrac{4}{12}$, exact — where base $10$ fails
$\tfrac14$$= \tfrac{3}{12}$, exact
$\tfrac15$never exact in base $12$

Which is a genuine argument for base $12$, and it has been made seriously more than once. Thirds turn up far more often in daily life than fifths — the twelve inches of a foot and the twelve hours of a clock face are what is left of people acting on that thought.
