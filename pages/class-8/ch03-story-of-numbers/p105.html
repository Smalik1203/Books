Why it stops: each division makes the number at least five times smaller, and a whole number cannot shrink for ever. It must reach zero, and it reaches it quickly — the number of steps is the number of digits.

Why the remainders come out backwards: the first division asks how many are left over after taking out all the fives, which is precisely the units digit. The second asks the same of the fives, which is the next digit up. Each step peels off the lowest place, so the digits emerge in the reverse of the order they are written.

A word about the third and the fourth, because they are a different kind of question. Both ask you to show that something is impossible or unclear, and neither can be settled by working an example. What settles them is an argument that covers every case, and in both the argument is two or three lines long once found.

The others are conversions and rules, and each rewards the same habit: write the number in expanded form, with the powers showing, and look at what the base is doing. Almost every question about bases becomes easy the moment you rewrite $4344_{\,6}$ as $4 \times 6^3 + 3 \times 6^2 + 4 \times 6 + 4$ and look again.

If you cannot get started, do it in base $10$ first, where you already know the answer, and then ask which step used the fact that the base was ten. The general rule lives in that step.

That last piece of advice is worth more than it looks, and it is a general method rather than a hint about bases. A problem may be stated with a letter in it: a base $n$, an exponent $a$, a side of length $x$. The way in is almost always to replace the letter with a number you know your way around, solve the easy version completely, and then reread your own solution asking which lines actually used the number. Those lines are the ones that need rewriting. The rest was general all along.
