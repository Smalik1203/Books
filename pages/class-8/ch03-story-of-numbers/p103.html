Digits in a numeral, symbols in a system

Two different counts, and the question was really about telling them apart.

How many digits does $1000$ need in base $7$? Find the largest landmark that fits. The powers of seven are $1, 7, 49, 343, 2401$, and $343$ fits while $2401$ does not — so the numeral runs from the $343$ place down to the units, which is four places.

$1000$ $= 2 \times 343 + 6 \times 49 + 2 \times 7 + 6$
so $2626$ in base $7$ 4 digits

How many symbols does the system need? Seven — the digits $0$ to $6$. A place can hold anything from none of its landmark up to six of them, and seven would become one of the next landmark instead.

So the first number counts places in one numeral and grows as the number grows; the second counts symbols in the alphabet and never changes at all. Confusing them is the commonest error with bases, and the Egyptians' problem was exactly that their second number grew too.

Two thousand and twenty-five in twos

Divide by $2$ repeatedly and keep the remainders, reading them upwards at the end.

$2025$ $= 1024 + 512 + 256 + 128 + 64 + 32 + 8 + 1$
in base 2 $11111101001$ 11 digits

For a number near a million, do not convert — estimate. Chapter 2 gave us $2^{10} \approx 10^3$, so $2^{20} \approx 10^6$: a million needs about twenty binary digits. The true answer is exactly twenty, and it was got without dividing anything.

Those twenty binary digits are worth a moment. A computer stores a number as a row of switches, each on or off, and twenty switches is what it takes to count to a million. Sixty-four of them — the size of a modern machine's word — reach past $10^{19}$, which is the chessboard of grain from the last chapter. That is the whole reason base two is the base of computing: it is the only base whose digits a piece of wire can hold.
