That is the crack in the system, and the next section is entirely about widening it.

3.4.1 Where the Roman system gives out

Adding in Roman numerals is not bad. Put the two numbers side by side, gather the symbols of each kind, and trade upward whenever you have enough.

Example 2

Add CCXXXII and CCCCXIII without converting to our numerals.

Gather like symbols first.

Cs CC and CCCC make six
Xs XXX and X make four
Is II and III make five

Now trade upward. Five Is make a V. Five Cs make a D, leaving one C over.

total DCXLV $645$

Notice the trading: five Is became a V, but it takes two Vs to make an X, and five Xs to make an L. The rate changes as you go up, and you have to remember which is which.

Multiplying is where it stops working. What is $\text{VII} \times \text{IX}$? There is no rule to reach for. You cannot say what a C times an X is without knowing what number the answer wants to be called, and there is no symbol pattern that produces it — the landmarks $1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000$ multiply into each other in no orderly way at all.

$\text{V} \times \text{X}$ $= 50 = \text{L}$— a landmark, by luck
$\text{V} \times \text{V}$ $= 25 = \text{XXV}$— not a landmark at all
$\text{L} \times \text{L}$ $= 2500 = \text{MMD}$— nor this

So multiplication had to be done on a machine. Merchants across the Roman world used a counting board — an abacus — with a line for each power of ten, and only specially trained clerks could work one quickly. The numerals recorded the answer; they were no help in finding it.

There is a second cost, quieter but just as real. Because the calculating was a craft, it belonged to the few people trained in it. A merchant who could read MMCCCLXVII perfectly well still had to hand the sum to a clerk. When a notation arrives that lets an ordinary person multiply on paper, arithmetic stops being a profession and becomes a skill — and that is one of the reasons the change, when it finally came to Europe, mattered so much beyond mathematics.
