Example 1

Write $2758$ in Roman numerals.

Work down the landmarks, taking as many of each as will fit.

Solution.

Step 1 $2758 = 1000 + 1000 + 500 + 100 + 100 + 50 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1$ largest
Step 2 M M D C C L V I I I a symbol for each
Step 3 $1000 + 1000 + 500 + 100 \times 2 + 50 + 5 + 1 \times 3 = 2758$ check
Answer MMDCCLVIII

One extra rule is worth knowing. Rather than write $4$ as IIII, the Romans often put the smaller symbol before the larger to mean subtraction: IV is $5-1$, IX is $10-1$, XL is $50-10$, CM is $1000-100$. They were not consistent about it — IIII appears on plenty of monuments and clock faces — but the rule shortens the awkward cases.

The subtractive rule only ever borrows one step down: IV and IX are allowed, IL and IC are not. So $49$ is XLIX — forty then nine — and not IL.

Example 2

What number is MCMLXXIV?

Solution. Split the numeral where a smaller symbol stands before a larger one.

Step 1 M | CM | L | XX | IV find the pairs
Step 2 $1000$, $\;1000 - 100 = 900$, $\;50$, $\;20$, $\;5 - 1 = 4$
Step 3 $1000 + 900 + 50 + 20 + 4 = 1974$
Answer $1974$

This is a real advance on grouping by a single size, and the reason is the sequence of landmarks. Where a tally system offers you one group size, the Roman system offers seven, so a large number can be built from a handful of symbols instead of hundreds of strokes. Two thousand seven hundred and fifty-eight takes ten characters.

And it survives. Roman numerals still number the volumes of books, the acts of plays, the faces of clocks and the Olympic Games — not because they are efficient, but because they look formal, which is a use of its own.
