So multiplication had to be done on a machine. Merchants across the Roman world used a counting board — an abacus — with a line for each power of ten, and only specially trained clerks could work one quickly. The numerals recorded the answer; they were no help in finding it.

There is a second cost, quieter but just as real. Because the calculating was a craft, it belonged to the few people trained in it. A merchant who could read MMCCCLXVII perfectly well still had to hand the sum to a clerk. When a notation arrives that lets an ordinary person multiply on paper, arithmetic stops being a profession and becomes a skill. That is one of the reasons the change, when it finally came to Europe, mattered so much beyond mathematics.

It is easy to look down on all of this from where we sit now, and it would be wrong to do so. The Roman system did what it was asked to do for nearly two thousand years: it recorded quantities, on stone and in ledgers, briefly and with no room for doubt. Nobody expected a numeral to help with the arithmetic, because in Rome the arithmetic was a separate craft done on a board by a specialist. What changed later was not that people got cleverer, but that a system arrived in which writing the number down and calculating with it were the same activity.

Hold on to that thought. The advantage of the modern system is not that it is shorter — $\text{MMCCCLXVII}$ and $2367$ are about the same length — but that its notation does arithmetic for you.
