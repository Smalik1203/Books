The product of two landmarks is always another landmark. That is Chapter 2's first law wearing different clothes: multiply two powers of ten and the exponents add, so the answer is a power of ten. It is true in every base, and it is true in no system whose landmarks were chosen by taste — recall $\text{V} \times \text{V} = 25$, which is nothing in particular.

Example 4

Multiply $324$ by $10$ in Egyptian symbols, without converting anything.

$324$ is three coils, two arches and four strokes. Multiply each symbol by ten and each one is promoted to the next landmark.

three coils become three lotuses$300 \to 3000$
two arches become two coils$20 \to 200$
four strokes become four arches$4 \to 40$

Every symbol simply moves one place up the ladder, and the answer is $3240$ without a single computation. That is what a base buys, and it is why you were taught to multiply by ten by adding a zero: the zero is the symbol moving up.

Multiplying by anything else works the same way, using the distributive law: $324 \times 30$ is $324 \times 10$ promoted once more and then taken three times. No such shortcut exists for the Romans, which is why they needed the abacus.

Notice how much of Chapter 2 has just been used. The landmarks are powers of ten; multiplying them adds the exponents; the product is therefore another landmark. Every one of those steps was proved there, about numbers, with no thought of Egypt — and it turns out to be the reason a four-thousand-year-old notation works. That is a common pattern in mathematics, and worth expecting: a result established for one purpose explains something quite unrelated.

It also settles a question the Egyptians could not have asked. Any base gives the shortcut, so base ten has no special mathematical virtue. It won because we have ten fingers, and for no better reason than that.

Which is not to say the choice is without consequences. A base with many factors divides tidily: base $60$ splits into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, tenths, twelfths, fifteenths, twentieths and thirtieths without ever leaving a fraction, which is why we still keep time in sixties. Base $10$ manages only halves and fifths, which is why a third of an hour is twenty minutes but a third of a metre is a decimal that never ends. And base $2$, with no factors at all beyond itself, is the worst for dividing and the best for machines, because a wire is either on or off.
