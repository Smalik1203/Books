The order of the symbols does not matter, since each one carries its own value wherever it stands. Three coils, two arches and four strokes make $324$ however they are arranged on the stone, and Egyptian scribes did sometimes stack them or write them right to left.

Now the important part. Because the landmarks were chosen by a rule, they are not a chosen list at all — they are the powers of ten:

$10^0 = 1, \quad 10^1 = 10, \quad 10^2 = 100, \quad 10^3 = 1000, \ \ldots$

And once you notice that, you notice that ten was a choice. Any number would do. Start at $1$, decide that five of any landmark make the next, and you get a different but perfectly workable system:

landmarks $1, \quad 5, \quad 25, \quad 125, \quad 625, \quad 3125, \ \ldots$
that is $5^0, \quad 5^1, \quad 5^2, \quad 5^3, \quad 5^4, \quad 5^5, \ \ldots$

That second line is the whole idea. The landmarks are no longer a list somebody chose; they are generated, one from the last, by a rule you can state in seven words. Ask for the tenth landmark and you do not have to ask anybody — it is $5^9$. Ask the Romans for their tenth landmark and there is no answer, because after M they simply stopped.

A base

A number system whose first landmark is $1$ and whose every later landmark is $n$ times the one before is a base-$n$ system. Its landmarks are exactly the powers of $n$. Base $10$ is also called decimal.

The smallest useful base is $2$. Its landmarks are $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, \ldots$, each double the one before. No landmark is ever used twice, because two of them make the next. So every whole number is a sum of different landmarks of base $2$: for example, $13 = 8 + 4 + 1$.
