Example 4

Write $187$ in a base-$5$ system.

Take as many of the largest landmark that fits, then work down.

Solution.

Step 1 $125$, and one of them leaves $62$ largest that fits
Step 2 $62 = 25 + 25 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1$ then work down
Step 3 $187 = 125 + 25 + 25 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1$
Answer One hundred-and-twenty-five, two twenty-fives, two fives and two ones

In Egyptian style that would be one symbol of the fourth landmark and two each of the third, second and first — seven symbols in all. Base ten would have needed $1 + 8 + 7 = 16$ symbols for the same number.

Notice that no landmark is ever needed five or more times. If it were, five of them would combine into one of the next landmark up, and the grouping was not finished.

That last point is true for every base. In a base-$n$ system no landmark is ever needed $n$ times or more, because $n$ of them make the next one. It is the single most useful fact about bases, because it says exactly how many of each symbol a numeral can carry. In base $2$ it means that each landmark is used once or not at all.

3.5.1 Why a base makes arithmetic easy

Adding in a base system is the trading of Example 3 with one improvement: the rate never changes. Ten of anything makes the next thing up, all the way. In the Roman system you had to remember that five Is make a V but two Vs make an X. Here there is one rule, and it is the same at every level.

The real gain is in multiplication, and it comes from a single fact.

$10 \times 100$ $= 10^1 \times 10^2 = 10^3 = 1000$
$100 \times 1000$ $= 10^2 \times 10^3 = 10^5$

The product of two landmarks is always another landmark. That is Chapter 2's first law wearing different clothes: multiply two powers of ten and the exponents add, so the answer is a power of ten. It is true in every base, and it is true in no system whose landmarks were chosen by taste — recall $\text{V} \times \text{V} = 25$, which is nothing in particular.
