Read what has happened. The number $7530$ is written with eight marks and two symbol shapes, and nowhere in it is there a picture meaning three thousand six hundred. The leftmost group is in the $3600$ place, and that is all it needs to be. Sixty times bigger again, and you move one group further left — no new symbol required, ever.

A place value system

In a positional or place value system, a symbol's landmark is decided by where it is written rather than by what it is. A finite set of symbols then writes every number there is.

That is the fourth idea, and it is the one that solves the original problem completely. But the Mesopotamians did not quite get it right, and the reason is worth as much as the idea.

Consider $3605$, which is $1 \times 3600 + 0 \times 60 + 5$. The middle place holds nothing. What do you write there?

They left a gap. And a gap is not a symbol. It is wider in one scribe's hand than in another's, it can be lost when clay dries or a tablet is copied, and two gaps in a row look exactly like one. A numeral meaning $3605$ could be read as $65$, or as $216\,005$, depending on how generously you measured the empty space.

Later Mesopotamian scribes did invent a mark to fill an empty place, and it worked. But they used it only between other symbols, never at the end — so $1$ and $60$ and $3600$ still looked alike, and the reader had to judge from context which was meant.

3.6.2 The Maya, and the Chinese rods

The same idea was found independently in Central America. Between the third and tenth centuries CE the Maya wrote numbers with a dot for $1$ and a bar for $5$, making every number up to $19$. They then stacked the groups vertically: units at the bottom, the next place above, and so on. They had a placeholder too, drawn as a shell.

Their landmarks are strange. The first is $20$, as you would expect from a people counting on fingers and toes; but the next landmark is $360$ rather than $400$, which breaks the rule that every landmark is the base times the last. It is generally thought that their calendar got in the way: $360$ is close to a year. Whatever the reason, it costs them the multiplication shortcut of § 3.5.1, since the product of two landmarks is no longer a landmark.

In China, from at least the third century CE, calculation was done with small rods laid out on a board. The rod numerals are a base-ten place value system, and they have an extra trick of their own: the digits in alternate places were laid out vertically and horizontally in turn.
