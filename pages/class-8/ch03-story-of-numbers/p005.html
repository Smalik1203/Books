Call these the landmark numbers of the system. To write any number, take as many of the largest landmark as will fit, then as many of the next, and so on down.

Example 1

Write $2367$ in Roman numerals.

Work down the landmarks, taking as many of each as will fit.

$2367$ $= 1000 + 1000 + 100 + 100 + 100 + 50 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1$
so MMCCCLXVII

Check by adding the symbols back up: $1000 + 1000 + 100 \times 3 + 50 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 2367$.

One refinement is worth knowing. Rather than write $4$ as IIII, the Romans often put the smaller symbol before the larger to mean subtraction: IV is $5-1$, IX is $10-1$, XL is $50-10$, CM is $1000-100$. They were not consistent about it — IIII appears on plenty of monuments and clock faces — but the convention shortens the awkward cases.

The subtractive rule only ever borrows one step down: IV and IX are allowed, IL and IC are not. So $49$ is XLIX — forty then nine — and not IL.

This is a real advance on grouping by a single size, and the reason is the sequence of landmarks. Where a tally system offers you one group size, the Roman system offers seven, so a large number can be built from a handful of symbols instead of hundreds of strokes. Two thousand three hundred and sixty-seven takes ten characters.

And it survives. Roman numerals still number the volumes of books, the acts of plays, the faces of clocks and the Olympic Games — not because they are efficient, but because they look formal, which is a use of its own.

Before leaving the Romans, it is worth being clear about what their system does and does not have. It does group, and at several levels: a C is a hundred ones, an M is ten Cs. It does not group at a constant rate — five Is make a V but two Vs make an X — and there is no rule that generates the next landmark from the last. The list $1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000$ has to be memorised, because nothing about it can be worked out.
