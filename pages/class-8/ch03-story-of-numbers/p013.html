Later Mesopotamian scribes did invent a mark to fill an empty place, and it worked. But they used it only between other symbols, never at the end — so $1$ and $60$ and $3600$ still looked alike, and the reader had to judge from context which was meant.

3.6.2 The Maya, and the Chinese rods

The same idea was found independently in Central America. Between the third and tenth centuries CE the Maya wrote numbers with a dot for $1$ and a bar for $5$, making every number to $19$, and then stacked the groups vertically — units at the bottom, the next place above, and so on. They had a placeholder too, drawn as a shell.

Their bases are odd. The first is $20$, as you would expect from a people counting on fingers and toes; but the next landmark is $360$ rather than $400$, which breaks the rule that every landmark is the base times the last. It is generally thought to be their calendar intruding: $360$ is close to a year. Whatever the reason, it costs them the multiplication shortcut of § 3.5.1, since the product of two landmarks is no longer a landmark.

In China, from at least the third century CE, calculation was done with small rods laid out on a board. The rod numerals are a base-ten place value system, and they carry a refinement of their own: the digits in alternate places were laid out vertically and horizontally in turn.

The alternation is a clever fix for the very problem that defeated Mesopotamia. If a place is empty and left blank, the neighbouring digits are still told apart, because one is a set of uprights and the other a set of bars. A blank between two identical-looking runs would be ambiguous; a blank between uprights and bars is not.

It is a good fix, and it is not the right one. A system that needs its digits to alternate in order to be readable has bought its clarity with a rule that has nothing to do with counting. The right fix is smaller and stranger.
