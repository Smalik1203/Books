What this chapter established
  1. Counting begins with a one-to-one mapping — one pebble for one cow — which answers questions about a collection without ever naming its size.
  2. A number system is a standard sequence of objects, names or symbols in a fixed order and with no end. The written symbols of one are its numerals.
  3. Marks alone never run out but cannot be read; the letters of an alphabet can be read but run out. Every system in this chapter is an attempt to have both.
  4. Gathering marks into groups — the five-bar gate — makes a tally readable, and the group size is chosen at the limit of what an eye can see at once, which is about four or five.
  5. Landmark numbers are the sizes given symbols of their own. The Roman landmarks $1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000$ let large numbers be written briefly, but they are an arbitrary list.
  6. In a base-$n$ system the landmarks are the powers of $n$: $n^0, n^1, n^2, \ldots$ No landmark is ever needed $n$ times or more, because $n$ of them make the next.
  7. A base makes arithmetic easy for one reason: the product of two landmarks is another landmark, since multiplying powers adds exponents. The Roman system has no such property, which is why it needed an abacus.
  8. In a place value system a symbol's landmark is decided by where it stands, not by what it is — so a finite set of symbols writes every number, and the old problem of inventing new symbols for ever disappears.
  9. Place value was reached independently in Mesopotamia (base $60$), among the Maya, in China and in India.
  10. A place value system is unreadable without a mark for an empty place. Mesopotamia used a gap and then a partial placeholder; the Chinese alternated upright and horizontal rods; India used $0$ everywhere and settled it.
  11. Zero does two jobs. As a placeholder it keeps the columns straight. As a number it can be calculated with — Āryabhaṭa (499 CE) computed with it, and Brahmagupta (628 CE) set out its arithmetic alongside that of the negative numbers.
  12. The system is Indian, reached the Arab world by about 800 CE through al-Khwārizmī and al-Kindī, and Europe by about 1100. Arabic numerals records the route rather than the source; Indian or Hindu-Arabic numerals is more accurate.
  13. Ten symbols, a base of ten, position, and a zero. That is the whole system, and there is nothing else in it.
