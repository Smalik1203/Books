3.6.1 Mesopotamia, and counting in sixties

In what is now Iraq, from around four thousand years ago, scribes pressed wedge-shaped marks into wet clay. Their system had two symbols — a narrow wedge for $1$ and a sideways one for $10$ — and with those two they could write any number from $1$ to $59$.

Fifty-nine, because their base was $60$. Why sixty is disputed: it may have come from their calendar, or from the convenience of a number with so many divisors, or from an older set of landmarks that happened to include it. What is not disputed is that we still use it — sixty minutes in an hour, sixty seconds in a minute — four thousand years later.

Read what has happened. The number $7530$ is written with eight marks and two symbol shapes, and nowhere in it is there a picture meaning three thousand six hundred. The leftmost group is in the $3600$ place, and that is all it needs to be. Sixty times bigger again, and you move one group further left — no new symbol required, ever.

A place value system

In a positional or place value system, a symbol's landmark is decided by where it is written rather than by what it is. A finite set of symbols then writes every number there is.

That is the fourth idea, and it is the one that solves the original problem completely. But the Mesopotamians did not quite get it right, and the reason is worth as much as the idea.

Consider $3605$, which is $1 \times 3600 + 0 \times 60 + 5$. The middle place holds nothing. What do you write there?

They left a gap. And a gap is not a symbol: it is wider in one scribe's hand than in another's, it can be lost when clay dries or a tablet is copied, and two gaps in a row are indistinguishable from one. A numeral meaning $3605$ could be read as $65$, or as $216\,005$, depending on how generously you measured the empty space.
