Summary
  1. Two collections can be compared by a one-to-one mapping: pair their items one for one and see whether anything is left over. No counting is needed.
  2. A number system is a sequence of objects, names or symbols in a fixed order that never runs out. Its written symbols are its numerals.
  3. Gathering marks into groups of one size, such as the five-bar gate, lets a number be read at a glance. The usual group sizes are 2, 5, 10 and 20.
  4. The Roman system writes numbers with landmark numbers: I, V, X, L, C, D and M stand for 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500 and 1000. For example, $2758$ is MMDCCLVIII.
  5. The Roman landmarks follow no single rule, so their products are not always landmarks: $\text{V} \times \text{V} = 25$. Adding works by trading; multiplying needed an abacus.
  6. In a base-$n$ system the landmarks are the powers of $n$: $1, n, n^2, n^3, \ldots$ No landmark is used $n$ times or more. Base 10 is called decimal.
  7. In a base system the product of two landmarks is always a landmark, so multiplying by the base moves every symbol one landmark up.
  8. In a place value system a symbol's value depends on where it is written. The Mesopotamians used base 60, the Maya a system built on 20, and the Chinese rods base 10.
  9. Zero does two jobs. As a placeholder it marks an empty place, as in $305$. As a number it can be added, subtracted and multiplied. India was the first to use it both ways.
  10. Our system, the Indian or Hindu-Arabic numerals, uses ten digits, base ten, place value and zero: $3605 = 3 \times 10^3 + 6 \times 10^2 + 0 \times 10^1 + 5 \times 10^0$.
