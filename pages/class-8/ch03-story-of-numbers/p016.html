India did not stop, and went one further: having written the sign, it treated what the sign stood for as a number. That is the move that changed mathematics, and it is why $0$ appears in the row of digits at the front of this chapter alongside $1$ to $9$, rather than as a piece of punctuation between them.

3.7.1 How the digits travelled

An idea this good does not stay put, and the route it took is worth following because it explains a piece of vocabulary that is still causing confusion.

by c. 800 CE the system reaches the Arab world
c. 825 al-Khwārizmī writes On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals
c. 830 al-Kindī, On the Use of the Hindu Numerals
by c. 1100 the numerals reach Europe and North Africa
c. 1200 Fibonacci argues the case to Europe

Europe took its time. Roman numerals were embedded in law, accounting and habit, and for three centuries the new digits were resisted — some cities banned them in ledgers, on the grounds that a $0$ could too easily be altered into a $6$ or a $9$. It was not until the seventeenth century that refusing them became impossible, because by then no serious science could be done without them.

Now the vocabulary. European scholars met the numerals through Arabic books and so called them Arabic numerals. The Arabic books themselves called them Hindu numerals, after the place they came from. So the older and more accurate name is the one Europe did not use, and modern textbooks increasingly say Indian numerals or Hindu-Arabic numerals instead. The word Hindu here names a geography and a people, not a religion.

The shapes themselves drifted over the centuries, which is why the digits in a Brahmi inscription do not look much like the ones on this page. They are the same system nonetheless: what travelled was never the shapes but the ideas — ten symbols, a base of ten, position, and a zero.

The astronomer Laplace, writing in about 1800, put the achievement in a way that has not been bettered: that the method of expressing every number with ten symbols emerged in India, that it now seems so simple that its profound importance is no longer appreciated, and that its simplicity is what placed arithmetic first among useful inventions.

Set the whole story out and it is five ideas, each fixing what the last one left broken.
