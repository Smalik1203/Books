What to carry forward

The mathematics of this chapter fits in four lines. A base-$n$ system has landmarks $n^0, n^1, n^2, \ldots$; it needs exactly $n$ symbols, one of which is zero; no place ever holds $n$ or more; and the digit-sum rule in that system tests for $n-1$. Everything else was an illustration.

But the habit is worth more than the facts. Twice in the solutions above, a question that looked like it needed a calculation was answered by asking what a system must do — base one is impossible, the Mesopotamian numeral is ambiguous — and neither could have been settled by working examples. Learning to tell those questions apart from the arithmetical ones is most of what this section has been for.

And there is one thing to carry that is not mathematics at all. Every system here was a reasonable answer to the problem as its inventors met it, and each failed only when asked to do something it was never built for. That is worth remembering the next time a method you have been taught stops working: usually it has not gone wrong, you have simply walked past the edge of what it was for.
