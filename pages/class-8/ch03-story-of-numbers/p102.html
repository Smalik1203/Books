Some fractions come out exactly as decimals and some do not: $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac15$ do, $\tfrac13$ does not. Which fractions would come out exactly in base $12$, and what does the answer have to do with the factors of the base?

An Egyptian scribe needs a new symbol for every new power of ten. How many symbols would be needed to write every number up to a million? How many does the Hindu system need for the same job?

Convert $143$ from base $10$ to base $5$ by repeatedly dividing by $5$ and keeping the remainders. Why does that procedure always stop, and why do the remainders come out in the right order?

A word about the third and the fourth, because they are a different kind of question. Both ask you to show that something is impossible or ambiguous, and neither can be settled by working an example. What settles them is an argument that covers every case, and in both the argument is two or three lines long once found.

The others are conversions and rules, and each rewards the same habit: write the number in expanded form, with the powers showing, and look at what the base is doing. Almost every question about bases becomes easy the moment $2626_{\,7}$ is rewritten as $2 \times 7^3 + 6 \times 7^2 + 2 \times 7 + 6$.

If a question resists, do it in base $10$ first, where you already know the answer, and then ask which step used the fact that the base was ten. That step is where the general rule lives.

That last piece of advice is worth more than it looks, and it is a general method rather than a hint about bases. When a problem is stated with a letter in it — a base $n$, an exponent $a$, a side of length $x$ — the way in is almost always to replace the letter with a number you know your way around, solve the easy version completely, and then reread your own solution asking which lines actually used the number. Those lines are the ones that need rewriting; the rest was general all along.
