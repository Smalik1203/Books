There is a limit, somewhere around four or five, past which a human being can no longer see a quantity and must start counting it. It is a fact about how eyes work and not about arithmetic, and it turns up in number systems all over the world. The most common group sizes in history are $2$, $5$, $10$ and $20$. Every one of them is either at that limit or a small multiple of it, or, in the case of $10$ and $20$, a count of fingers and toes.

Some systems grouped by two and went no further. The Gumulgal of Australia counted like this:

numbernamewhat it says
1uraponone
2ukasartwo
3ukasar-urapontwo and one
4ukasar-ukasartwo and two
5ukasar-ukasar-urapontwo, two and one
6ukasar-ukasar-ukasartwo, two and two

Anything beyond six was simply many. The names are built by repeating the word for two, which is the idea of grouping showing up in speech rather than in writing.

There is something strange about that system. Almost identical ones — the same counting by twos, the same words built by repetition — have been recorded among the Bakairi of South America and the San of southern Africa. Three peoples on three continents, with no known contact between them, arrived at the same arrangement. Historians disagree about why. One idea is that the three peoples share a very old common ancestor, and the system travelled with them. Another is simply that counting in twos is an obvious thing to do, and obvious things get invented more than once.
