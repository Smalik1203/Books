The gate mark in the lower row is the first real idea in this chapter, and it is a small one: gather the marks into groups. Nothing has been added and nothing taken away; the twenty-nine notches are still twenty-nine. But they can now be read rather than counted.

3.3Counting in Groups

Why five? The choice is not arbitrary, and the reason is in your eyes rather than in mathematics.

There is a limit, somewhere around four or five, past which a human being can no longer see a quantity and must start counting it. It is a fact about perception and not about arithmetic, and it turns up in number systems all over the world: the commonest group sizes in history are $2$, $5$, $10$ and $20$, and every one of them is either at that limit or a small multiple of it — or, in the case of $10$ and $20$, a count of fingers and toes.

Some systems grouped by two and went no further. The Gumulgal of Australia counted like this:

numbernamewhat it says
1uraponone
2ukasartwo
3ukasar-urapontwo and one
4ukasar-ukasartwo and two
5ukasar-ukasar-urapontwo, two and one
6ukasar-ukasar-ukasartwo, two and two

Anything beyond six was simply many. The names are built by repeating the word for two, which is the idea of grouping showing up in speech rather than in writing.
