A base

A number system whose first landmark is $1$ and whose every later landmark is $n$ times the one before is a base-$n$ system. Its landmarks are exactly the powers of $n$. Base $10$ is also called decimal.

Example 3

Write $143$ in a base-$5$ system.

Take as many of the largest landmark that fits, then work down.

largest that fits $125$, and one of them leaves $18$
then $18 = 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1$
so $143 = 125 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1$

One hundred-and-twenty-five, three fives and three ones. In Egyptian style that would be one symbol of the third landmark, three of the second and three of the first — seven symbols in all, where base ten would have needed eight.

Notice that no landmark is ever needed five or more times. If it were, five of them would combine into one of the next landmark up, and the grouping was not finished.

That last remark holds for every base. In a base-$n$ system no landmark is ever needed $n$ times or more, because $n$ of them make the next one — and it is the single most useful fact about bases, because it says exactly how many of each symbol a numeral can carry.

3.5.1 Why a base makes arithmetic easy

Adding in a base system is the trading of Example 2 with one improvement: the rate never changes. Ten of anything makes the next thing up, all the way. In the Roman system you had to remember that five Is make a V but two Vs make an X; here there is one rule and it is the same at every level.

The real gain is in multiplication, and it comes from a single fact.

$10 \times 100$ $= 10^1 \times 10^2 = 10^3 = 1000$
$100 \times 1000$ $= 10^2 \times 10^3 = 10^5$
