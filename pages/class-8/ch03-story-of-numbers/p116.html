7 The powers of three are $1, 3, 9, 27, 81, 243, 729, 2187$. The largest inside $1000$ is $729$, so the numeral runs from the $729$ place down to the units: seven places. (c) leaves out the units place.
9 Divide: $\tfrac18 = 0.125$, $\tfrac1{16} = 0.0625$ and $\tfrac1{20} = 0.05$, and each division stops. Dividing $1$ by $6$ gives $0.1666\ldots$: after the first step the remainder is $4$ every time, so the division never ends.
11 $121$ in base $3$ is $9 + 6 + 1 = 16$; in base $5$ it is $25 + 10 + 1 = 36$. The difference is $20$. (a) and (d) are the two values themselves.
12 The first digit has three choices and the other two have four each, so $3 \times 4 \times 4 = 48$. (b) is $4^3$, which lets a numeral begin with $0$.
13 $606_{\,7} = 6 \times 49 + 0 \times 7 + 6 = 300$. (b) reads the numeral as base ten; (c) leaves out the units; (d) puts the first $6$ in the sevens place.
14 The smallest is $1000_{\,8} = 8^3 = 512$ and the largest is $7777_{\,8} = 8^4 - 1 = 4095$. (a) is the three-place range and (c) the five-place range.
15 $2 \times 360 + 0 \times 20 + 5 = 725$. (a) uses $400$ for the third landmark, as a true base $20$ would; (b) reads the places as base-ten digits; (d) uses $3600$.
17 $100_{\,6} = 6^2 = 36$, because the third place is the thirty-sixes place. R is true, but it is about how many symbols there are, not what a place is worth. So the answer is (b).
18 $33_{\,4} = 3 \times 4 + 3 = 15$, so A is true. The second place is the fours place; the sixteens place is the third. So R is false, and the answer is (c).

The mathematics of this chapter fits in four lines. A base-$n$ system has landmarks $n^0, n^1, n^2, \ldots$. It needs exactly $n$ symbols, one of which is zero. No place ever holds $n$ or more. And the numeral $10$ is the base itself. Everything else was an illustration.

But the habit is worth more than the facts. Twice in the first stage, a question that looked like it needed a calculation was answered by asking what a system must do. Base one is impossible; the Mesopotamian numeral can be read more than one way. Neither could have been settled by working examples. Learning to tell those questions apart from the arithmetical ones is most of what this section has been for.

And there is one thing to carry that is not mathematics at all. Every system here was a reasonable answer to the problem as its inventors met it, and each failed only when asked to do something it was never built for. That is worth remembering the next time a method you have been taught stops working: usually it has not gone wrong, you have simply walked past the edge of what it was for.
