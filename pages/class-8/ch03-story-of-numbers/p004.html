3.4Landmark Numbers

Grouping by fives is a start, but a number like $1345$ written as two hundred and sixty-nine groups of five and no more is not much of an improvement on writing it in strokes. What is missing is a second level of grouping — groups of groups.

The Roman system does exactly that. It grew out of an older Greek one around the eighth century BCE, and was used across Europe for the better part of two thousand years. It has a short list of numbers with symbols of their own:

IVXLCDM
1510501005001000

Call these the landmark numbers of the system. To write any number, take as many of the largest landmark as will fit, then as many of the next, and so on down.

Example 1

Write $2367$ in Roman numerals.

Work down the landmarks, taking as many of each as will fit.

Solution.

Step 1 $2367$ $= 1000 + 1000 + 100 + 100 + 100 + 50 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1$ largest landmarks first
Step 2 M M C C C L X V I I a symbol for each
Step 3 $1000 + 1000 + 100 \times 3 + 50 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 2367$ add back to check
Answer MMCCCLXVII

One extra rule is worth knowing. Rather than write $4$ as IIII, the Romans often put the smaller symbol before the larger to mean subtraction: IV is $5-1$, IX is $10-1$, XL is $50-10$, CM is $1000-100$. They were not consistent about it — IIII appears on plenty of monuments and clock faces — but the rule shortens the awkward cases.

The subtractive rule only ever borrows one step down: IV and IX are allowed, IL and IC are not. So $49$ is XLIX — forty then nine — and not IL.
