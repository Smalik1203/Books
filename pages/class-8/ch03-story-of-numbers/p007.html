Before leaving the Romans, it is worth being clear about what their system does and does not have. It does group, and at several levels: a C is a hundred ones, an M is ten Cs. It does not group at a constant rate — five Is make a V but two Vs make an X — and there is no rule that generates the next landmark from the last. The list $1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000$ has to be memorised, because nothing about it can be worked out.

That is the crack in the system, and the next section is entirely about widening it.

3.4.1 Where the Roman system runs out

Adding in Roman numerals is not bad. Put the two numbers side by side, gather the symbols of each kind, and trade upward whenever you have enough.

Example 3

Add CCXXXIII and CCCCXXII without converting to our numerals.

Solution.

Step 1 Cs: CC and CCCC make six gather like symbols
Step 2 Xs: XXX and XX make five
Step 3 Is: III and II make five
Step 4 five Is make a V, and five Xs make an L trade upward
Step 5 five Cs make a D, leaving one C over
Answer DCLV, which is $655$

Notice the trading: five Is became a V and five Xs an L, but it takes two Vs to make an X and two Ls to make a C. The rate changes as you go up, and each rate has to be remembered.

Multiplying is where it stops working. What is $\text{VII} \times \text{IX}$? There is no rule to reach for. You cannot say what a C times an X is without knowing what number the answer wants to be called, and no symbol pattern produces it. The landmarks $1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1000$ multiply into each other in no orderly way at all.

$\text{V} \times \text{X}$ $= 50 = \text{L}$— a landmark, by luck
$\text{V} \times \text{V}$ $= 25 = \text{XXV}$— not a landmark at all
$\text{L} \times \text{L}$ $= 2500 = \text{MMD}$— nor this

So multiplication had to be done on a machine. Merchants across the Roman world used a counting board — an abacus — with a line for each power of ten, and only specially trained clerks could work one quickly. The numerals recorded the answer; they were no help in finding it.
