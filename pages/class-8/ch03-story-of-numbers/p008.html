3.5The Idea of a Base

The trouble with the Roman landmarks is that they are an arbitrary list. Fix that, and almost everything else fixes itself.

Around 3000 BCE the Egyptians were using a system whose landmarks were chosen by a rule rather than by taste. Start at $1$. Ten of those make the next landmark, $10$. Ten of those make the next, $100$. Ten of those, $1000$ — and so on, each landmark ten times the one before.

Each landmark got a picture of its own — a stroke, a heel bone, a coil of rope, a lotus — and a number was written by drawing as many of each as it contained.

The order of the symbols does not matter, since each one carries its own value wherever it stands. Three coils, two arches and four strokes make $324$ however they are arranged on the stone, and Egyptian scribes did sometimes stack them or write them right to left.

Now the important part. Because the landmarks were chosen by a rule, they are not an arbitrary list at all — they are the powers of ten:

$10^0 = 1, \quad 10^1 = 10, \quad 10^2 = 100, \quad 10^3 = 1000, \ \ldots$

And once you notice that, you notice that ten was a choice. Any number would do. Start at $1$, decide that five of any landmark make the next, and you get a different but perfectly workable system:

landmarks $1, \quad 5, \quad 25, \quad 125, \quad 625, \quad 3125, \ \ldots$
that is $5^0, \quad 5^1, \quad 5^2, \quad 5^3, \quad 5^4, \quad 5^5, \ \ldots$

That second line is the whole idea. The landmarks are no longer a list somebody chose; they are generated, one from the last, by a rule you can state in seven words. Ask for the tenth landmark and you do not have to consult anybody — it is $5^9$. Ask the Romans for their tenth landmark and there is no answer, because after M they simply stopped.
