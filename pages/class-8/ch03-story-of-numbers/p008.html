The counting board worked because it was a place value system in disguise. Each line stood for a power of ten, and a counter meant one, ten, a hundred or a thousand depending on which line it lay on. To calculate, a clerk moved counters and traded ten on one line for one on the next. The Romans used the idea of position every day on their boards. They just never wrote it down.

There is a second cost, quieter but just as real. Because the calculating was a craft, it belonged to the few people trained in it. A merchant who could read MMDCCLVIII perfectly well still had to hand the sum to a clerk. When a notation arrives that lets an ordinary person multiply on paper, arithmetic stops being a profession and becomes a skill. That is one reason why the change, when it came to Europe, mattered far beyond mathematics.

It is easy to look down on all of this from where we sit now, and it would be wrong to do so. The Roman system did what it was asked to do for nearly two thousand years: it recorded quantities, on stone and in ledgers, briefly and with no room for doubt. Nobody expected a numeral to help with the arithmetic, because in Rome the arithmetic was a separate craft done on a board by a specialist. What changed later was not that people got cleverer, but that a system arrived in which writing the number down and calculating with it were the same activity.

Hold on to that thought. The advantage of the modern system is not that it is shorter — $\text{MMDCCLVIII}$ and $2758$ are about the same length — but that its notation does arithmetic for you.
