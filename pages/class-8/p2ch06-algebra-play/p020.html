Summary
  1. A letter-number such as $x$ stands for any number. Working a trick with $x$ shows that it works for every number, not only for the numbers tried.
  2. A think-of-a-number trick has two parts: steps that make the unknown cancel, and a number that sets the answer. For example, double, add $10$, halve and take away the number always gives $5$.
  3. Two numbers can be packed into one. The date trick gives $100m + d$ plus a fixed number. Taking away that number leaves the month in the hundreds and the day in the last two places.
  4. In a number pyramid, each number is the sum of the two below it. Naming the empty boxes with letters turns a pyramid into equations. Putting one equation into another leaves one unknown.
  5. The top of a pyramid comes straight from its bottom row: $a + 2b + c$ for three rows, and $a + 3b + 3c + d$ for four. The multipliers count the routes from each bottom box to the top.
  6. On a calendar, a $2 \times 2$ square with top-left date $a$ adds to $4a + 16$. A $3 \times 3$ square adds to nine times its middle date.
  7. A two-digit number with digits $a$ and $b$ is $10a + b$. The number minus its reverse is $9(a - b)$, and the number plus its reverse is $11(a + b)$.
  8. To make $\square\,\square \times \square$ as large as possible, use the largest digit as the multiplier and put the other two in decreasing order.
  9. A story can be undone backwards, step by step in reverse order. It can also be solved forwards, by calling the start $x$ and solving the equation at the end.
