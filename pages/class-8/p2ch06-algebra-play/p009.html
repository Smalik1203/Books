6.6A Square on a Calendar

Here is a trick that needs no instructions at all. Hand a friend a page from a calendar and ask them to draw a square round any four dates that touch, add the four, and tell you the total. From that one number you name all four dates.

What makes it possible is that the four dates are not four free numbers. A calendar row runs in ones and a calendar column runs in sevens, so once the top-left date is known the other three follow.

Call the top-left date $a$. Then the square holds $a$, $a + 1$ below it in the same week, $a + 7$ in the week after, and $a + 8$ in the corner, and the four add to

$$a + (a+1) + (a+7) + (a+8) = 4a + 16.$$

For the shaded square $a = 6$, and $4 \times 6 + 16 = 40$, which is the total counted out on the calendar. Going the other way is now an equation with one unknown: told the total is $36$, subtract $16$ and divide by $4$ to get $a = 5$, so the square is $5$, $6$, $12$, $13$.

Only one number in $4a + 16$ came from the calendar, and it is the $7$ hiding inside the $16$: the four dates were $a$, $a+1$, $a+7$ and $a+8$, so the constant is $0 + 1 + 7 + 8$. Everything else came from there being four of them. A calendar with a ten-day week — and there have been such calendars — would give $a$, $a+1$, $a+10$, $a+11$ and a total of $4a + 22$, and the trick would work exactly as well with a different number to subtract.

That is a question worth putting to any formula: which of its numbers came from the situation, and which came from the shape of the question asked? Here the $4$ counts the dates in the square, and the $16$ is the only place the length of the week gets in. Change the week and just one of the two moves.
