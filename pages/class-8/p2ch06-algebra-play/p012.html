6.8The Largest Product

Fill the digits $2$, $3$ and $5$ into the boxes of $\square\,\square \times \square$, using each digit once. Which arrangement gives the largest product?

There are six arrangements, since any of the three digits can go in the first box and the remaining two can then be placed in two ways.

multiplier 2 $35 \times 2 = 70$ and $53 \times 2 = 106$
multiplier 3 $25 \times 3 = 75$ and $52 \times 3 = 156$
multiplier 5 $23 \times 5 = 115$ and $32 \times 5 = 160$

The winner is $32 \times 5 = 160$, and it is worth seeing why rather than reading it off the list. The six arrangements have been grouped in pairs that share a multiplier, and inside each pair the one with the larger two-digit number obviously wins, so three of the six can be dropped at once. That leaves $53 \times 2$, $52 \times 3$ and $32 \times 5$.

Comparing what is left is where the algebra earns its place. Split each product into its tens part and its units part.

$32 \times 5$ $(30 \times 5) + (2 \times 5) = 150 + 10$
$52 \times 3$ $(50 \times 3) + (2 \times 3) = 150 + 6$

The first parts are equal, and not by accident: in both, the tens digit and the multiplier are the two largest digits, so both come to $10 \times 3 \times 5$. The whole comparison therefore falls on the second parts, where the smallest digit $2$ is multiplied by whichever of $3$ and $5$ is doing the multiplying. It should be multiplied by the larger.

That argument used nothing about the digits $2$, $3$ and $5$ except their order, so it will run again for any three. Let the digits be $p$, $q$ and $r$ with $p < q < r$. Dropping the loser of each pair leaves $rq \times p$, $rp \times q$ and $qp \times r$, and writing each out settles them.

$rq \times p$ $10rp + qp$
$rp \times q$ $10rq + pq$
$qp \times r$ $10qr + pr$

The first two share the term $pq$, so the comparison is $10rp$ against $10rq$, and $q > p$ settles it for the second. The second and third share $10qr$, so the comparison is $pq$ against $pr$, and $r > q$ settles it for the third.

Largest digit multiplies, the rest go in order

To make $\square\,\square \times \square$ as large as possible from three digits, use the biggest digit as the multiplier and put the other two in decreasing order to make the two-digit number: $qp \times r$ when $p < q < r$. Six cases were checked in the first instance and none in the second, because the letters covered them all.
