A genie doubles a traveller's coins each time he walks round a tree, and takes $c$ coins after each round. The traveller starts with $45$ and, after four rounds, has nothing at all. What was $c$?

The chapter's expression for the coins after $n$ rounds does this one without any further thought. Put $x = 45$, $n = 4$, and set the whole thing to nothing.

the expression $2^{\,4}(45 - c) + c = 0$
expanding $720 - 16c + c = 0$
so $15c = 720$, giving $c = 48$

Eight questions, and not one of them needed an idea the chapter had not already given. What they needed was the willingness to write the situation down before looking for the answer: three consecutive numbers as $a$, $a+1$, $a+2$, a two-digit number as $10a + b$, a whole bargain as $2^{\,n}(x - c) + c$. In every one of the eight, the line that did the work was the first line.

Two of them also needed something the algebra could not supply. The digits had to stop at $9$, and the coins had to be whole. An expression will happily hand back $a = 4.5$ or a charge of two-thirds of a coin, and knowing which answers the situation can actually accept is the reader's job and not the equation's.

Type 1 · Think-of-a-number tricks

Example 1

Think of a number, multiply it by $3$, add $12$, divide by $3$, and subtract the number you thought of. Show that everybody ends at the same number, and find it.

Solution.

Step 1 think of $x$; multiply by $3$: $3x$
Step 2 add $12$: $3x + 12$
Step 3 divide by $3$: $x + 4$ both terms divided
Step 4 subtract $x$: $x + 4 - x = 4$ the unknown cancels
Answer Everybody ends at $4$, whatever the number
