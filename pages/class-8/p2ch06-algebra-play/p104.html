The same division runs through the chapter. The pyramid formula says what the top is once a bottom row is chosen; which bottom row to choose is a separate question. Knowing which of the two you are being asked is most of knowing where to start.

A four-row pyramid has $204$ at the top, and its bottom row is four consecutive whole numbers. Find them.

Four consecutive numbers are $a$, $a+1$, $a+2$, $a+3$, and the top of a four-row pyramid is $a + 3b + 3c + d$ with those put in for $b$, $c$ and $d$.

the top $a + 3(a+1) + 3(a+2) + (a+3)$
collecting $8a + 12$
so $8a + 12 = 204$, giving $a = 24$

The row is $24$, $25$, $26$, $27$. Compare it with the three-row answer, which was $4a + 4$: both tops are $2^{\,n-1}$ times the smallest number, plus a constant. The doubling comes from the number of rows and nothing else, which is why the same bottom number can appear under two quite different tops.

A game stall doubles a player's tokens in each round, and takes $c$ tokens after each round. A player starts with $45$ and, after four rounds, has nothing at all. What was $c$?

The chapter's expression for the tokens after $n$ rounds does this one without any further thought. Put $x = 45$, $n = 4$, and set the whole thing to nothing.

the expression $2^{\,4}(45 - c) + c = 0$
expanding $720 - 16c + c = 0$
so $15c = 720$, giving $c = 48$

Eight questions, and not one of them needed an idea the chapter had not already given. What they needed was the willingness to write the situation down before looking for the answer: three consecutive numbers as $a$, $a+1$, $a+2$, a two-digit number as $10a + b$, a whole game as $2^{\,n}(x - c) + c$. In every one of the eight, the line that did the work was the first line.

Two of them also needed something the algebra could not supply. The digits had to stop at $9$, and the tokens had to be whole. An expression will happily hand back $a = 4.5$ or a fee of two-thirds of a token, and knowing which answers the situation can actually accept is the reader's job and not the equation's.
