Set C, 7. Options (a) and (b) are the two cases where the deal fails: below the charge the traveller's shortfall doubles each round, and at the charge exactly he stands still for ever. Only above it does the doubling work in his favour, which is what $2^{\,n}(x - c) + c$ says when the bracket is positive.

What to carry forward

Write the situation down before doing anything to it. A two-digit number is $10a + b$. Three consecutive numbers are $a$, $a+1$, $a+2$. Four calendar dates in a square are $a$, $a+1$, $a+7$, $a+8$. In almost every question in this division the difficulty was over as soon as that line had been written, and in almost every one the temptation was to start calculating first. Write first.

Count the unknowns before choosing a method. A condition with one unknown in it gives a number. A condition with two gives a relationship, and needs either a second condition or some fact from outside the algebra: that a digit stops at $9$, that a date stops at $31$, that a count of flowers is a whole number. Knowing which of the two you are holding saves a great deal of hunting for an answer that was never there.

A formula answers more than it was asked. The chapter's expressions kept giving away a second fact for free. $4a + 16$ says which totals are impossible as well as which square produced a given one. $4a + 4$ says that a pyramid of consecutive numbers can never be topped by $101$. $2^{\,n}(x - c) + c$ says who should take the genie's bargain. When you have written something in letters, it is worth a moment to ask what else it is telling you.

The chapter opened with somebody appearing to read a mind and closed with the arithmetic that made it look that way. Nothing was lost in the exchange. A trick explained is a trick you can rebuild, aim at a different answer, test on the awkward cases and hand to somebody else. And the explaining took nothing more than agreeing to write down the number you were not allowed to know.
