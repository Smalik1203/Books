6.2A Date in One Number

The next trick gets two numbers back out of one. Ask a friend to think of a date, such as Children’s Day, the $14$th day of the $11$th month. Then ask them to follow these steps.

multiply the month by 4 $11 \times 4 = 44$
add 7 $44 + 7 = 51$
multiply by 5 $51 \times 5 = 255$
add 3 $255 + 3 = 258$
multiply by 5 $258 \times 5 = 1290$
add the day $1290 + 14 = 1304$

Your friend tells you only the answer, $1304$. Take away $190$ to get $1114$, and read it as month $11$, day $14$. To see why this works, follow the same steps with a month $m$ and a day $d$.

after the first three $(4m + 7) \times 5 = 20m + 35$
then $(20m + 35 + 3) \times 5 = 100m + 190$
and the day $100m + 190 + d$

So the $190$ is there to be taken away, and what is left is $100m + d$. Two choices make the trick work. First, the three multipliers are $4$, $5$ and $5$, and $4 \times 5 \times 5 = 100$. So the month ends up in the hundreds. Second, a day is never more than $31$. So $d$ is always less than $100$, and it cannot spill over into the month’s place.

What are the steps add 7 and add 3 for? They only put in the $190$ that is taken away later. They hide the design. Without them, the steps would be: multiply the month by $4$, then by $5$, then by $5$, and add the day. Anyone could then see that the month has just been multiplied by $100$. If you change add 7 to add 8, the number to take away changes from $190$ to $215$.

The same design packs any two numbers into one, as long as the multiplier is bigger than the second number can ever be.

Two numbers in one
  • In $100m + d$, the month $m$ sits in the hundreds and the day $d$ in the last two places.
  • This works because the day $d$ is always less than $100$.
