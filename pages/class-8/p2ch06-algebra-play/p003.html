6.3A Date in One Number

The next trick does something the first one did not: it gets two numbers back out of one. Ask somebody to think of a date — Republic Day, say, which is the twenty-sixth of the first month. Then:

multiply the month by 5 $1 \times 5 = 5$
add 6 $5 + 6 = 11$
multiply by 4 $11 \times 4 = 44$
add 9 $44 + 9 = 53$
multiply by 5 $53 \times 5 = 265$
add the day $265 + 26 = 291$

Told only that the answer is $291$, you subtract $165$ and get $126$ — and read it as the first month, twenty-sixth day. Follow the same steps with a month $m$ and a day $d$ and it is no longer surprising.

after the first three $(5m + 6) \times 4 = 20m + 24$
then $(20m + 24 + 9) \times 5 = 100m + 165$
and the day $100m + 165 + d$

So the $165$ is there to be taken off, and what is left is $100m + d$. Two choices in the design are doing all the work, and both are worth naming. The three multipliers are $5$, $4$ and $5$, whose product is $100$ — which is why the month ends up in the hundreds. And a day is never more than $31$, so $d$ cannot reach $100$ and spill over into the month's place. Read $126$ as one hundred, and twenty-six left over and the date falls out.

Which raises a fair question about the other two steps. If the whole design is the $\times 5$, $\times 4$, $\times 5$, what are the add 6 and the add 9 for? They contribute nothing but the $165$ that has to be taken off again. They are camouflage. Strip them out and the instructions read multiply the month by five, then by four, then by five, and add the day — at which point anybody can see that the month has merely been multiplied by a hundred, and the trick is over before it starts.

The same design packs any two numbers into one, so long as the multiplier is chosen larger than anything the second number can reach. A month and a day need a hundred. A month, a day and a two-digit year would need ten thousand — and a trick built to hide three numbers is a trick with a great many steps.
