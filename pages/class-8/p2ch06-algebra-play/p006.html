substituting $(9 + c) + (c + 7) = 50$
collecting $2c + 16 = 50$, so $2c = 34$
giving $c = 17$, and then $a = 26$ and $b = 24$

Nothing was guessed. The rule was written down, and the algebra did the rest. This is the second use of algebra in this chapter. The first use was to explain a trick. This one finds numbers we did not know.

Example 3

A three-row pyramid has $56$ at the top, and $6$ and $12$ at the two ends of its bottom row. Complete it.

Solution.

Step 1 call the middle $c$, so the second row is $6 + c$ and $c + 12$ the rule, twice
Step 2 $(6 + c) + (c + 12) = 56$ the top
Step 3 $2c + 18 = 56$, giving $c = 19$
Check the second row is $25$ and $31$, and $25 + 31 = 56$
Answer bottom row $6$, $19$, $12$; second row $25$, $31$; top $56$

How many numbers must be given for a three-row pyramid to have just one answer? Three. The bottom row has three boxes, and everything above follows from them. Give only the top and one end, and the equation has two unknowns. Then many pyramids fit. Give four numbers, and they may not agree. A top of $56$ over the bottom row $6$, $20$, $12$ is not a pyramid, because $26 + 32 = 58$.

Solving a pyramid
  • Name the empty boxes with letters, and write the rule as equations.
  • Put one equation into another until only one letter is left.
