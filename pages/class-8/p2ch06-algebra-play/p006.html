Three equations look like more than one unknown's worth of work, and they are not: the last two say what $a$ and $b$ are in terms of $c$, so putting them into the first leaves an equation in $c$ alone.

substituting $(12 + c) + (c + 8) = 60$
collecting $2c + 20 = 60$, so $2c = 40$
giving $c = 20$, and then $a = 32$ and $b = 28$

Notice what carried the argument. Nothing was guessed, and no pyramid was tried out to see whether it fitted. The rule was written down as it stood, and the writing did the rest — which is the second use of algebra in this chapter, and the more useful one. The first was to explain a trick already known to work; this is to find something not yet known at all.

Example 3

A three-row pyramid has $44$ at the top and $7$ and $9$ at the two ends of its bottom row. Complete it.

call the middle $c$, so the second row is $7 + c$ and $c + 9$
the top $(7 + c) + (c + 9) = 44$
so $2c + 16 = 44$, giving $c = 14$
and the row above $21$ and $23$, which do add to $44$

The last line is the check, and it is free — the two numbers were built from $c$, so if they fail to add to $44$ the arithmetic went wrong somewhere earlier.

How much has to be given before a pyramid has one answer? Three numbers were supplied both times, and three is what it takes: a three-row pyramid has three free entries along its bottom, and everything above them follows. Supply only the top and one end and the equation left over has two unknowns in it, so there are many pyramids and no way to choose between them. Supply four numbers and you may have supplied a contradiction — a top of $44$ over a bottom row of $7$, $10$ and $9$ simply is not a pyramid, and the algebra says so by contradicting itself rather than by handing you a wrong answer.

Both faults are worth recognising on sight, because they ask for opposite things. Too little given means you must go back for another number; too much given means one of the numbers you have is wrong. Neither is a failure of arithmetic, and neither is repaired by doing the arithmetic again more carefully.
