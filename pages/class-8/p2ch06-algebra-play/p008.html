Think and Reflect

Write the first three Virahānka–Fibonacci numbers $1$, $2$, $3$ along the bottom of a three-row pyramid, where each number is the sum of the two before it, and fill it in.

Every entry that appears is itself a Virahānka–Fibonacci number. Say why that had to happen — and then say which of them stands at the top of a pyramid with four rows, and with twenty-nine.

Exercise Set 6.2
  1. Without building the pyramid, find the topmost number for each bottom row.
    1. $4$, $13$, $8$
    2. $7$, $11$, $3$
    3. $10$, $14$, $25$
  1. Do the same for these bottom rows of four.
    1. $8$, $19$, $21$, $13$
    2. $7$, $18$, $19$, $6$
    3. $9$, $7$, $5$, $11$
  1. Write the expression for the top of a five-row pyramid in terms of its bottom row $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, $e$. Check your multipliers against Fig. 6.5 by extending it one row.
  1. Complete each three-row pyramid.
    1. Top $50$, bottom ends $4$ and $6$.
    2. Top $40$, bottom ends $5$ and $7$.
    3. Top $36$, bottom ends $9$ and $7$.
  1. A three-row pyramid has $2$, $5$ and $2$ along the bottom, and another has $4$, $4$ and $2$. Both tops are $14$. Find a third bottom row of whole numbers that also gives $14$, and explain how you found it.
  1. A four-row pyramid has $100$ at the top, and its bottom row is $6$, $x$, $x$, $10$. Find $x$. Then explain why no such pyramid can have $101$ at the top.
  1. Extend Fig. 6.5 by one more row, for a five-row pyramid. Which box in the bottom row has the most routes to the top, and how many? If you may raise just one bottom entry by $1$, which one lifts the top the most?
