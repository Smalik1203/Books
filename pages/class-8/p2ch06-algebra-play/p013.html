Largest digit multiplies, the rest go in order

To make $\square\,\square \times \square$ as large as possible from three digits, use the biggest digit as the multiplier and put the other two in decreasing order to make the two-digit number: $qp \times r$ when $p < q < r$. Six cases were checked in the first instance and none in the second, because the letters covered them all.

Example 5

Arrange $3$, $5$ and $9$ to make the largest product, and check the rule against the runner-up.

by the rule $r = 9$, and $q = 5$, $p = 3$, giving $53 \times 9 = 477$
runner-up $93 \times 5 = 465$

Both use $10 \times 5 \times 9 = 450$ for the tens part, and the $12$ that separates them is $3 \times 9$ against $3 \times 5$. The gap between first and second is exactly $3 \times (9 - 5)$, which is the argument again in numbers.

6.9Turning a Number Round

It is Mukta's turn again. Choose a two-digit number whose digits differ, she says, and keep it to yourself. Reverse the digits to make a second number. Subtract the smaller from the larger. Now divide by $9$ — and there will be no remainder.

Shubham takes $47$, reverses it to $74$, and finds $74 - 47 = 27$, which is $3$ nines exactly. He tries $52$: reversed it is $25$, the difference is $27$ again, and again it divides. He tries $91$ and $19$ and gets $72$, which is $8$ nines. Whatever he starts with, the nine is there.

A two-digit number is $10a + b$

The digits $a$ and $b$ written side by side do not mean $a$ times $b$. They mean $10a + b$, because the left digit counts tens. Writing that down is the one move every trick in this section needs, and without it there is nothing algebra can get hold of.

With the number written as $10a + b$, its reverse is $10b + a$, and the subtraction is three lines of collecting.
