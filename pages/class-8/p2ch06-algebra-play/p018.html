6.11Inventing Your Own

Ten sections, and every one of them had the same skeleton. There is a quantity the audience knows and you do not. There are operations arranged so that quantity either cancels or is driven into a place value of its own. And there is a constant, chosen by you, that decides where the whole thing lands.

Building one is therefore done in the wrong order: write the algebra first, and turn it into instructions afterwards. Here is a new trick made that way. A two-digit number is $10a + b$, the sum of its digits is $a + b$, and taking one from the other leaves

$$(10a + b) - (a + b) = 9a.$$

The $b$ has gone, and what is left is nine times the tens digit. So: think of a two-digit number, add its two digits together, and subtract that from the number you thought of. The answer is always a multiple of nine — and if you are told the answer you can name the tens digit, because it is the answer divided by nine. Shubham's $47$ gives $47 - 11 = 36$, and $36 \div 9 = 4$.

One habit is worth keeping when you invent. Test the trick on the awkward cases before you show anyone: a number with a zero in it, a number whose digits are equal, the largest input allowed. That is where the wording turns out to have said something you did not mean, and it is easier to find yourself than in front of an audience.
