The same trick, one row taller

Four consecutive numbers are $a$, $a+1$, $a+2$, $a+3$, and the top of a four-row pyramid is $a + 3b + 3c + d$ with those put in for $b$, $c$ and $d$.

the top $a + 3(a+1) + 3(a+2) + (a+3)$
collecting $8a + 12$
so $8a + 12 = 204$, giving $a = 24$

The row is $24$, $25$, $26$, $27$. Compare it with the three-row answer, which was $4a + 4$: both tops are $2^{\,n-1}$ times the smallest number, plus a constant. The doubling comes from the number of rows and nothing else, which is why the same bottom number can appear under two quite different tops.

A charge set to empty a pocket

The chapter's expression for the coins after $n$ rounds does this one without any further thought. Put $x = 15$, $n = 4$, and set the whole thing to nothing.

the expression $2^{\,4}(15 - c) + c = 0$
expanding $240 - 16c + c = 0$
so $15c = 240$, giving $c = 16$

Sixteen, against a purse of fifteen — the same shape as Karim's eight against seven, and the same one-coin margin. That is not a coincidence: emptying a traveller in $n$ rounds needs a charge of $2^{\,n}x \div (2^{\,n} - 1)$, and for a start that is one less than a power of two, that division comes out at a whole number exactly one bigger. A genie who wants round figures picks travellers with $7$, $15$ or $31$ coins.

Eight questions, and not one of them needed an idea the chapter had not already given. What they needed was the willingness to write the situation down before looking for the answer — three consecutive numbers as $a$, $a+1$, $a+2$; a two-digit number as $10a + b$; a whole bargain as $2^{\,n}(x - c) + c$. In every one of the eight, the line that did the work was the first line.

Two of them also needed something the algebra could not supply. The digits had to stop at $9$, and the coins had to be whole. An expression will happily hand back $a = 4.5$ or a charge of two-thirds of a coin, and knowing which answers the situation can actually accept is the reader's job and not the equation's.
