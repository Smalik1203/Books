6.10Inventing Your Own

Every trick in this chapter has the same parts. There is a number the other person knows and you do not. There are steps that make that number cancel, or push it into a place of its own. And there is a number, chosen by you, that decides the answer.

So build a trick backwards. Write the algebra first, and turn it into steps afterwards. Here is a new trick made that way. A two-digit number is $10a + b$, and the sum of its digits is $a + b$. Take one from the other.

$$(10a + b) - (a + b) = 9a$$

The $b$ has gone, and what is left is nine times the tens digit. So the trick is: think of a two-digit number, add its two digits, and take that sum away from the number. The answer is always a multiple of $9$. If you are told the answer, divide it by $9$ to get the tens digit. For example, $26$ gives $26 - 8 = 18$, and $18 \div 9 = 2$.

Before you show a trick to anyone, test the awkward cases: a number with a zero in it, a number whose digits are equal, and the largest number allowed. That is where a trick most often goes wrong.

Every section of this chapter began with something that looked clever and ended with some simple algebra. A trick is arithmetic arranged so that the number you cannot see cancels out. Once the trick is written in letters, nothing is hidden. So the next time someone shows you a trick, write it in letters and see how it works.
