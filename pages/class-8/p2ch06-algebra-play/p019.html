What this chapter established
  1. A letter-number stands for any number, so a calculation carried out in letters settles every case at once. That is the difference between noticing that something works and knowing why.
  2. A think-of-a-number trick has two parts: steps that cancel the unknown, and a constant that sets the answer. Design the cancelling first, and the answer is then yours to choose.
  3. The date trick returns two numbers from one because its three multipliers come to $100$, and a day never reaches $100$ and so cannot spill into the month's place.
  4. In a number pyramid each entry is the sum of the two below it. Filled upwards it is addition; filled downwards it is subtraction; and where neither works, name the empty boxes and let the rule become equations.
  5. The top of a pyramid is a weighted sum of its bottom row — $a + 2b + c$ for three rows, $a + 3b + 3c + d$ for four. Each multiplier counts the routes from that box up to the peak, and each row of multipliers is built from the one before by the pyramid's own rule.
  6. A calendar runs in ones across and sevens down, so a $2 \times 2$ square is $a$, $a+1$, $a+7$, $a+8$ and totals $4a + 16$ — always a multiple of $4$. A $3 \times 3$ square totals nine times the date at its centre.
  7. Shapes standing for numbers are letter-numbers in another costume. Start with a row holding only one kind of shape, because that row is an equation in one unknown.
  8. To make $\square\,\square \times \square$ as large as possible from digits $p < q < r$, take $qp \times r$: the largest digit multiplies, and the other two go in decreasing order.
  9. A two-digit number is $10a + b$, and writing it that way is what makes the reversal tricks yield. The difference from its reverse is $9(b - a)$; the sum is $11(a + b)$; cycling three digits and adding gives $111(a+b+c)$; and repeating a three-digit number multiplies it by $1001 = 7 \times 11 \times 13$.
  10. A chain of operations can be undone step by step, backwards, or written forwards as a single expression in $x$. The expression costs more to set up and is worth more, because it answers questions the story never asked.

Every section of this chapter began with something that looked like cleverness and ended with something that looked like bookkeeping. That is the whole transaction. A trick is a piece of arithmetic arranged so that what you cannot see cancels out, and once it is written in letters there is nothing left to see through.
