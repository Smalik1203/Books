A two-digit number is exactly four times the sum of its digits. Find every number for which this is true.

Write the number as $10a + b$ and the sum of its digits as $a + b$. The condition is then a single equation, and collecting it is the whole argument.

the condition $10a + b = 4(a + b)$
expanding $10a + b = 4a + 4b$
collecting $6a = 3b$, so $b = 2a$

One equation in two unknowns does not give a number, and it was never going to — it gives a relationship: the units digit is twice the tens digit. What turns that into a finite list is something the algebra does not know, namely that a digit runs from $0$ to $9$. So $a$ can be $1$, $2$, $3$ or $4$ and no more, giving $12$, $24$, $36$ and $48$. Check one: $4 + 8 = 12$, and $4 \times 12 = 48$.

Of all the ways of filling $\square\,\square \times \square$ with three different digits, none of them zero, which gives the largest product of all?

Two questions are hiding here, and the chapter answered only one of them. Its rule says how to arrange three given digits: largest as the multiplier, the other two in decreasing order, giving $qp \times r$. Which three digits to choose is a separate matter, and for the largest product the answer is unsurprising — take $7$, $8$ and $9$.

largest $87 \times 9 = 783$
the near miss $97 \times 8 = 776$

Those two questions are worth keeping apart, because they are answered by different kinds of argument and it is easy to slide from one to the other. A rule for arranging the digits you have been given is a piece of algebra: it was proved once, for every $p < q < r$, and it never has to be checked again. Choosing which digits to be given is a search, and it was settled by noticing that larger digits make larger products and then letting the rule arrange the largest three.
