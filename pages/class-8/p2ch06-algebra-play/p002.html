Now the trick can be changed on purpose. Add $6$ in place of $10$, and the fourth step leaves $x + 3$, so the answer is $3$. Add $20$, and the answer is $10$. In general, if you add $2k$, the trick ends at $k$, whatever number is chosen.

Example 1

Build a think-of-a-number trick that always ends at $6$, using different steps from Tanvi’s.

Solution.

Step 1 think of $x$ the unknown
Step 2 add $4$: $x + 4$
Step 3 multiply by $3$: $3x + 12$
Step 4 take away three times the number: $3x + 12 - 3x = 12$ $x$ cancels
Step 5 halve it: $12 \div 2 = 6$ sets the answer
Answer Add $4$, multiply by $3$, take away three times the number, and halve. It always ends at $6$

Plan a trick backwards. First decide how the $x$ will be removed. Then choose the other numbers so that what is left is the answer you want.

Every trick in this chapter has these two parts: one part removes the unknown, and one part sets the answer. Leave out the removing part and there is no trick. Think of a number, add five, double it ends at $2x + 10$, which is a different answer for every person.

Look again at the halving step in Tanvi’s trick. What if someone starts with an odd number? The step before the halving doubles the number, and adds an even number. So what reaches the halving is always even. A trick that divides must first make sure that the division comes out exactly.
