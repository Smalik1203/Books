A letter carries every number at once

Trying a trick on six numbers shows that it worked six times. Carrying it out on $x$ shows that it works, because $x$ was never any particular number. This is the difference between noticing a pattern and knowing one, and it is most of what algebra is for.

Now that the $4$ is exposed as the source of the answer, the trick can be steered. Add six instead of four and the fourth step leaves $x + 3$, so the answer is three. Add ten and it is five. In general, add $2k$ and the trick ends at $k$, whatever the audience thinks of.

Example 1

Build a think-of-a-number trick that always ends at $7$, using a different shape from Mukta's.

Solution.

Step 1 think of $x$ the unknown
Step 2 add 3: $x + 3$
Step 3 triple it: $3x + 9$
Step 4 subtract 3 times the original: $3x + 9 - 3x = 9$ the unknown cancels
Step 5 divide by 3, add 4: $3 + 4 = 7$ sets the answer
Answer Add 3, triple, subtract three times the number, divide by 3, add 4: it always ends at $7$

The design is done backwards. First decide how the $x$ will be removed — here by tripling and then subtracting three times the original — and only then arrange the constant so that what is left comes to $7$. Every trick in this section has that shape: a part that cancels the unknown, and a part that sets the answer.

Leave the cancelling out and there is no trick at all. Think of a number, add four, double it ends at $2x + 8$, which is a different answer for every audience, so the teller would have to be told the number — and then there is nothing left to guess.

One step deserves a second look, and it is the division. A reader might reasonably worry about somebody who begins with an odd number and is asked to halve something. Nobody is ever caught out: the step before it doubles, so whatever reaches the division is even. A trick that divides has to manufacture its own divisibility first, which is why the doubling stands where it does and not anywhere else.
