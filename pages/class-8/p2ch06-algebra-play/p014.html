6.8Turning a Number Round

Now it is Irfan’s turn to set a trick. “Choose a two-digit number whose digits are different,” he tells Tanvi. “Reverse its digits to make a second number. Take the smaller number from the larger. Now divide by $9$. There will be no remainder.”

Tanvi takes $26$. Reversed, it is $62$, and $62 - 26 = 36$, which is $4$ nines. She tries $73$. Reversed, it is $37$, and $73 - 37 = 36$ again. She tries $81$ and $18$, and gets $63$, which is $7$ nines. The nine is always there.

A two-digit number is $10a + b$

The digits $a$ and $b$ written side by side do not mean $a \times b$. They mean $10a + b$, because the left digit counts tens.

With the number written as $10a + b$, its reverse is $10b + a$. If $b$ is the larger digit, the difference is found like this.

the difference $(10b + a) - (10a + b)$
collecting $10b - b - 10a + a = 9b - 9a$
factorising $9(b - a)$

So the difference is $9$ times the gap between the digits. For $26$, the gap is $6 - 2 = 4$, and $36 = 9 \times 4$. If $a$ is the larger digit, the difference is $9(a - b)$. If the digits are equal, the difference is $0$, which is why Irfan asked for different digits.

Adding gives another pattern: $(10a + b) + (10b + a) = 11(a + b)$. So a number plus its reverse is $11$ times the sum of its digits. For example, $47 + 74 = 121$. The digits of $47$ add to $11$, and $121 = 11 \times 11$.
