The half-turn about $N$ takes $A$ to $C$, because $N$ is the midpoint of $AC$. Nothing is lost and nothing overlaps, so the parallelogram has the same area as the triangle. It stands on the same base with half the height, so its area is $b \times \tfrac{h}{2}$, which is $\tfrac12 \times b \times h$ again.

Now the changes can be joined in a chain. A triangle becomes a parallelogram, and a parallelogram becomes a rectangle. So a triangle becomes a rectangle in two steps. A rhombus can be cut into two triangles and follow the same path, and so can a trapezium.

Example 4

Give a method for changing a rectangle $9$ cm by $4$ cm into a triangle of the same area. How many such triangles are there?

Solution.

Step 1 $9 \times 4 = 36$ cm2 the area to keep
Step 2 $\tfrac12 \times 9 \times h = 36$, so $h = 8$ cm a triangle on the same base
Answer Keep the base of $9$ cm and use twice the height. There are infinitely many such triangles

There are infinitely many because, by section 7.4, the top corner can be anywhere on a line $8$ cm above the base. The base can change too: a triangle with base $4$ cm and height $18$ cm also has an area of $36$ cm2.

A formula tells a builder how big the new shape must be. But the builder also has to draw it on the ground, with a rope and pegs. That is why the Śulbasūtras give cutting instructions rather than formulas.
