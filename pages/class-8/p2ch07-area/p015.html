Think and Reflect

This chapter has cut a parallelogram into a rectangle, a triangle into a parallelogram, and a rhombus into two triangles. Each time, one shape was taken apart and the pieces made another of the same area.

Suppose you are handed any two polygons at all that happen to have the same area — a long thin triangle, say, and a regular hexagon. Do you think the first can always be cut into pieces that rebuild the second? Argue for your answer, then try it on a triangle and a square of equal area.

7.10Areas in Real Life

An A4 sheet is $21$ cm by $29.7$ cm, so it holds $623.7$ cm2. A school desk is perhaps eight or nine of those laid out; a classroom floor is a few hundred. Once the numbers get that large the unit changes, and changing an area unit is not the same as changing a length unit.

Furniture in India is often measured in inches and feet, and an inch is $2.54$ cm. A square inch is a square of side $2.54$ cm, so it holds

$$2.54 \times 2.54 = 6.4516 \text{ cm}^2.$$

That is the whole of the difficulty, and it catches people every time: doubling a length multiplies an area by four, and multiplying a length by $2.54$ multiplies an area by $6.45$. A conversion factor for lengths has to be squared before it can be used on areas.

The reason is the unit square itself. Change the unit of length and the unit square changes in both directions at once, which is one change too many for anybody's intuition. A room described as twice as large as another is a room whose floor holds twice as many tiles; a room described as twice as long is a room with four times the floor, if it grew in both directions, and only twice if it grew in one. Ask which was meant before believing either.
