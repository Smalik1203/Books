7.3Why Perimeter Is Not Area

Counting squares is slow. The perimeter, the length all the way round the edge, is quicker to measure: a piece of string will do. It seems likely that a bigger region has a longer edge. So why not use the perimeter to compare areas?

The answer is that it does not work. Look at these three rectangles. All their sides are whole numbers of centimetres.

RectanglePerimeter (cm)Area (cm2)
$9$ by $1$$20$$9$
$6$ by $4$$20$$24$
$4$ by $4$$16$$16$

The first two rectangles have the same perimeter, but the second holds more than twice as many squares. Now compare the first and the third. The $9$ by $1$ rectangle has the longer edge, $20$ cm against $16$ cm, and the smaller area. So a longer edge does not even promise a bigger area.

Perimeter and area measure different things. One cannot be used in place of the other. This is why a fence and the grass inside it are bought separately.

There is more to see here. Take all the rectangles with a perimeter of $20$ cm and whole-centimetre sides. Their two sides always add up to $10$ cm. The $9$ by $1$ rectangle holds $9$ squares, $8$ by $2$ holds $16$, $7$ by $3$ holds $21$, $6$ by $4$ holds $24$ and $5$ by $5$ holds $25$. The area grows as the two sides come closer together. It is largest when the rectangle is a square.

This is true for every perimeter, not only for $20$ cm. Among all rectangles with the same perimeter, the square has the largest area. Turned round, among all rectangles with the same area, the square has the shortest perimeter. This is why a field that needs the least fencing is close to a square.
