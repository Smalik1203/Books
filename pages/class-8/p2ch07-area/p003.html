The first two settle the first question: equal perimeters, and one holds two and a half times as much as the other. The first and third settle a stronger one. The $8$ by $1$ rectangle has the longer boundary of the two — $18$ cm against $12$ — and the smaller area. So a longer edge does not even guarantee more inside it.

Perimeter and area measure different things, and neither can stand in for the other. A fence and the grass it encloses are bought separately for exactly this reason: a field can be given more fence and less grass by making it long and thin, which is why nobody does it.

Something in the table is worth a second look, because it is the beginning of a real result. Among the rectangles of perimeter $18$ cm, the $5$ by $4$ holds the most. Try the others: $7$ by $2$ gives $14$, $6$ by $3$ gives $18$. The count climbs as the two sides come closer together, peaking when the rectangle is as close to square as whole numbers allow.

That is a general truth and not a coincidence of these numbers. Of all rectangles with a given perimeter, the square holds the most. Of all rectangles with a given area, the square has the shortest perimeter. It is why a field is fenced squarish, why a room is not built long and narrow if the floor is what is wanted, and why the cheapest box for a given amount of card is close to a cube.

That is the first of several places in this chapter where the extreme case turns out to be the symmetric one, and it is worth watching for. A shape that is as regular as its constraints allow is very often the largest, or the smallest, or the cheapest of its family.
