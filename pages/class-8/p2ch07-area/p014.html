The reason is the unit square itself. Change the unit of length and the unit square changes in both directions at once, which is one change too many for anybody's intuition. A room described as twice as large as another is a room whose floor holds twice as many tiles. A room described as twice as long is a room with four times the floor, if it grew in both directions, and only twice if it grew in one. Ask which was meant before believing either.

Example 5

Convert $10$ in2 to cm2, and $161.29$ cm2 to in2.

Solution.

Step 1 $10 \times 6.4516 = 64.516$ cm2 in2 to cm2: multiply
Step 2 $161.29 \div 6.4516 = 25$ in2 cm2 to in2: divide
Answer 64.516 cm2, and 25 in2

The check that stops a mistake is to ask which number should be bigger. A square inch is larger than a square centimetre, so a count in square inches is always the smaller of the two — $25$ against $161.29$. Get the division the wrong way round and the answer comes out at more than $1{,}040$, which fails that test at a glance.

Larger areas leave the metric scale behind altogether. A floor is given in square feet, and $1$ ft2 is $144$ in2 — twelve squared, not twelve. Land is sold by the acre, which is $43{,}560$ ft2, a number with no roundness in it because an acre began as the area a pair of oxen could plough in a day. A district is measured in square kilometres, and a square kilometre is $10^6$ square metres.

Different parts of India measure land in units of their own — the bigha, the gaj, the katha, the dhur, the cent, the ankanam. Most of them began as a working quantity of land rather than a defined one, so a bigha is not the same size in two states, and sometimes not in two districts. That is exactly why a standard unit was agreed on; it is also why the local names have survived every attempt to retire them.
