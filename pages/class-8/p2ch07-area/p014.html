The trapezium, in one cut

The first book found the area of a trapezium by taking two copies, turning one of them over, and fitting the pair together into a parallelogram. That works, and it needs a second trapezium. The half-turn of Fig. 7.9 does the same job with one.

Let the parallel sides be $a$ at the top and $b$ at the bottom, with height $h$ between them. Join the midpoints of the two slanting sides and cut along that line. Now turn the upper piece half a circle about one end of the cut, exactly as the triangle's top was turned about $N$. The side of length $a$ swings round and comes to rest in line with the side of length $b$, so what is left is a parallelogram — of base $a + b$, and of height $\tfrac{h}{2}$, since the cut was halfway up.

$$\text{area of a trapezium} = \tfrac12 \, h \, (a + b).$$

And now look back at Fig. 7.9 with that in mind. A triangle is a trapezium whose top side has shrunk to nothing — the case $a = 0$ — and the formula duly reads $\tfrac12 h(0 + b)$, which is $\tfrac12 bh$. The two arguments were never different arguments. The triangle was the trapezium all along, cut at the same halfway line and turned about the same kind of point.

A field is rarely a rectangle and almost never a rhombus. It is some shape with five or six corners and no name at all, and there is no formula waiting for it — but there does not need to be one.
