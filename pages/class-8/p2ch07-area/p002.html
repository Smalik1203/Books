7.2Counting Squares

A school garden has two vegetable beds. One bed is $6$ m long and $5$ m wide. The other is $7$ m long and $4$ m wide. The gardener wants to spread compost evenly over both. Which bed needs more compost?

To answer this, cover each bed with unit squares. Here a unit square is a square of side $1$ m. The squares must not overlap and must leave no gaps. Then count them.

The first bed holds $30$ unit squares and the second holds $28$. So the first bed needs more compost, even though the second bed is longer. We write these areas as $30$ m2 and $28$ m2, read as square metres, because we counted squares of side $1$ m.

Area is a count of unit squares

The area of a region is the number of unit squares that fit inside it. This number need not be a whole number: a region may hold three squares and half of a fourth. For a rectangle, the count is a product:

$$\text{area of a rectangle} = \text{length} \times \text{width}.$$

One more fact follows at once. A diagonal cuts a rectangle into two triangles. Turn one triangle round and it fits exactly on the other, so each triangle holds half of the squares. The $6$ m by $5$ m bed, cut from corner to corner, gives two triangles of $\tfrac12 \times 6 \times 5 = 15$ m2 each.
