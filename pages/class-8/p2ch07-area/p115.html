Choose the base before reaching for the formula. A triangle has three bases and a parallelogram two, and the pair you are given is not always the pair you want. Nearly every question in this division was decided by that choice: the rhombus gave up its height only after being read as a parallelogram, and the unmeasurable perpendicular was found by working the area out on one base and spending it on another.

Two parallel lines fix a height. That single sentence settled more of these questions than any formula did: the triangle and the parallelogram in the ratio $1 : 2$, the leaning triangle that loses nothing, the apex that may be slid past the end of its own base. Whenever a figure is described as standing between two parallels, the height is already known and only the base is still in question.

A scale factor works on lengths once and on areas twice. Trebling every side multiplies the paper by nine, an inch becomes $2.54$ cm but a square inch becomes $6.45$ cm2, and a room made twice as long in one direction only is not the same as one made twice as large. Ask which was stretched, and how many directions it was stretched in, before multiplying anything.

The chapter began by dividing a square into four equal parts in infinitely many ways and ended with a formula for a rhombus, and the road between them never left one idea: cut it up, put the pieces down somewhere more convenient, and count. The formulas are shorthand for that, and they are worth having. But a reader who remembers the cutting can rebuild any of them, and a reader who remembers only the formulas has to hope they are the right ones.
