Area

7.1Introduction

Take a rectangular sheet of paper and cut it into two pieces of the same size. The easy cuts are across the middle, down the middle, or along a diagonal. But there are many more. Any straight cut that passes through the centre of the sheet gives two pieces of equal size. The two pieces are not always the same shape, but each piece holds exactly half of the paper.

The amount of flat surface that a shape covers is called its area. The paper shows the idea that this whole chapter uses. Area does not depend on shape. Cut a shape into pieces and put the pieces together in a new way. The new shape covers the same area as the old one, because no paper was added and none was thrown away.

Each area formula in this chapter comes from one such cut. Earlier, you found the area of a parallelogram with a rule. In this chapter you will see where that rule comes from: cut a triangle off one end of the parallelogram and move it to the other end. The picture at the top of this page shows the whole argument.

This gives us a way to compare shapes that look very different. If the pieces of one shape can be laid down to cover another shape exactly, the two shapes have the same area. The rest of the chapter uses this idea again and again: it finds the area of a new shape by turning it into a shape whose area is already known.

Area and shape

Cutting a shape into pieces and joining the pieces in a new way changes the shape but not the area.
