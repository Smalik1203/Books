The Same Area, a Different Shape

7.1Introduction

Divide a square into four parts of equal area. Two ways come to anybody at once — four strips, or four smaller squares — and a third, cutting along both diagonals, comes almost as quickly. After that it begins to look as though the supply has run out.

It has not. Take any of those divisions and push one of the cutting lines sideways: whatever area a part loses along one edge it gains along the other, so all four parts still match. The line can be pushed by any amount at all, so there are infinitely many divisions, and hardly any of them make four congruent pieces.

That is the idea this whole chapter turns on. Area does not care what shape a region is in. Cut a shape into pieces and lay the pieces down somewhere else, and the area you finish with is the area you began with, because nothing was added and nothing thrown away.

Every area formula in this chapter is a record of one such rearrangement. The first book found the area of a trapezium by doubling it into a parallelogram, and took the parallelogram's own rule on trust. Here that rule is earned, by cutting a triangle off one end of the parallelogram and carrying it to the other. The picture on this page is the whole argument.

7.2Counting Squares

A rangoli is to be filled in evenly with coloured powder, and there are two patches to choose between: one $7$ cm by $4$ cm, the other $8$ cm by $3$ cm. Which will take more powder?

The question is settled by covering each patch with unit squares — squares of side $1$ cm — laid down without gaps and without overlaps, and counting them.
