7.6Cutting and Moving

A parallelogram has no neat rows of unit squares inside it. But it has a spare corner at one end and a matching gap at the other end. Its area formula comes from seeing that the corner fits the gap.

In the parallelogram $ABCD$, drop a line $AX$ from $A$ to $DC$ at a right angle. This cuts a right-angled triangle $AXD$ off the left-hand end. Move it across to the right-hand end, and the parallelogram becomes a rectangle.

We should not trust the eye that the piece fits. Three facts prove it. $BY$ equals $AX$, because $ABYX$ is a rectangle. Both triangles have a right angle, at $X$ and at $Y$. And $BC$ equals $AD$, because they are opposite sides of a parallelogram. A right angle, the hypotenuse and one other side: by the RHS rule, $\triangle AXD$ and $\triangle BYC$ are congruent. So one fits exactly over the other, with nothing left over.

Cutting a figure into pieces and putting them together to make a different figure of the same area is called dissection. The rest of this chapter uses this method again and again.

Now read the rectangle's sides from the parallelogram. Its height is $AX$, which is the height of the parallelogram. Its width is $XY$. Since $DX$ and $CY$ are matching sides of the two congruent triangles, they are equal. Adding $XC$ to each gives $DC$ and $XY$, so $XY = DC$.

$$\text{area of a parallelogram} = \text{base} \times \text{height}.$$

Nothing in the argument needed $DC$ in particular. Take $AD$ as the base instead, and drop a line to it from $C$ at a right angle. The same cut and move gives a different rectangle, taller and narrower, with the same area. Either side can be the base, as long as the height is measured at a right angle to that side.
