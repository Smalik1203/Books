Which answers a question the last section raised. Build a rectangle and a parallelogram on the same base, with the other pair of sides the same length in both. The perimeters are equal. The rectangle's height is its side; the parallelogram's height is less, because a slanting side is longer than the perpendicular it leans away from. So of two figures with the same four side lengths, the rectangle is always the larger.

7.7The Rhombus and Its Diagonals

A rhombus is a parallelogram, so its area is base times height and there is nothing more to prove. There is something more to want, though. All four of its sides are the same length, and its height has to be constructed before it can be measured — whereas its two diagonals are already drawn across it and can be measured with a ruler.

Two facts from the first book's chapter on quadrilaterals do the work: the diagonals of a rhombus cut each other exactly in half, and they cross at right angles. Draw the rectangle that just contains the rhombus and the consequence is visible at once.

Each quarter of the rhombus is a right-angled triangle, and the small rectangle it sits in is cut in two by its hypotenuse — so each quarter fills half of its own corner. Four halves make half, and the rectangle measures $AC$ by $BD$.

$$\text{area of a rhombus} = \tfrac12 \times \text{product of the diagonals}.$$

The same answer comes out of the triangle formula, and it is worth doing both ways because the second is the one that survives when the picture is harder to draw. Cut the rhombus along $BD$ into triangles $ABD$ and $CBD$. Both stand on $BD$; the height of the first is $AO$ and of the second $CO$, where $O$ is the crossing point.
