So the square wins with $24$ cm and the long thin $36$ by $1$ loses with $74$ cm. The second half of the question is the interesting half. Drop the whole-number condition and the sides may be $360$ by $0.1$, with a perimeter of $720.2$ cm, or $3600$ by $0.01$, with a perimeter over $7{,}200$ cm. There is no longest, because the rectangle can always be made thinner — and it holds its $36$ cm2 the whole way, which is the strongest form of the warning in section 7.3.

Eight questions, and only two of them were answered by putting numbers into a formula. The rest were answered by choosing what to call the base, by noticing that two things had to be equal, or by replacing the thing asked about with something easier that is always the same size.

That is the habit worth taking out of the division. A formula is the last step, and by the time you reach it the thinking is over.

Example 1 · Single correct

A rectangular garden is 15 m by 6 m. A square garden has the same perimeter. Which garden has the larger area?

Choose one correct option.

  1. the square, by $20.25 \text{ m}^2$
  2. the rectangle, by $20.25 \text{ m}^2$
  3. neither: the areas are equal
  4. the square, by $11.25 \text{ m}^2$

Solution.

Step 1The perimeter is $2(15 + 6) = 42$ m, so the square has side 10.5 m.
Step 2The square has area $110.25 \text{ m}^2$ and the rectangle $90 \text{ m}^2$: $110.25 - 90 = 20.25$.
Answer(a) the square, by $20.25 \text{ m}^2$
Example 2 · Single correct

A floor 4.5 m by 3.6 m is covered with square tiles of side 30 cm. How many tiles are needed?

Choose one correct option.

  1. 162
  2. 180
  3. 18
  4. 1620

Solution.

Step 1Along the length $450 \div 30 = 15$ tiles fit, and across $360 \div 30 = 12$.
Step 2$15 \times 12 = 180$ tiles.
Answer(b) 180
