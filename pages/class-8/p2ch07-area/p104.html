So the square wins with $24$ cm and the long thin $36$ by $1$ loses with $74$ cm. The second half of the question is the interesting half. Drop the whole-number condition and the sides may be $360$ by $0.1$, with a perimeter of $720.2$ cm, or $3600$ by $0.01$, with a perimeter over $7{,}200$ cm. There is no longest, because the rectangle can always be made thinner — and it holds its $36$ cm2 the whole way, which is the strongest form of the warning in section 7.3.

Eight questions, and only two of them were answered by putting numbers into a formula. The rest were answered by choosing what to call the base, by noticing that two things had to be equal, or by replacing the thing asked about with something easier that is always the same size.

That is the habit worth taking out of the division. A formula is the last step, and by the time you reach it the thinking is over.

Solved Examples

Type 1 · Rectangles: area against perimeter

Example 1

A rectangular garden is $15$ m by $6$ m. A square garden has the same perimeter. Which garden has the larger area, and by how much?

Solution.

Step 1 $2(15 + 6) = 42$ m the shared perimeter
Step 2 $42 \div 4 = 10.5$ m side of the square
Step 3 $10.5 \times 10.5 = 110.25$ m2 and $15 \times 6 = 90$ m2 the two areas
Step 4 $110.25 - 90 = 20.25$ m2
Answer The square garden, by 20.25 m2
Example 2

A floor $4.5$ m by $3.6$ m is to be covered with square tiles of side $30$ cm. How many tiles are needed?

Solution.

Step 1 $4.5$ m is $450$ cm and $3.6$ m is $360$ cm one unit throughout
Step 2 $450 \div 30 = 15$ and $360 \div 30 = 12$ tiles along each side
Step 3 $15 \times 12 = 180$ rows times columns
Step 4 $4.5 \times 3.6 = 16.2$ m2, and $16.2 \div 0.09 = 180$ check by area
Answer 180 tiles

The check divides the floor's area by one tile's area, $0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$ m2. Both ways need the lengths in the same unit first.
