7.7The Rhombus and Its Diagonals

A rhombus is a parallelogram, so its area is base times height. But the height of a rhombus has to be drawn before it can be measured. Its two diagonals are easier: they can be measured straight across it with a ruler. Is there a way to find the area from the diagonals?

Two facts about a rhombus help. Its diagonals cut each other in half, and they cross at right angles. Draw the rectangle that just holds the rhombus, and the answer can be seen at once.

Each quarter of the rhombus is a right-angled triangle. It sits in a small rectangle, and its long side cuts that rectangle in half. So each quarter fills half of its own small rectangle. Four halves make half of the large rectangle, which measures $AC$ by $BD$.

$$\text{area of a rhombus} = \tfrac12 \times \text{product of the diagonals}.$$

The triangle formula gives the same answer, and this second way works even when the picture is harder to draw. Cut the rhombus along $BD$ into triangles $ABD$ and $CBD$. Both stand on $BD$. Let $O$ be the point where the diagonals cross. The height of the first triangle is $AO$ and the height of the second is $CO$.

the two triangles $\tfrac12 \times BD \times AO \;+\; \tfrac12 \times BD \times CO$
common factor $\tfrac12 \times BD \times (AO + CO)$
and $AO + CO = AC$, giving $\tfrac12 \times BD \times AC$

Notice that this argument did not use the fact that the diagonals cut each other in half. It used only two things: $AO$ and $CO$ add up to $AC$, and the diagonals meet at a right angle. Without the right angle, $AO$ would not be a height. So the formula holds for every quadrilateral whose diagonals cross at right angles. A kite and a square are two more examples of such shapes.
