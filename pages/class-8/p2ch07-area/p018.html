In this chapter a parallelogram was cut into a rectangle, a triangle into a parallelogram, and a rhombus into two triangles. Each time, the pieces of one shape made another shape of the same area.

Suppose you are given any two polygons with the same area, such as a long thin triangle and a regular hexagon. Can the first always be cut into pieces that make the second? Give your reasons. Then try it with a triangle and a square of equal area.

7.10Areas in Real Life

A floor tile $30$ cm by $30$ cm covers $900$ cm2. A classroom floor needs a few hundred such tiles. When areas get this large, we change to a bigger unit. But changing a unit of area is not the same as changing a unit of length.

A metre is $100$ cm, so a square metre is a square of side $100$ cm. It holds $100$ rows of $100$ unit squares:

$$1 \text{ m}^2 = 100 \times 100 = 10{,}000 \text{ cm}^2.$$

In the same way, furniture and cloth are often measured in inches. An inch is $2.54$ cm. A square inch is a square of side $2.54$ cm, so it holds

$$2.54 \times 2.54 = 6.4516 \text{ cm}^2.$$

This is the main difficulty, and it catches many people. When a length is doubled, an area is multiplied by four. When lengths are multiplied by $2.54$, areas are multiplied by about $6.45$. A factor for lengths must be squared before it is used for areas.

The reason is the unit square itself. When the unit of length changes, the unit square changes in two directions at once. A room twice as long and twice as wide as another has four times the floor. A room only twice as long, with the same width, has twice the floor.
