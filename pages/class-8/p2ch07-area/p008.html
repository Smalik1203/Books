Take the two triangles that share the corner $B$. Their bases are the two halves of the diagonal $AC$. The diagonals of a rectangle cut each other in half, so these bases are equal. Both triangles have their top corner at $B$, so they have the same height from $B$ to $AC$. Equal bases and equal heights give equal areas. Going round the rectangle, each pair of neighbours is equal in the same way. So all four triangles have equal areas, a quarter of the rectangle each. A rectangle $10$ cm by $6$ cm has an area of $60$ cm2, so each of its four triangles has an area of $15$ cm2, even though two of them are tall and narrow and two are short and wide.

Congruent shapes always have equal areas. Shapes with equal areas need not be congruent. The four triangles above have equal areas but not all the same shape.

Any side can be the base

For a triangle, $\tfrac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$ gives the same area whichever side is taken as the base. So when the area is known, the height to any side is twice the area divided by that side.
