7.8Shapes Exchanged for Shapes

The Śulbasūtras are ancient Indian texts on the building of altars — the same texts Chapter 2 took the theorem of the diagonal from. An altar had to be built to a prescribed shape and a prescribed area, and the two requirements do not always arrive together, so the Śulbasūtras are full of problems of one kind: given this shape, construct that one, with the area unchanged.

Section 7.6 has already solved one of them — a parallelogram exchanged for a rectangle. Here is a second, and it is the one that makes the rest easy: turn a triangle into a parallelogram.

The turn about $N$ carries $A$ to $C$, because $N$ is the midpoint of $AC$ and a half-turn about a midpoint swaps the two ends. Nothing is lost and nothing overlaps, so the parallelogram has the triangle's area — and it stands on the same base with half the height, which is $\tfrac12 \times b \times h$ over again.

With those two exchanges in hand, a chain becomes possible. A triangle turns into a parallelogram, and a parallelogram turns into a rectangle, so a triangle turns into a rectangle in two moves. A rhombus splits into two triangles and follows the same road. So does a trapezium, which the first book turned into a parallelogram by doubling it.

Everything ends up a rectangle

A rectangle is where these chains stop, because a rectangle is the one shape whose area can be read straight off a count of unit squares. Every formula in this chapter is an instruction for reaching a rectangle, and the formula's ingredients are the rectangle's two sides in disguise.
