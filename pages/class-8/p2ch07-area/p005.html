Look at the left-hand rectangle on its own. Its diagonal is $AB$, and a diagonal halves a rectangle, so the shaded piece $ABX$ is exactly half of it. The same argument on the right makes $AXC$ half of the right-hand rectangle. Two halves of two rectangles that together make $EBCD$ come to half of $EBCD$, and $EBCD$ has area $b \times h$.

$$\text{area of a triangle} = \tfrac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height}.$$

The height here is the perpendicular height — the distance from the apex square to the base, not the length of a sloping side. Fig. 7.3 is the reason for insisting: $ZDC$ has two long sides and the same area as the upright triangle beside it, because what counts is how far $Z$ stands from the line $DC$, and nothing else.

One case escapes that argument. If the triangle leans far enough over, the perpendicular from the apex misses the base altogether and lands outside it, and no rectangle can be drawn round the triangle with $BC$ as a side. The formula still holds, and the reason is a subtraction rather than a halving.
