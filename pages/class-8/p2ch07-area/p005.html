Two parallels fix the height

Triangles on the same base, with their top corners on one line parallel to that base, all have the same area. The top corner may slide anywhere along the line, even past the end of the base.

Now slide the top corner until it is straight above one end of the base. The triangle becomes half of a rectangle, and section 7.2 has already found that area. So the area of any triangle is half of base times height. Here is a second way to see this, which works for any triangle directly. Draw the rectangle that just holds the triangle, and drop a line from the top corner to the base at a right angle.

Look at the left-hand rectangle $EBXA$. Its diagonal is $AB$, and a diagonal cuts a rectangle in half. So triangle $ABX$ is half of that rectangle. In the same way, triangle $AXC$ is half of the right-hand rectangle. Together the two triangles make half of the whole rectangle $EBCD$, whose area is $b \times h$.

$$\text{area of a triangle} = \tfrac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height}.$$

Remember that the height is measured at a right angle to the base. It is not the length of a sloping side. In Fig. 7.3, $MPQ$ has two long sloping sides, but its area is the same as that of $LPQ$, because $M$ is the same distance from $PQ$.

There is one case this argument misses. If the triangle leans far over, the line from the top corner at a right angle to the base misses the base and lands outside it. Then no rectangle can be drawn round the triangle with $BC$ as one side. The formula still holds, but the reason is a subtraction.
