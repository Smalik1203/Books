Beyond the Book

The chapter had one method and several formulas. The method was to cut a shape up and lay the pieces down somewhere better, and the formulas were what that came to when it was written down. The questions below mostly ask for the method, because the formula alone will not reach them.

Two of them are not about area at all until you notice that they are. Try each before reading what follows it.

A triangle and a parallelogram stand on the same base, with their far sides on one line parallel to it. What is the ratio of their areas, and why does it not depend on how far the triangle leans?

Both figures stand on the base $b$, and both have their far sides on a line parallel to it, so both have the same perpendicular height $h$ — that is exactly what parallel lines guarantee.

the triangle $\tfrac12 \, b \, h$
the parallelogram $b \, h$

So the ratio is $1 : 2$, whatever $b$ and $h$ happen to be, and leaning the triangle over changes neither. It is the same reason both figures were indifferent to leaning in the chapter: a lean moves the apex along the parallel and the parallel is where the height is fixed.

A rhombus has area $240$ cm2 and one diagonal of $30$ cm. Find the other diagonal, the side, and the perpendicular height.

Each part uses a different fact, and they have to be taken in order because each answer feeds the next.

the other diagonal $\tfrac12 \times 30 \times d = 240$, so $d = 16$ cm
the side $\sqrt{15^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{289} = 17$ cm
the height $17 \times h = 240$, so $h = \tfrac{240}{17} \approx 14.1$ cm

The middle line is Chapter 2's theorem, used on the quarter of the rhombus cut off by the two half-diagonals — $15$ and $8$, because the diagonals bisect each other. The last line is the rhombus read as a parallelogram instead. One shape, three formulas, and the area passed between them.
