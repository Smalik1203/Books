What makes it work here is that a triangle has three base-and-height pairs and only one area. Three ways of writing one quantity give two equations for free, and a question that names five of the six numbers involved has already told you the sixth.

Example 1

A triangle has area $84$ cm2. One of its sides is $24$ cm and another is $21$ cm. Find the perpendicular height to each of them.

Solution.

Step 1 $\tfrac12 \times 24 \times h = 84$, so $h = 7$ cm on the 24 cm side
Step 2 $\tfrac12 \times 21 \times h = 84$, so $h = 8$ cm on the 21 cm side
Answer 7 cm to the 24 cm side, and 8 cm to the 21 cm side

The longer side takes the shorter height, and it has to: their product is fixed at twice the area, so one rises exactly as fast as the other falls. A triangle's longest side always carries its smallest height.

Equal heights are worth looking out for, because they turn a question about areas into a question about lengths. Draw both diagonals of a rectangle and they cut it into four triangles, which are plainly not all the same shape. Are they the same size?

Take two that sit side by side, sharing the vertex $B$. Their bases are the two halves of the diagonal $AC$, and the diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other, so those bases are equal. Both triangles have their apex at $B$, so both have the same perpendicular height from $B$ down to $AC$. Equal bases and equal heights make equal areas. Going round the figure, every adjacent pair matches, so all four are equal — a quarter of the rectangle each, without a single measurement.

Congruent shapes have equal areas, and shapes of equal area need not be congruent at all. Almost every argument in this chapter runs the second way round: the four triangles above match in area and not in shape, and the rearranged pieces of the next section match in area precisely because they are the same pieces in different places.
