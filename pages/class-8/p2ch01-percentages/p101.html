Using What You Know

Three things came out of this chapter. A percentage is a fraction of a stated whole. A change of $r\%$ is a multiplication by $1 \pm \tfrac{r}{100}$, and multiplications can be strung together. And the whole a percentage is taken of can move between one sentence and the next, which is where the interest lies.

Every question below turns on the third. None of them says which amount is the base — that is the part you have to decide — and in several of them the obvious answer is the one that forgets the base has moved. Try each before reading what follows it.

Each question is answered straight after it. Read each answer even where your own agreed with it, because what is worth taking away is not the number at the end but the sentence near the start that decides which amount the percentage is of. Once that sentence is written down, most of these questions come to a single multiplication.

Where a question names no starting amount, one has been chosen here, and it is usually a hundred — a percentage of a hundred is the percentage itself, which saves a line of arithmetic. The choice never changes the answer: what these questions ask for is a multiplier, and a multiplier is the same whatever it multiplies.

A shopkeeper marks his goods up by $60\%$ and then advertises $25\%$ off. Is he making a profit or a loss on a sale, and of what percentage?

Sixty per cent on and twenty-five per cent off look as though they should settle at thirty-five per cent on. They do not, because the sixty is taken of the cost price and the twenty-five of the marked price. Take the cost to be $100$ and follow it.

marked $100 \times 1.60 = 160$
sold $160 \times 0.75 = 120$
profit $20$ on a cost of $100$, so $20\%$

A profit of $20\%$, and the two multipliers gave it in one line: $1.6 \times 0.75 = 1.2$. Choosing $100$ for the cost costs nothing — any other starting figure would leave the same multiplier behind.
