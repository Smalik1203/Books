What this chapter established
  1. A percentage is a fraction with $100$ underneath it: $\ p\% = \tfrac{p}{100}$. Multiply a fraction or a decimal by $100$ to reach a percentage, divide by $100$ to come back.
  2. $p\%$ of a quantity $q$ is $\tfrac{p}{100} \times q$. Read that line in whichever direction the question needs — for the part, for the percentage, or for the whole.
  3. A percentage on its own says nothing about an amount. $25\%$ and $35\%$ cannot be compared until you know what each is of, and $140\%$ of something is a perfectly ordinary quantity, larger than what it came from.
  4. Unlike wholes are compared by putting both out of a hundred. That is what percentages are for, and it is why $\tfrac{42}{50}$ and $\tfrac{70}{80}$ can be set beside each other once they are $84\%$ and $87.5\%$.
  5. A ratio becomes a percentage through the total number of parts, not through the other side of the ratio: $2 : 7$ makes millet $\tfrac29$ of the mixture, not $\tfrac27$.
  6. An increase or a decrease is measured against the original amount — the amount the change started from.
  7. Every change is a multiplier. Up by $r\%$ is $\times (1 + \tfrac{r}{100})$, down by $r\%$ is $\times (1 - \tfrac{r}{100})$. Multipliers can be strung together, which is what makes several changes in a row easy.
  8. Profit and loss are percentages of the cost price; a discount is a percentage of the marked price. The base is different because the question is different.
  9. Interest left in the account earns interest itself. Over $t$ terms at rate $r$, a principal $p$ grows to $p(1+rt)$ without compounding and to $p(1+r)^t$ with it. Depreciation is the same formula with a multiplier below $1$.
  10. A rise and a fall of the same percentage do not cancel, because the second is taken of an amount the first has already changed. A markup of $50\%$ is undone by a discount of $33.3\%$, and if you take $50\%$ off instead you have lost a quarter of what you paid.
