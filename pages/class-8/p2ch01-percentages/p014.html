If the interest is paid out every year, the principal stays ₹$8{,}000$ and earns ₹$800$ each year. Interest worked out on the principal alone is called simple interest.

three years $8{,}000 + 3 \times 800 = $ ₹$10{,}400$

If the interest is left in the account, it becomes part of the principal. Next year's $10\%$ is then taken of a larger amount. Each year is a multiplication by $1.1$. Interest worked out this way is called compound interest.

after year 1 $8{,}000 \times 1.1 = $ ₹$8{,}800$
after year 2 $8{,}800 \times 1.1 = $ ₹$9{,}680$
after year 3 $9{,}680 \times 1.1 = $ ₹$10{,}648$

The same rate, the same principal and the same three years give ₹$248$ more. This is called compounding. It happens whenever a percentage is taken of an amount that the percentage has already changed.

With letters, both are short. Let $p$ be the principal, $r$ the rate as a decimal (so $10\%$ is ${r = 0.1}$), and $t$ the number of years. The amount at the end is

$$\text{simple interest} \quad p(1 + rt) \qquad\qquad \text{compound interest} \quad p(1 + r)^{\,t}$$

The first adds the same interest $t$ times. The second multiplies by $(1+r)$, $t$ times. The power $t$ does the same work as the repeated multiplication in the year-by-year working. You met powers in Part I, Chapter 2.

Compounding is not only about money, and not only about growth. A quantity that falls by the same percentage again and again is also compounding, with a multiplier smaller than one. A car, a phone or a machine loses a part of its value every year in this way, and each year it loses a part of a smaller value.
