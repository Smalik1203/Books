Example 5

Shambhavi buys notebooks at ₹$36$ each and sells them at a profit of $20\%$. She sells crayon boxes at ₹$50$ each, also at a profit of $25\%$. Find the selling price of a notebook and the cost price of a crayon box.

notebook selling price $= 36 \times 1.20 = $ ₹$43.20$
crayons $1.25 \times \text{cost} = 50$, so cost $= \dfrac{50}{1.25} = $ ₹$40$

The second is the one to be careful with. A $25\%$ profit does not mean taking $25\%$ off the selling price — that would give ₹$37.50$, and a check settles it: a profit of ₹$12.50$ on a cost of ₹$37.50$ would be a third, not a quarter.

Example 6

Raghu bought rice at ₹$35$ a kilogram. To clear old stock he sells $10$ kg for ₹$300$. What is his percentage loss?

cost $10 \times 35 = $ ₹$350$
loss $350 - 300 = $ ₹$50$
loss per cent $\dfrac{50}{350} \times 100 = 14.3\%$

Tax is the third thing that moves a price, and it moves it the other way. The Goods and Services Tax is quoted as a percentage and added to what you would otherwise have paid, so it appears on the bill as a separate line and the total is what leaves your pocket.

two shirts at ₹$600$ ₹$1{,}200.00$
GST at $5\%$ ₹$60.00$
to pay ₹$1{,}260.00$

Which is $1200 \times 1.05$, the multiplier again. Bills are worth reading for exactly this reason: the tax has a rate and a base printed beside it, and the two should agree with the total. Look at a bill from home and check that they do.

Note the order in which the three things happen to a price, because it decides what each percentage is taken of. The shop starts from what it paid and marks the price up. A discount comes off that marked price. The tax is added last, to the price after the discount — which is why a discount saves you slightly more than the figure on the sign, and why a shop that added the tax first and discounted afterwards would be charging you differently.
