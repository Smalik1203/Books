He gets back $84\%$ of what he paid, which is a loss of $16\%$. If the shoes brought him ₹$21{,}000$, then $0.84x = 21{,}000$, so $x = $ ₹$25{,}000$ and he is ₹$4{,}000$ down.

What discount would have left him exactly even? Ask for the discount $d$ that takes $1.4x$ back to $x$:

the condition $1.4x - d \times 1.4x = x$
so $d = \dfrac{0.4x}{1.4x} = \dfrac27 \approx 28.6\%$

A markup of $40\%$ is undone by a discount of about $28.6\%$, not by a discount of $40\%$. The two percentages describe the same gap in rupees. They differ because they are measured from different prices: the markup from what he paid, and the discount from the marked price. Check it on the shoes: a pair that cost ₹$1{,}400$ is marked at ₹$1{,}960$, and $\tfrac27$ of $1{,}960$ is $560$, which brings it back to $1{,}400$.

The same thing happens in a comparison. Salma says: my savings are $125\%$ of Vikram's. What can Vikram say about his savings?

He cannot say he has $75\%$ of hers. Suppose Vikram has ₹$100$, so Salma has ₹$125$. Then Vikram's share of Salma's is $\tfrac{100}{125} = 80\%$. Salma has $25\%$ more than Vikram, and Vikram has $20\%$ less than Salma. Both sentences are true, and the numbers in them differ. More than Vikram counts out of Vikram's savings, and less than Salma counts out of Salma's. So always ask which amount a percentage is counted out of before you use it.

Example 8

The number of students in a school rose by $20\%$ one year and fell by $20\%$ the next year. There are now $960$ students. How many were there two years ago?

Solution. Write $n$ for the number two years ago.

Step 1 $1.2 \times 0.8 = 0.96$ the two changes together
Step 2 $0.96 \times n = 960$, so $n = \dfrac{960}{0.96} = 1000$ divide by the multiplier
Check $1000 \times 1.2 = 1200$, and $1200 \times 0.8 = 960$
Answer $1000$ students

The two changes together are a fall of $4\%$. Adding $20\%$ back to $960$ would give $1152$, which is wrong.
