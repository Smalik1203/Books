1.6Bought, Marked, Sold

A shopkeeper's sweater has three prices attached to it, and telling them apart is most of the work in any question about a shop.

cost price what the seller paid for it
marked price what the tag says — the price before any bargaining
selling price what the customer actually pays

Kishanlal buys sweaters from a wholesaler at ₹$300$ each, marks them at ₹$480$, and after some bargaining sells one for ₹$430$. Two things happened at once, and they are measured against two different prices.

The profit is $430 - 300 = $ ₹$130$, and the discount is $480 - 430 = $ ₹$50$. Had the selling price come out below the cost price, the difference would have been a loss instead.

Both amounts can be turned into percentages, and each has its own base. There is a reason for that, and it is not arbitrary.

Two percentages, two bases

Profit and loss are percentages of the cost price, because what the seller wants to know is how well the money they put in came back. A discount is a percentage of the marked price, because what the buyer is being offered is a cut in the price they were shown. Take either percentage of the wrong price and the answer is simply about something else.

profit per cent $\dfrac{130}{300} \times 100 = 43.3\%$
discount per cent $\dfrac{50}{480} \times 100 = 10.4\%$
