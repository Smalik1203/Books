The same rate, the same principal, the same three years, and ₹$186$ more. That difference is called compounding, and it is what happens whenever a percentage is taken of an amount that the percentage itself has already changed.

Written with letters, the two are short. Let $p$ be the principal, $r$ the rate as a decimal — $10\%$ is $r = 0.1$ — and $t$ the number of years.

$$\text{without compounding} \quad p(1 + rt) \qquad\qquad \text{with compounding} \quad p(1 + r)^{\,t}$$

The first adds the same interest $t$ times. The second multiplies by $(1+r)$ $t$ times, and the exponent is doing exactly what the repeated multiplication did in the table opposite.

Compounding is not only about money, and it is not only about growth. A quantity that falls by the same percentage over and over is compounding downwards, and the arithmetic is the multiplier again — this time smaller than one.

Example 7

A television bought for ₹$21{,}000$ loses $5\%$ of its value every year. What is it worth after one year, and after three?

after 1 year $21{,}000 \times 0.95 = $ ₹$19{,}950$
after 3 years $21{,}000 \times 0.95^3 = $ ₹$18{,}015$ (to the nearest rupee)

This loss of value with age and use is called depreciation. Note that three years did not take away $15\%$: each year's $5\%$ was of a smaller television than the year before.

So one formula covers both directions, and the sign of the change is carried by the multiplier rather than by a separate rule. Growth at $r$ and decline at $r$ are $(1+r)^t$ and $(1-r)^t$, and a quantity falling by a fixed percentage never reaches zero — it only ever loses a share of what is left. That is why an old television is still worth something, and why the population of a village that keeps losing a tenth of itself takes a very long time to empty.
