$$\frac{60}{160} \times 100 = 37.5\%,$$

so the audience has dropped by $37.5\%$. Notice what went underneath in each case: the amount the quantity started from. Not the amount it ended at, and not the larger of the two. The cinema's fall of $60$ is $60\%$ of $100$ and $37.5\%$ of $160$, and only the second is the answer to the question that was asked.

The base is where you started

An increase or a decrease is always measured against the original amount. Every argument in the rest of this chapter is about which amount that is, and the arithmetic is never the hard part.

There is a second way to say the same thing, and it is the more useful one. If a quantity rises by $40\%$, the new amount is the old one plus $40\%$ of it — which is $140\%$ of the old one.

Example 3

Do these two statements say the same thing?

the first the town's population in 1991 was $165\%$ of its population in 1961
the second the town's population rose by $65\%$ between 1961 and 1991

Write $p$ for the 1961 population and $q$ for the 1991 one.

the first says $q = \dfrac{165}{100} \times p = 1.65\,p$
the second says $q = p + \dfrac{65}{100} \times p = 1.65\,p$

The same line twice, so yes. One statement reports where the population ended and the other reports how far it moved, and they are two readings of a single multiplication.

Reading a change as a multiplication is the habit worth forming here, and it is worth forming for two reasons. It replaces two steps — work out the increase, then add it on — with one. And unlike the two steps, it survives being repeated: a quantity that rises by $10\%$ twice is multiplied by $1.1$ twice, which comes to $1.21$ and not to $1.2$. Everything in the last three sections of this chapter is that observation, applied to a price, to a bank balance and to a town.
