Up and down by the same percentage

The two changes are the same size and not the same amount, because the second is taken of a number the first has already enlarged.

together $1.2 \times 0.8 = 0.96$
so a single decrease of $4\%$

The order makes no difference — $0.8 \times 1.2$ is the same product — so a cut before a rise leaves you exactly as short. And the answer never depended on the number itself, which is why none was given.

A percentage of a percentage

The two groups are different sizes, so the two rates cannot simply be averaged. Take a class of $100$: $60$ girls and $40$ boys.

girls $20\%$ of $60 = 12$
boys $30\%$ of $40 = 12$
class $24$ out of $100$, so $24\%$

Halfway between $20\%$ and $30\%$ is $25\%$, and the answer is below it — the smaller group is the one with the higher rate, so its rate counts for less.

The price the tax was taken of

The tax was $12\%$ of the price before tax, not of the bill. So ₹$560$ is $112\%$ of what we want.

the equation $1.12 \times \text{price} = 560$
so price $= \dfrac{560}{1.12} = $ ₹$500$

Taking $12\%$ off ₹$560$ instead gives ₹$492.80$, which is the answer to a different question: what $12\%$ of the bill would have been. The two differ by ₹$7.20$ here and would differ by more on a larger bill, so the habit of asking which price the rate was applied to is worth more than the seven rupees suggests. Undoing a percentage always means dividing by its multiplier, never subtracting the percentage from the result.
