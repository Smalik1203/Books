A trader sells two shawls for ₹$990$ each. On one he makes a profit of $10\%$; on the other he takes a loss of $10\%$. Taking the two together, has he gained or lost, and how much?

The two shawls sold for the same money, so it is tempting to say the gain and the loss cancel. They do not, because the two percentages were taken of two different cost prices, and the one he lost on was the dearer.

the profitable one $1.1 \times c = 990$, so $c = $ ₹$900$
the other $0.9 \times c = 990$, so $c = $ ₹$1{,}100$
altogether cost ₹$2{,}000$, sold ₹$1{,}980$

A loss of ₹$20$, which is $1\%$. Whenever two items go at the same price with equal profit and loss percentages, the result is a loss — and the same arithmetic will tell you it is always the square of the rate: $10\%$ each way loses $1\%$.

The price of rice rises by $60\%$. By what percentage must a family cut the amount it buys if it is to spend exactly what it spent before?

What has to stay fixed is the product of the two, so if one is multiplied by $1.6$ the other must be multiplied by whatever undoes it.

the condition $1.6 \times m = 1$

Not $60\%$. A rise of three fifths in the price is undone by a cut of three eighths in the amount, for the same reason a markup of $50\%$ needed a discount of a third.

₹$10{,}000$ is deposited at $10\%$ a year with the interest left in. After how many whole years does the deposit first pass ₹$15{,}000$?

Each year multiplies the deposit by $1.1$, so the question is how many tens per cent it takes to get from $10{,}000$ past $15{,}000$ — that is, when $1.1^{\,t}$ first passes $1.5$.
