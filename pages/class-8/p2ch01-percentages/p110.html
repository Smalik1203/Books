Set B, 4. Option (a) takes $12\%$ off ₹$22{,}400$. But the $12\%$ was of the old salary, so the new one is $112\%$ of it and the old one is $22{,}400 \div 1.12$. Taking the percentage off the wrong number is the single commonest slip in this chapter.

Set B, 11. Option (a) is the answer that feels fair: the same price, equal and opposite percentages. But the vase sold at a loss must have cost more to begin with — ₹$1{,}500$ against ₹$1{,}000$ — so the loss is taken of a larger sum than the profit. Two equal prices with equal profit and loss rates always end in a loss.

Set C, 2. Option (a) says the two statements are two ways of saying one thing. They are not: if Q is $100$ then P is $125$, and Q falls short of P by $25$ out of $125$, which is $20\%$. More than Q counts out of Q; less than P counts out of P.

Set C, 6. Options (a) and (b) look different and are the same number, because $\tfrac{30}{100} \times 80$ and $\tfrac{80}{100} \times 30$ are the same product written in a different order. Since $24\%$ of $100$ is $24$ as well, all three agree, and (d) is the answer.

Set C, 10. Option (a) adds $25$ and $20$. The discount is taken of the marked price and the profit is measured on the cost, so what is needed is the markup $m$ with $0.8\,m = 1.25$ — that is, $m = 1.5625$. Adding the two percentages ignores that they have different bases, which is what the whole of Section 1.8 was about.

What to carry forward

Ask what the percentage is of, before anything else. Nearly every wrong answer above is the right arithmetic applied to the wrong whole. Name the base out loud — the cost price, the marked price, last year's population, the solution before boiling — and the working after it is usually one line.

Work in multipliers. A rise of $r\%$ is $\times (1 + \tfrac{r}{100})$ and a fall is $\times (1 - \tfrac{r}{100})$. Changes one after another multiply, so several steps collapse into one number, and that number tells you at a glance whether the end is above or below the start.

A change and its opposite are not the same size. Undoing a rise of $r\%$ needs a fall of less than $r\%$, and undoing a fall needs a rise of more. If you remember nothing else from this division, remember that up and down by the same percentage always leaves you lower than you began.
