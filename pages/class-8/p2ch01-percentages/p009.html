Measure from where you started

An increase or a decrease is always measured against the original amount. The rest of this chapter is mostly about which amount that is. The working itself is never the hard part.

There is a second way to say the same thing, and it is the more useful one. If a quantity rises by $30\%$, the new amount is the old amount plus $30\%$ of it. That is $130\%$ of the old amount. In the same way, a fall of $20\%$ leaves $80\%$ of the old amount.

Example 3

Do these two sentences say the same thing?

the first a tree's height now is $175\%$ of its height five years ago
the second the tree has grown taller by $75\%$ in five years

Solution. Write $h$ for the height five years ago and $k$ for the height now.

Step 1 $k = \dfrac{175}{100} \times h = 1.75\,h$ the first says
Step 2 $k = h + \dfrac{75}{100} \times h = 1.75\,h$ the second says
Answer Yes. Both say $k = 1.75\,h$

One sentence says where the height ended. The other says how far it grew. Both come from the same multiplication.

Think of a change as a multiplication. This habit is worth forming, for two reasons. First, it replaces two steps (find the increase, then add it on) with one step. Second, it still works when a change is repeated. A quantity that rises by $10\%$ twice is multiplied by $1.1$ twice. That makes $1.21$, not $1.2$. The last three sections of this chapter use this idea for prices, for money in a bank and for values that fall year after year.

A change is a multiplier
  • A rise of $r\%$ is multiplying by $\left(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\right)$. A rise of $8\%$ is $\times 1.08$.
  • A fall of $r\%$ is multiplying by $\left(1 - \dfrac{r}{100}\right)$. A fall of $12.5\%$ is $\times 0.875$.
