A ratio can also be used to copy a shape at a different size.

Example 5

Measure the width and height of the blackboard in your classroom, to the nearest centimetre. What is the ratio of its width to its height? Draw a rectangle in your notebook whose width and height are in the same ratio.

Every classroom gives its own answer. Here the blackboard is taken to be $360$ cm wide and $120$ cm high.

Solution.

Step 1 width to height is $360 : 120$
Step 2 $360 : 120$ is $3 : 1$ divide by the HCF, $120$
Step 3 $3 \times 3 : 1 \times 3$ is $9 : 3$ any factor will do
Answer A rectangle $9$ cm wide and $3$ cm high, or any rectangle three times as wide as it is high

Compare your rectangle with those your classmates have drawn. Do they all look the same? They may be of different sizes. If each has the same ratio of width to height as the blackboard, they all have the same shape.

The next example asks what happens to a ratio when the same number is added to both of its terms.

Example 6

When Neelima was $3$ years old, her mother's age was $10$ times her age. What was the ratio of Neelima's age to her mother's age? What will the ratio of their ages be when Neelima is $12$ years old? Will it stay the same?

Solution.

Step 1 mother's age $= 3 \times 10 = 30$ years
Step 2 $3 : 30$ is $1 : 10$ divide by $3$
Step 3 $9$ years later: $12 : 39$ each age grows by $9$
Step 4 $12 : 39$ is $4 : 13$ divide by $3$
Answer $1 : 10$ at first, and $4 : 13$ when Neelima is $12$. The ratio does not stay the same

When the same number is added to both terms of a ratio, or taken away from both, the ratio changes. The new ratio is usually not proportional to the old one. Multiplying or dividing both terms by the same number is what keeps a ratio proportional.

A missing term can be found by working out the factor of change, as in Example 2. The factor need not be a whole number.
