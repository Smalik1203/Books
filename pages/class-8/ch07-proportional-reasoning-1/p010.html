$d = \dfrac{bc}{a}$

Cross multiplication
  • If $a : b :: c : d$, then $a \times d = b \times c$: the product of the extremes equals the product of the means.
  • An unknown fourth term is found from $d = \dfrac{bc}{a}$.

Indian mathematicians worked with such problems long ago. Āryabhaṭa, who wrote in 499 CE, and many after him called them rule of three problems, trairāśika in Sanskrit, which means three quantities. The three known numbers have names. The pramāṇa is the measure, our $a$. The phala is the fruit, our $b$. The ichchhā is the wish, our $c$. The number to be found is the ichchhāphala, the fruit of the wish, our $d$. The rule says: multiply the phala by the ichchhā, and divide the product by the pramāṇa. That is exactly $d = \dfrac{bc}{a}$.

Example 9

A goods train covers $84$ km in $105$ minutes. If it keeps the same speed, how far will it travel in $2\frac{1}{2}$ hours?

At the same speed, the ratio of time to distance stays proportional. But $105$ is in minutes and $2\frac{1}{2}$ is in hours. Both ratios must use the same unit of time.

Solution.

Step 1 $2\frac{1}{2}$ hours $= 150$ minutes one unit of time
Step 2 $105 : 84 :: 150 : x$ minutes to kilometres
Step 3 $105 \times x = 84 \times 150$ cross multiply
Step 4 $x = \dfrac{84 \times 150}{105} = 120$
Answer $120$ km in $2\frac{1}{2}$ hours

There are other ways to reach $120$. The train covers $84 \div 7 = 12$ km in each $15$ minutes, and $150$ minutes holds ten lots of $15$ minutes: $10 \times 12 = 120$. Cross multiplication checks it: $105 \times 120 = 12600$ and $84 \times 150 = 12600$.
