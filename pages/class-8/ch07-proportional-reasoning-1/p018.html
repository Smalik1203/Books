Summary
  1. When two measurements are multiplied by the same factor, they change proportionally. A picture keeps its shape only when its width and height change proportionally. Adding the same amount to both does not keep the shape.
  2. A ratio $a : b$ says that for every $a$ units of the first quantity there are $b$ units of the second. The numbers $a$ and $b$ are its terms, and their order matters.
  3. A ratio is in its simplest form when the HCF of its terms is $1$. To reduce a ratio, divide both terms by their HCF: $80 : 60$ becomes $4 : 3$.
  4. Two ratios are in proportion when their simplest forms are the same. We write $a : b :: c : d$. Ratios with the same simplest form are equivalent ratios.
  5. If $a : b :: c : d$, then $ad = bc$. This is cross multiplication: the product of the extremes equals the product of the means. A missing fourth term is $d = \frac{bc}{a}$.
  6. Finding a fourth term from three known ones is the rule of three, trairāśika, which Āryabhaṭa described in 499 CE. Finding the value of one unit first is the unitary method.
  7. Not every pair of quantities is proportional. When the speed of a journey goes up, the time goes down, so the rule of three does not apply.
  8. To divide $x$ in the ratio $m : n$, cut it into $m + n$ equal groups. The parts are $m \times \frac{x}{m + n}$ and $n \times \frac{x}{m + n}$. For example, $63$ in the ratio $5 : 4$ gives $35$ and $28$.
  9. Both terms of a ratio must be in the same unit before two ratios are compared. Temperatures change by $F = \frac{9}{5} \times C + 32$, which is not a proportion.
