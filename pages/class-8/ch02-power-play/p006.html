2.3.1 A power of a power

There is more than one way to split a row of factors, and looking at those splits closely gives the second law. Here is $4^6$, cut into two rows of three and then into three rows of two:

two rows of three $(4 \times 4 \times 4) \times (4 \times 4 \times 4) = 4^3 \times 4^3 = (4^3)^2$
three rows of two $(4 \times 4) \times (4 \times 4) \times (4 \times 4) = (4^2)^3$

Both are $4096$, because both are six fours however they are bracketed. And the count of fours is what the two exponents multiply to:

$(4^3)^2 = 4^{3 \times 2} = 4^6 \qquad (4^2)^3 = 4^{2 \times 3} = 4^6$

A power raised to a power

$(n^a)^b = n^{ab}$, and since $ab = ba$ this also equals $(n^b)^a$. Raising to a power multiplies the exponents; multiplying powers adds them.

2.3.2 The same exponent, different bases

Damayanti keeps two ponds. In the first the lotuses double every day; in the second they triple. She puts one lotus in the doubling pond, leaves it four days, moves everything to the tripling pond, and leaves it four days more.

after four days $1 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^4 = 16$
after four more $2^4 \times 3^4 = 16 \times 81$ $1296$

If she had used the tripling pond first, the answer would be $3^4 \times 2^4$, which is the same number — but there is more to see than that. Write the eight factors out and pair them off one from each group:

$2^4 \times 3^4$ $= (2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2) \times (3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3)$
$= (2 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3)$
$= 6^4$ $1296$

The regrouping is allowed because multiplication may be done in any order, and it works only because the two exponents are equal — there were exactly as many twos as threes, so every two found a partner.

Powers with a shared exponent

$m^a \times n^a = (mn)^a$. Read the other way it is often more useful: $(mn)^a = m^a n^a$, so a power of a product is the product of the powers.
