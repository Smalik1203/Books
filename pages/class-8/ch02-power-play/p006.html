Nothing about $4$ and $3$ was special. Seven threes can be split anywhere at all — $3^2 \times 3^5$, or $3^6 \times 3^1$ — because all that is happening is that a row of seven threes is being divided into two shorter rows.

Multiplying powers of the same base

$n^a \times n^b = n^{a+b}$. A row of $a$ copies of $n$ set beside a row of $b$ copies is a row of $a + b$ copies, and nothing else has happened.

Two warnings about that law, both of which cost marks every year. It is about multiplying, so $n^a + n^b$ is not $n^{a+b}$ and is not usually anything tidy at all. And the bases must match: $2^3 \times 5^4$ cannot be gathered into a single power, because there is no single number being repeated.

Example 2

Simplify $p^4 \times p^6$, and then $2^3 \times 2^5 \times 2$.

$p^4 \times p^6$ $= (p\,p\,p\,p) \times (p\,p\,p\,p\,p\,p) = p^{10}$

For the second, remember that a base written alone carries an invisible exponent of $1$: $2$ means $2^1$.

$2^3 \times 2^5 \times 2^1$ $= 2^{3+5+1} = 2^9$ $512$

2.3.1 A power of a power

There is more than one way to split a row of factors, and taking the splits seriously gives the second law. Here is $4^6$, cut into two rows of three and then into three rows of two:

two rows of three $(4 \times 4 \times 4) \times (4 \times 4 \times 4) = 4^3 \times 4^3 = (4^3)^2$
three rows of two $(4 \times 4) \times (4 \times 4) \times (4 \times 4) = (4^2)^3$

Both are $4096$, because both are six fours however they are bracketed. And the count of fours is what the two exponents multiply to:

$(4^3)^2 = 4^{3 \times 2} = 4^6 \qquad (4^2)^3 = 4^{2 \times 3} = 4^6$
