Three digits? Every one of those hundred can be followed by any of ten, so $100 \times 10 = 1000$. The pattern is now visible, and it is a power.

$2$ digits $10 \times 10 = 10^2 = 100$
$3$ digits $10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10^3 = 1000$
$5$ digits $10^5$ $1\,00\,000$
Independent choices multiply

If a thing is built by making $a$ choices in turn, and each choice has $n$ options whatever the earlier ones were, then there are $n^a$ possibilities. Each new slot multiplies the count rather than adding to it — which is why the total grows the way § 2.1 described.

The two words to watch are independent and whatever the earlier ones were. If the options change as you go, the count is a product but not a power. Roxie has $7$ dresses, $2$ hats and $3$ pairs of shoes, so she has $7 \times 2 \times 3 = 42$ ways to dress — a product of three different numbers, because the three choices offer different amounts.

Example 7

A lock has six slots and each slot takes any capital letter, A to Z. How many codes are there? How does that compare with a five-digit numeric lock?

letters $26^6 = 308\,915\,776$ $\approx 3.1 \times 10^8$
digits $10^5 = 1\,00\,000$ $= 1 \times 10^5$

The exponents are $8$ and $5$, so the letter lock has roughly three thousand times as many codes.

It is worth asking which of the two changes did more work, and the answer says something about powers generally. Adding a seventh slot would multiply the count by $26$. Adding a twenty-seventh letter to the alphabet would multiply it by only $(27/26)^6$, which is about $1.25$. The exponent is by far the stronger lever, and that is true of every power: a small change to what is being repeated is nothing beside a change to how often.

The same arithmetic explains why a password of eight characters is so much safer than one of six, and why adding a couple of characters helps far more than choosing cleverer ones. It is the folded sheet again, in a different costume: the count is multiplied at every step, and steps are the thing to spend on.
