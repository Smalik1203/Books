B6 A negative exponent turns a fraction upside down and then applies the power: $\left(\tfrac25\right)^{-3} = \left(\tfrac52\right)^{3}$. (a) has forgotten to invert; (c) has made the answer negative, which a negative exponent never does.
B7 (a) and (d) both come to $130$, which is what you get by adding the choices rather than multiplying them. (b) has the base and exponent the wrong way round: there are $26$ options in each of $5$ slots, not $5$ options in each of $26$.
C1 $40$ and $30$ share the factor $10$, so write both with exponent $10$: $2^{40} = 16^{10}$ and $3^{30} = 27^{10}$. Then $27 \gt 16$ settles it. (d) is the answer a calculator gives, not mathematics.
C2 The last digits of the powers of $3$ run $3, 9, 7, 1$ and repeat. $2024$ is a multiple of $4$, so it lands on the last of the four. (b) is the answer for an exponent one past a multiple of four.
C4 Exponent $0$ means the number is the coefficient itself, and standard form requires that to be at least $1$ and under $10$. (a) is true only when the coefficient happens to be $1$.
C6 $8^4 = 2^{12}$, $16^3 = 2^{12}$ and $32^2 = 2^{10}$, but $6 = 2 \times 3$ brings a factor of $3$ that no power of $2$ can contain.
C9 Divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents: $(1.67 \div 9.11) \times 10^{4}$, which is about $0.18 \times 10^4 = 1800$. Reading the exponents alone would have given $10^4$; the coefficients pull it down by a factor of five, and this is one of the cases where they matter.

What to carry forward

Three habits outlast the questions that carried them. When two powers cannot be compared, force them to share a base or an exponent — that single move settled four of the eight problems above. When a number is too large to compute, trade it for a power of ten using $2^{10} \approx 10^3$, and be content with an answer that is right to one figure. And when a rule seems to run out, as it did at $n^0$, ask what value would let the existing laws keep working; that is how the boundaries of mathematics get extended, and it is nearly always more productive than treating the gap as a special case to memorise.

There is a fourth habit that is harder to name and worth more than the three. Every question in this section began by looking impossible — a number with two hundred digits, a power no calculator will hold, a cycle running to the thousandth term. In every case the impossibility was in the arithmetic and not in the question, and the way through was to stop trying to compute and start asking what the answer must look like. The last digit of $7^{100}$ was never going to be found by multiplying; it was found by noticing that last digits repeat.
