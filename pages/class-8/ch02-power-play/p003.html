$n \times n$ $= n^2$— $n$ squared
$n \times n \times n$ $= n^3$— $n$ cubed
$n \times n \times n \times n$ $= n^4$— $n$ to the power $4$

and in general $n^a$ means $n$ multiplied by itself $a$ times. In $5^4 = 625$, the $5$ is the base and the $4$ is the exponent, also called the power or the index. The whole thing is read five to the power four, and $625$ written as $5^4$ is in exponential form.

Do not confuse $3 \times 4$ with $3^4$. The first is $4$ added to itself three times and comes to $12$; the second is $3$ multiplied by itself four times and comes to $81$. Similarly $4 + 4 + 4 = 12$ while $4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64$.

The notation extends to letters and to several bases at once. Whatever appears repeatedly gets counted:

$a \times a \times a \times b \times b$ $= a^3 b^2$
$2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 5$ $= 2^4 \times 5^2$

That second line is the shape a prime factorisation naturally takes, and from here on it is how we shall write one. It is shorter, and it puts the exponents where you can read them — which, after Chapter 1, is where the answers live.

Example 1

Write $32\,400$ as a product of powers of primes.

Divide by primes repeatedly until nothing is left but $1$.

$32400$ $= 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$
in exponential form $= 2^4 \times 3^4 \times 5^2$

Ten primes on the first line and three terms on the second, saying exactly the same thing. And a question Chapter 1 would have asked — is $32\,400$ a perfect square? — is now answered by looking: $4$, $4$ and $2$ are all even, so it is, and $\sqrt{32400} = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 180$.

That last step is worth pausing on, because it is the first sign of what the notation buys. Halving each exponent is what taking a square root is, once the number is written this way. Chapter 1 described the same operation as dealing the primes into two identical piles, which is the same thing said in longer words.
