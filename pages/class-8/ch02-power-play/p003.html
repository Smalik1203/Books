Exponential notation
  • $n^a$ means $a$ copies of $n$ multiplied: $n$ is the base, $a$ the exponent.
  • $2^5 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 32$, but $2 \times 5 = 10$.

Do not mix up $3 \times 4$ and $3^4$. The first is three fours added, $4 + 4 + 4 = 12$. The second is four threes multiplied, $3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 81$.

The notation works for letters too, and for several bases. We count each factor that repeats:

$a \times a \times a \times b \times b$ $= a^3 b^2$
$2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 5$ $= 2^4 \times 5^2$

The second line is a prime factorisation written with powers. From now on we will write prime factorisations this way. It is shorter, and it shows the exponents clearly. In Chapter 1, the exponents told us whether a number is a square or a cube.

Example 1

Write $44\,100$ as a product of powers of primes. Then use the result to decide whether it is a perfect square.

Solution.

Step 1 $44100 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5 \times 7 \times 7$ divide by primes
Step 2 $= 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5^2 \times 7^2$ in exponential form
Step 3 $\sqrt{44100} = 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 = 210$ halve each exponent
Answer $2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5^2 \times 7^2$; a perfect square, with root $210$

Every exponent is even, so the primes split into two equal groups. Halving each exponent gives the square root. But $72 = 2^3 \times 3^2$ is not a square, because the exponent $3$ is odd.

One more point is easy to miss. A number written with no exponent has the exponent $1$. So $7$ means $7^1$, and $a$ means $a^1$. Every law in the next section needs this. If you forget it, you lose one factor each time: $2 \times 2^3$ has four factors of $2$, not three, so it equals $2^4 = 16$. Keep the hidden $1$ in mind whenever you count factors. In the same way, $a \times a^2$ has three factors of $a$, so it is $a^3$.
