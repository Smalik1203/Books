$a \times a \times a \times b \times b$ $= a^3 b^2$
$2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 5$ $= 2^4 \times 5^2$

That second line is the shape a prime factorisation naturally takes, and from here on it is how we will write one. It is shorter, and it puts the exponents where you can read them — which, after Chapter 1, is where the answers are.

Example 1

Write $32\,400$ as a product of powers of primes.

Divide by primes repeatedly until nothing is left but $1$.

Solution.

Step 1 $32400 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$ divide by primes
Step 2 $= 2^4 \times 3^4 \times 5^2$ in exponential form
Answer $32\,400 = 2^4 \times 3^4 \times 5^2$

Ten primes on the first line and three terms on the second, saying exactly the same thing. And a question Chapter 1 would have asked — is $32\,400$ a perfect square? — is now answered by looking: $4$, $4$ and $2$ are all even, so it is, and $\sqrt{32400} = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 180$.

That last step is worth pausing on, because it is the first sign of what the notation gives you. Halving each exponent is what taking a square root is, once the number is written this way. Chapter 1 described the same operation as dealing the primes into two identical piles, which is the same thing said in longer words.

One more piece of shorthand, and it is the one most often missed. A base written with no exponent carries an invisible $1$: the number $7$ means $7^1$, and the letter $a$ means $a^1$. That is not a small detail — every law in the next section needs it, because a lone base has to be counted along with the rest, and a student who forgets it loses exactly one factor every time.

2.2.1 Negative bases, and the exponent zero

Nothing in the definition requires the base to be positive. It is worth seeing what happens when it is not, because the answers change sign each time.

$(-4)^2$ $= (-4) \times (-4) = 16$
$(-4)^3$ $= (-4) \times (-4) \times (-4) = -64$
$(-1)^5$ $= -1$
$(-1)^{56}$ $= 1$

The minus signs cancel in pairs, so a negative base raised to an even power is positive and to an odd power is negative. There is no need to remember that separately from what Chapter 1 said about squares and cubes. It is the same fact, stated for every exponent rather than the first two.
