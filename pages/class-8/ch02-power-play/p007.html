Compare that law with the first one, and see how they divide the work between them. The first gathers powers that share a base and adds their exponents; this one gathers powers that share an exponent and multiplies their bases. Between them they cover both ways two powers can be alike. When two powers are alike in neither way, as $2^3$ and $5^4$ are, there is nothing to be done and the expression is already as simple as it gets.

Example 3

Find $2^5 \times 5^5$.

Evaluating $32 \times 3125$ would work. Noticing the shared exponent is quicker.

Solution.

Step 1 $2^5 \times 5^5 = (2 \times 5)^5$ the exponents match
Step 2 $= 10^5$
Answer $100000$

Whenever a pair of bases multiplies to something convenient — and $2$ and $5$ almost always do, because they make $10$ — this is the law to look for.

There is a matching law for division, which we will state properly in a moment: $\dfrac{m^a}{n^a} = \left(\dfrac{m}{n}\right)^a$. The argument is the same, with the pairs written as fractions.

2.3.3 Dividing

A line is $16$ units long — that is $2^4$ — and half of it is rubbed out. What is left is $2^4 \div 2$, and it is easiest to see written as a fraction with the cancelling shown:

$\dfrac{2^4}{2^1} = \dfrac{2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2}{2} = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^3 = 8$

Rub out half of what is left, twice more, and the same cancelling happens each time:

$2^4 \div 2^2$ $= 2^2 = 4$
$2^4 \div 2^3$ $= 2^1 = 2$

Every factor on the bottom cancels one on the top, so the exponents subtract. That is the third law, and it is the first law run backwards — which it has to be, since division undoes multiplication.

Dividing powers of the same base

$n^a \div n^b = n^{a-b}$, for any $n$ other than $0$. Multiplying adds the exponents, so dividing subtracts them.

Ruling out $n = 0$ is not being fussy. Dividing by $0^b$ would be dividing by zero, which is not defined, so the law has nothing to say there.
