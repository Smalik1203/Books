2.3.1 A power of a power

A row of factors can also be split into equal groups. This gives the second law. Here is $3^6$, split into two groups of three, and then into three groups of two:

two groups of three $(3 \times 3 \times 3) \times (3 \times 3 \times 3) = 3^3 \times 3^3 = (3^3)^2$
three groups of two $(3 \times 3) \times (3 \times 3) \times (3 \times 3) = (3^2)^3$

Both are $729$, because both are six threes, however we group them. The number of threes is the product of the two exponents:

$(3^3)^2 = 3^{3 \times 2} = 3^6 \qquad (3^2)^3 = 3^{2 \times 3} = 3^6$

A power raised to a power

$(n^a)^b = n^{ab}$, and since $ab = ba$, this also equals $(n^b)^a$. Raising to a power multiplies the exponents; multiplying powers adds them.

2.3.2 The same exponent, different bases

Anil plays a video game. He starts with $1$ point. His points double at each of the first four levels, and triple at each of the next four levels.

after four levels $1 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^4 = 16$
after four more $2^4 \times 3^4 = 16 \times 81$ $1296$

If the tripling levels came first, the answer would be $3^4 \times 2^4$, the same number. But there is more to see. Write out all eight factors, and pair each $2$ with a $3$:

$2^4 \times 3^4$ $= (2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2) \times (3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3)$
$= (2 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3)$
$= 6^4$ $1296$

We may regroup the factors because multiplication can be done in any order. It works only because the two exponents are equal. There are as many twos as threes, so every $2$ has a $3$ to pair with.

Powers with the same exponent

$m^a \times n^a = (mn)^a$. Read the other way, $(mn)^a = m^a n^a$: a power of a product is the product of the powers.
