Any number at all can be written as something between $1$ and $10$, multiplied by a power of ten. Take $5900$:

$5900$ $= 590 \times 10^1 = 59 \times 10^2 = 5.9 \times 10^3 = 0.59 \times 10^4$

All four are correct, and one of them is standard. The rule is that the first part must be at least $1$ and less than $10$ — which picks out $5.9 \times 10^3$ and rejects the rest.

Standard form

A number is in standard form, also called scientific notation, when it is written $x \times 10^y$ with $1 \le x \lt 10$ and $y$ an integer. The $x$ is the coefficient and the $y$ is the exponent.

The three facts become readable at once.

stars in our galaxy $1 \times 10^{11}$
Sun to Earth $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m
Sun to Saturn $1.4335 \times 10^{12}$ m

Now the comparison takes no effort at all: Saturn's exponent is $12$ and Earth's is $11$, so Saturn is about ten times further out. Nothing about the coefficients had to be examined.

That is the point of the notation, and it is worth stating plainly. The exponent matters more than the coefficient. If Mumbai's population of two crore is written $2 \times 10^7$ and someone changes the $2$ to a $3$, the city has grown by half. If they change the $7$ to an $8$, it has grown tenfold. The digit that carries the most meaning is the one the ordinary notation buries at the end in a run of zeros.

This is why every science writes its numbers this way. A chemist dealing with $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles and an astronomer dealing with $9.5 \times 10^{15}$ metres are not showing off; they are using the only notation in which the two numbers can be held in the head at the same time. Written out in full, one has twenty-four digits and the other sixteen, and nobody can compare those by eye. Written in standard form, the difference is $23$ against $15$, and the comparison is over before it began.

There is a second reason, less obvious and more practical. Multiplying two numbers in standard form is easy: multiply the coefficients and add the exponents, because the second half is just the first law of § 2.3. So $(3 \times 10^8) \times (2 \times 10^5)$ is $6 \times 10^{13}$, done in one line and in the head. Written out in full, the same multiplication is a page of arithmetic and an excellent chance to lose a zero.
