by cancelling $\dfrac{2^4}{2^5} = \dfrac{2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2} {2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2} = \dfrac12$
by the law $\dfrac{2^4}{2^5} = 2^{4-5} = 2^{-1}$

So $2^{-1} = \tfrac12$. Divide by $2^{10}$ instead and the same working gives $2^{-6} = \tfrac{1}{64}$, which is $\tfrac{1}{2^6}$. A negative exponent is a reciprocal, and again we did not decide it — the law did.

A negative exponent

$n^{-a} = \dfrac{1}{n^a}$, and equally $\dfrac{1}{n^{-a}} = n^a$, for every $n$ except $0$. The minus sign says divide by rather than multiply by; it never makes the answer negative.

$2^{-3}$ is $\tfrac18$, a small positive number. It is not $-8$, and it is not $-\tfrac18$. Compare $(-2)^3 = -8$, where the minus is on the base and does change the sign.

With that, the three laws hold for every integer exponent — positive, zero or negative — and they are the whole of the arithmetic of powers. Nothing further needs to be learnt about them; the rest of this chapter is about using them.

Set the powers of $2$ out in a row and the whole system can be seen at once. Each step to the right multiplies by $2$; each step to the left divides by it. The exponent is simply how many steps you are from $1$, counted positive to the right and negative to the left.

Read the line and the laws become obvious rather than remembered. Going from $2^1$ to $2^4$ is three steps right, which is multiplying by $2^3$; that is $2^1 \times 2^3 = 2^4$. Going from $2^4$ back to $2^1$ is three steps left, which is $2^4 \div 2^3 = 2^1$. And $2^0 = 1$ is not a special value but simply the place you happen to be standing when you have taken no steps at all.
