2.8A Note on Thought Experiments

Several questions in this chapter could not be answered exactly by anybody: how thick a sheet folded forty-six times would be, how many bees there are, how much clothing the world owns. They were answered anyway, and the method deserves a name.

The method is: write down what the answer depends on, make an honest guess at each piece, and multiply. Nobody knows how many bees there are, but the number of colonies is roughly known and the size of a colony is roughly known, and their product is a great deal better than nothing. An answer got this way is called an estimate, and the point of standard form is that it lets you say how much of it you believe.

Two rules keep such an estimate honest.

Write the assumptions down. If you assumed a colony holds fifty thousand bees, say so. Then a reader who thinks it is twenty thousand can correct your answer without redoing it, and you can see for yourself which assumption the answer really rests on.

Do not keep digits you have not earned. If the inputs are known to one figure, the answer is known to one figure. Multiplying $1 \times 10^8$ by $5 \times 10^4$ gives $5 \times 10^{12}$, and writing $5\,000\,000\,000\,000$ instead would suggest a precision that nothing in the calculation supports.

An estimate made this way is not a lesser kind of answer. It is often the only kind available, and it is usually enough. A town planner deciding whether a bridge will carry the traffic needs to know whether the answer is thousands or tens of thousands, not the exact figure to the last vehicle. The habit worth taking from this section is to ask how big, roughly? before asking exactly how much? — because the first question can nearly always be answered, and the second often cannot.

Think and Reflect

What date was one billion seconds ago? Estimate it before you calculate it — a year, a decade, a century?

Then work it out: a year is about $3.15 \times 10^7$ seconds. Most people are badly wrong on this one, and the reason is the same reason they were wrong about the folded sheet.

The reason, in both cases, is that we have no feel for repeated multiplication. Our instincts were built for adding. A number that doubles forty-six times, or a billion seconds laid end to end, is outside anything we have ever seen. The only reliable way through is to stop guessing and let the notation do the work.
