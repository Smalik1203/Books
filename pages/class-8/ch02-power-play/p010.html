2.4Zero and Negative Powers

The three laws came from rows of factors, so they were only about counting numbers. But the laws themselves do not mention counting. This raises a question. If the laws are to keep working, what must $2^0$ be?

Use the division law with two equal exponents. On the left, a number divided by itself gives $1$. On the right, the law gives an exponent of zero.

by cancelling $\dfrac{2^4}{2^4} = 1$
by the law $\dfrac{2^4}{2^4} = 2^{4-4} = 2^0$

The two answers must agree, so $2^0 = 1$. There was no choice. Once we ask the law to keep working, the value is fixed.

Anything to the power zero

$n^0 = 1$ for every $n$ except $0$. It is not a special rule to remember; the division law makes it so.

Now go one step further, and divide by more than you have:

by cancelling $\dfrac{2^4}{2^5} = \dfrac{2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2} {2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2} = \dfrac12$
by the law $\dfrac{2^4}{2^5} = 2^{4-5} = 2^{-1}$

So $2^{-1} = \tfrac12$. Divide $2^4$ by $2^{10}$ instead, and the same working gives $2^{-6} = \tfrac{1}{64}$, which is $\tfrac{1}{2^6}$. A negative exponent gives a reciprocal. Again, we did not decide this. The law did.

A negative exponent

$n^{-a} = \dfrac{1}{n^a}$, and $\dfrac{1}{n^{-a}} = n^a$, for every $n$ except $0$. The minus sign says divide by instead of multiply by. It never makes the answer negative.

$2^{-3}$ is $\tfrac18$, a small positive number. It is not $-8$, and it is not $-\tfrac18$. Compare $(-2)^3 = -8$, where the minus sign is on the base and does change the sign.

With this, the three laws work for every integer exponent: positive, zero or negative. The rest of this chapter is about using them.
