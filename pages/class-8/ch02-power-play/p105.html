Two things follow for free. The same is true of the ninth power, and the thirteenth, and every power of the form $4k + 1$. And the last digit of any power can be found by dividing the exponent by the length of its cycle and keeping the remainder, which is how the $7^{100}$ question was answered without a single multiplication of large numbers.

Two remarks now that you have worked them. The questions on $2^{60}$, $2^{100}$, $3^{500}$ and the rumour are all the same move in different clothes: two quantities cannot be compared as written, so rewrite one of them until it shares something with the other. Get into the habit of asking what could these two have in common? rather than reaching for a calculator that cannot hold the answer anyway.

The questions on $7^{100}$ and the fifth power are about patterns in last digits, and there the manoeuvre is different: compute the first four or five cases, look for the cycle, and then find the reason the cycle must repeat. A pattern you have spotted is only a guess; a pattern you have explained is a result. Both questions ask for the second.

If you cannot get started, make the question smaller. Ask about $7^{10}$ before $7^{100}$, or about eight squares of the chessboard before sixty-four. Every explanation above was found that way, and the small version is usually enough to see the shape of the large one.

One more thing to notice as you work. Not one of these questions can be finished on a calculator, and several would break it — $2^{100}$ has thirty-one digits and $3^{500}$ has two hundred and thirty-nine. That is not an accident of how they were chosen. It is what happens as soon as exponents grow at all, and it is why the laws of § 2.3 are worth having: they are the only way to reason about numbers that cannot be written down.

The chapter proper showed the laws being used to simplify. Here they are being used to compare, to estimate, and to rule things out, which is where they do the most work. A law you can only apply when the question is already in the right shape is half a law.

Type 1 · A number as powers of primes

Example 1

Write $2352$ as a product of powers of primes. Is it a perfect square? If not, find the smallest number it must be multiplied by to make one.

Solution.

Step 1 $2352 \div 2 \div 2 \div 2 \div 2 = 147$, and $147 = 3 \times 49$ divide by primes
Step 2 $2352 = 2^4 \times 3^1 \times 7^2$
Step 3 the exponent of $3$ is odd so not a square
Step 4 $2352 \times 3 = 7056 = 2^4 \times 3^2 \times 7^2$ every exponent even
Answer $2^4 \times 3 \times 7^2$; not a square; multiply by $3$, and $7056 = 84^2$
