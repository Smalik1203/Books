One more piece of shorthand, and it is the one most often missed. A base written with no exponent carries an invisible $1$: the number $7$ means $7^1$, and the letter $a$ means $a^1$. That is not a technicality — every law in the next section needs it, because a lone base has to be counted along with the rest, and a student who forgets it loses exactly one factor every time.

2.2.1 Negative bases, and the exponent zero

Nothing in the definition requires the base to be positive. It is worth seeing what happens when it is not, because the answers alternate.

$(-4)^2$ $= (-4) \times (-4) = 16$
$(-4)^3$ $= (-4) \times (-4) \times (-4) = -64$
$(-1)^5$ $= -1$
$(-1)^{56}$ $= 1$

The minus signs cancel in pairs, so a negative base raised to an even power is positive and to an odd power is negative. There is no need to remember that separately from Chapter 1's remark about squares and cubes; it is the same fact, stated for every exponent rather than the first two.

The brackets are not decoration. $(-4)^2 = 16$, but $-4^2 = -16$, because without brackets the power applies to the $4$ alone and the minus sign is applied afterwards.

The exponent $0$ is a different kind of question, since $n$ multiplied by itself no times is not something you can picture. We shall come to it in § 2.4, where the laws of the next section will settle it for us — and settle it in only one possible way.
