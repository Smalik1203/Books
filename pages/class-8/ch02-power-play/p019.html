2.8A Note on Estimating

Some questions in this chapter have no exact answer. Nobody can fold paper forty-two times, or count every leaf in a forest. We answered them anyway, and the method we used deserves a name.

Here is the method. Write down what the answer depends on. Make a sensible guess for each part. Then multiply. Nobody knows the exact number of leaves in a forest. But we can estimate the number of trees and the number of leaves on one tree, and their product is a useful answer. An answer found this way is called an estimate. Standard form lets you show how much of it you trust.

Two rules keep an estimate honest.

Write the assumptions down. If you assumed that a tree has twenty thousand leaves, say so. A reader who thinks it has fifty thousand can then correct your answer without starting all over again.

Do not keep digits you have not earned. If each input is known to one digit, the answer is known to one digit. Multiplying $4 \times 10^5$ by $2 \times 10^4$ gives $8 \times 10^9$. Writing $8\,000\,000\,000$ instead would suggest an accuracy that the inputs do not have.

An estimate is often the only answer we can get, and it is usually enough. A town planner deciding how wide to make a road needs to know whether about a thousand or about ten thousand vehicles use it each day. The exact figure is not needed. So ask how big, roughly? before you ask exactly how much?

Example 10

A family of four eats rice every day. Estimate the number of grains of rice the family eats in a year.

Solution.

Step 1 assume $200$ g a person a day, and $0.02$ g a grain write the assumptions
Step 2 $(2 \times 10^2) \div (2 \times 10^{-2}) = 10^4$ grains a person a day divide the powers
Step 3 $4 \times 10^4 \times 365 = 1.46 \times 10^7$ four people, a year
Answer about $1 \times 10^7$ grains, or one crore

Each assumption is known to one digit only, so the answer keeps one digit. Writing $14\,600\,000$ would claim far more than we know.
