Using What You Know

The chapter left three laws and one habit. The laws: multiplying powers of the same base adds the exponents, raising a power to a power multiplies them, and dividing subtracts. The habit: when two powers cannot be compared as they stand, rewrite them until they share a base or an exponent, and then the comparison is easy.

Every question below can be done with that. Four of them look like they need a calculator and none of them does — in each case there is a way of rewriting that makes the answer visible. Try each before reading what follows it.

Which is larger, $2^{60}$ or $60^3$? Then find the whole number $n$ from which $2^n$ is larger than $n^3$ and stays larger.

$60^3 = 216\,000$, which is six digits. $2^{60}$ is a great deal more: since $2^{10} = 1024$ is a little over $10^3$, raising both sides to the sixth power gives $2^{60}$ a little over $10^{18}$. So $2^{60}$ wins, and not narrowly — it is about $5 \times 10^{12}$ times larger.

The second half is more interesting, because for small $n$ the answer goes the other way. Make a table:

$n$12345678910
$2^n$2481632641282565121024
$n^3$1827641252163435127291000

$2^n$ is ahead only at $n = 1$; from $n = 2$ to $n = 9$, $n^3$ leads. At $n = 10$ the power takes the lead, $1024$ against $1000$, and never gives it back. Each step doubles $2^n$, but from here on it multiplies $n^3$ by much less than $2$: $11^3 = 1331$ is not even one and a half times $1000$, and the step only gets smaller as $n$ grows.

That is the shape of every such race. A power with the variable in the exponent starts slowly, loses for a while, and then wins by a distance nothing can close.

What is the last digit of $7^{100}$?

You cannot compute $7^{100}$, and you do not need to: last digits depend only on last digits, so work with those alone.
