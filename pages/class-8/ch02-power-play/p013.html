2.5Powers of Ten

Of all the bases, $10$ is the one our numerals are built from, and writing its powers out makes the place-value system say what it has always meant. Take $47\,561$, written in expanded form:

$(4 \times 10000) + (7 \times 1000) + (5 \times 100) + (6 \times 10) + 1$

Every one of those multipliers is a power of ten, so

$47561 = (4 \times 10^4) + (7 \times 10^3) + (5 \times 10^2) + (6 \times 10^1) + (1 \times 10^0)$

Look at what the exponents are doing: $4, 3, 2, 1, 0$, one for each column, counting down from the left. The exponent is the place. That is what place value has always meant, and writing it in powers is the first time it says so out loud.

Which raises an obvious question. The columns do not stop at the units — there is a decimal point and then more columns. If the exponents have been counting down, what comes after $0$?

$561.903 = (5 \times 10^2) + (6 \times 10^1) + (1 \times 10^0) + (9 \times 10^{-1}) + (0 \times 10^{-2}) + (3 \times 10^{-3})$

They go negative, and they mean what § 2.4 said they mean: $10^{-1}$ is $\tfrac{1}{10}$, $10^{-2}$ is $\tfrac{1}{100}$, $10^{-3}$ is $\tfrac{1}{1000}$. The tenths, hundredths and thousandths columns were negative powers of ten all along, and the decimal point is not a break in the pattern but the place where the exponent passes zero.

The decimal point is a marker, not a boundary

Every column of a decimal numeral is a power of ten, and the exponents run down through the whole number without interruption: $\ldots, 10^2, 10^1, 10^0$, point, $10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, \ldots$

2.5.1 Standard form

Here are three facts about the sky, written the ordinary way.

stars in our galaxy about $100\,000\,000\,000$
Sun to Earth $149\,600\,000\,000$ m
Sun to Saturn $1\,433\,500\,000\,000$ m

Nobody can read those. You cannot see at a glance which is biggest, and counting zeros by eye is exactly the sort of task at which eyes fail. What is wanted is a way of writing a number that puts its size first and its digits second.
