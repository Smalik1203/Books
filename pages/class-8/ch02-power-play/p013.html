This is why every science writes its numbers this way. A chemist dealing with $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles and an astronomer dealing with $9.5 \times 10^{15}$ metres are not showing off. They are using the only notation in which the two numbers can be held in the head at the same time. Written out in full, one has twenty-four digits and the other sixteen, and nobody can compare those by eye. Written in standard form, the difference is $23$ against $15$, and the comparison is over before it began.

There is a second reason, less obvious and more practical. Multiplying two numbers in standard form is easy: multiply the coefficients and add the exponents, because the second half is just the first law of § 2.3. So $(3 \times 10^8) \times (2 \times 10^5)$ is $6 \times 10^{13}$, done in one line and in the head. Written out in full, the same multiplication is a page of arithmetic and an excellent chance to lose a zero.

Example 6

Write $59\,853$ and $0.00042$ in standard form.

For the first, put the point after the leading digit and count how many places it moved.

For the second the point moves the other way, so the exponent is negative.

Solution.

Step 1 $59853 = 5.9853 \times 10^4$ point moved $4$ places left
Step 2 $0.00042 = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}$ point moved $4$ places right
Answer $5.9853 \times 10^4$ and $4.2 \times 10^{-4}$

A check worth doing every time: a number bigger than $10$ has a positive exponent and a number smaller than $1$ has a negative one. If your sign disagrees with the size of the number, the sign is wrong.

Think and Reflect

A town's population is reported as $1\,42\,395$. Written as $1.42395 \times 10^5$ that claims to be right down to the last person, which no census ever is.

What does writing it as $1.42 \times 10^5$ claim instead? And $1.4 \times 10^5$? What is the coefficient's length telling you?

The answer is that the coefficient's length is a statement about how well the number is known. A census that is sure to the nearest thousand should be written $1.42 \times 10^5$; one sure only to the nearest ten thousand should be written $1.4 \times 10^5$. Writing more digits than you are sure of is not extra precision, it is a false claim — and standard form makes the claim visible, which the ordinary notation does not.

The same care applies at the small end. Physicists write the mass of an electron as $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, and the three digits of that coefficient are a claim that the value is known to about one part in a thousand. Writing $9 \times 10^{-31}$ would have claimed less and been equally true. What you must never do is add digits that were never measured.
