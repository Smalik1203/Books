The decimal point is a marker, not a boundary

Every column of a decimal numeral is a power of ten, and the exponents run down through the whole number without interruption: $\ldots, 10^2, 10^1, 10^0$, point, $10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, \ldots$

2.5.1 Standard form

Here are three facts about the sky, written the ordinary way.

stars in our galaxy about $100\,000\,000\,000$
Sun to Earth $149\,600\,000\,000$ m
Sun to Saturn $1\,433\,500\,000\,000$ m

Nobody can read those. You cannot see at a glance which is biggest, and counting zeros by eye is exactly the kind of thing eyes are bad at. What is wanted is a way of writing a number that puts its size first and its digits second.

Any number at all can be written as something between $1$ and $10$, multiplied by a power of ten. Take $5900$:

$5900$ $= 590 \times 10^1 = 59 \times 10^2 = 5.9 \times 10^3 = 0.59 \times 10^4$

All four are correct, and one of them is standard. The rule is that the first part must be at least $1$ and less than $10$ — which picks out $5.9 \times 10^3$ and rejects the rest.

Standard form

A number is in standard form, also called scientific notation, when it is written $x \times 10^y$ with $1 \le x \lt 10$ and $y$ an integer. The $x$ is the coefficient and the $y$ is the exponent.

The three facts become readable at once.

stars in our galaxy $1 \times 10^{11}$
Sun to Earth $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m
Sun to Saturn $1.4335 \times 10^{12}$ m

Now the comparison takes no effort at all: Saturn's exponent is $12$ and Earth's is $11$, so Saturn is about ten times further out. You did not have to look at the coefficients at all.

That is the point of the notation, and it is worth stating plainly. The exponent matters more than the coefficient. If Mumbai's population of two crore is written $2 \times 10^7$ and someone changes the $2$ to a $3$, the city has grown by half. If they change the $7$ to an $8$, it has grown tenfold. The digit that carries the most meaning is the one the ordinary notation buries at the end in a run of zeros.
