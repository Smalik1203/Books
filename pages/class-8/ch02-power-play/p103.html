The general recipe is worth keeping. To compare $a^m$ with $b^n$, take the highest common factor of $m$ and $n$, write both with that as the exponent, and compare the new bases. It converts an impossible arithmetic problem into one you can do in your head.

Those two explanations used the same idea from opposite ends. The first forced a shared base — powers of ten — so that the exponents could be compared. The second forced a shared exponent so that the bases could be. Which of the two is available depends on the numbers, and finding out costs a moment: look at whether the bases are powers of a common number, and look at whether the exponents have a common factor. One of the two nearly always works.

A rumour starts with one person. Every hour, everybody who knows it tells two people who do not. How many hours before a city of a million knows?

Read the situation carefully before writing anything, because the obvious answer is the wrong one. If everybody who knows tells two people who do not, then after an hour there are the original knowers and twice as many new ones — so the number knowing is multiplied by $3$, not by $2$.

start $1$
after 1 hour $1 + 2 = 3$
after 2 hours $3 + 6 = 9 = 3^2$
after $n$ hours $3^n$

We need $3^n \ge 10^6$. Since $3^6 = 729$, we get $3^{12} = 729^2 = 531\,441$, which is not enough. One hour more triples it: $3^{13} = 1\,594\,323$, which is more than a million.

answer $13$ hours a city of a million

Thirteen hours, from one person, using nothing but conversation. The result is not really about rumours — it is the same arithmetic that governs how an epidemic spreads, how a chain letter travels, and how a video goes around the world overnight. In each case what looks like an explosion is only a modest number multiplied a modest number of times.

A king agrees to pay a debt in rice: one grain on the first square of a chessboard, two on the second, four on the third, and so on to the sixty-fourth. Roughly how many grains is that, and is it a debt anyone could pay?

The squares hold $1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots$ so the sixty-fourth holds $2^{63}$ and the total is

$1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 2^{63} = 2^{64} - 1$

The sum is one short of the next power, which you can see by noticing that $1 + 2 = 3$, $1 + 2 + 4 = 7$, $1+2+4+8 = 15$ — always one less than the doubling that comes next.
