A king agrees to pay a debt in rice: one grain on the first square of a chessboard, two on the second, four on the third, and so on to the sixty-fourth. Roughly how many grains is that, and is it a debt anyone could pay?

The chapter said $n^0 = 1$ for every $n$ except $0$. Work out what $0^n$ is for $n = 1, 2, 3$, and then say why $0^0$ is left undefined rather than given a value.

Take any whole number, raise it to the fifth power, and compare the last digit of the answer with the last digit you started with. Try it on six different numbers, say what you find, and then explain it.

Two remarks before you start. The first three questions and the fifth are all the same manoeuvre in different clothes: two quantities cannot be compared as written, so rewrite one of them until it shares something with the other. Get into the habit of asking what could these two have in common? rather than reaching for a calculator that will overflow anyway.

The other three are about patterns in last digits, and there the manoeuvre is different: compute the first four or five cases, look for the cycle, and then find the reason the cycle must repeat. A pattern spotted is a conjecture; a pattern explained is a result. Both questions ask for the second.

If a question resists, make it smaller. Ask about $7^{10}$ before $7^{100}$, or about eight squares of the chessboard before sixty-four. Every solution overleaf was found that way, and the small version is usually enough to see the shape of the large one.

One more thing to notice as you work. Not one of these questions can be finished on a calculator, and several would break it — $2^{100}$ has thirty-one digits and $3^{500}$ has two hundred and thirty-nine. That is not an accident of how they were chosen. It is what happens as soon as exponents grow at all, and it is why the laws of § 2.3 are worth having: they are the only way to reason about numbers that cannot be written down.

The chapter proper showed the laws being used to simplify. Here they are being used to compare, to estimate, and to rule things out, which is where they earn their keep. A law you can only apply when the question is already in the right shape is half a law.
