Example 6

Write $59\,853$ and $0.00042$ in standard form.

For the first, put the point after the leading digit and count how many places it moved.

$59853$ $= 5.9853 \times 10^4$ — the point moved $4$ places left

For the second the point moves the other way, so the exponent is negative.

$0.00042$ $= 4.2 \times 10^{-4}$ — the point moved $4$ places right

A check worth doing every time: a number bigger than $10$ has a positive exponent and a number smaller than $1$ has a negative one. If your sign disagrees with the size of the number, the sign is wrong.

Think and Reflect

A town's population is reported as $1\,42\,395$. Written as $1.42395 \times 10^5$ that claims to be right down to the last person, which no census ever is.

What does writing it as $1.42 \times 10^5$ claim instead? And $1.4 \times 10^5$? What is the coefficient's length telling you?

The answer is that the coefficient's length is a statement about how well the number is known. A census that is sure to the nearest thousand should be written $1.42 \times 10^5$; one sure only to the nearest ten thousand should be written $1.4 \times 10^5$. Writing more digits than you are sure of is not extra precision, it is a false claim — and standard form makes the claim visible, which the ordinary notation does not.

The same care applies at the small end. Physicists write the mass of an electron as $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, and the three digits of that coefficient are a claim that the value is known to about one part in a thousand. Writing $9 \times 10^{-31}$ would have claimed less and been equally true. What nobody may do is add digits that were never measured.

One consequence catches people out. Converting units does not improve a measurement. A distance given as $4$ km is known to the nearest kilometre; writing it as $4000$ m does not suddenly make it known to the nearest metre, though the three zeros give that impression. In standard form the difficulty disappears, because $4 \times 10^3$ m carries exactly the one digit it started with.
