Trading powers of two for powers of ten

$2^{10} = 1024$ and $10^3 = 1000$, so $2^{10}$ is larger — by $2.4\%$, which is small enough to ignore and large enough to matter later.

Now raise both to the tenth power. Raising the larger of two positive numbers to a power keeps it the larger, so

$2^{100}$ $= (2^{10})^{10} = 1024^{10}$
$10^{30}$ $= (10^3)^{10} = 1000^{10}$

So $2^{100}$ is larger. And the margin can be estimated too: each of the ten factors is $2.4\%$ bigger, and $1.024^{10}$ is about $1.27$, so $2^{100}$ is roughly $1.27 \times 10^{30}$. That is the true value to three figures.

$2^{10} \approx 10^3$ is the single most useful approximation in this chapter. It says that ten doublings are about a thousandfold, which is why computer memories come in sizes like $1024$ and why the folded sheet reached the Moon: forty-six doublings are about four and a half thousandfolds stacked up.

Two powers with nothing in common, made comparable

$3^{500}$ and $5^{300}$ share neither base nor exponent, so neither law applies as written. The move is to force a shared exponent, and $500$ and $300$ have $100$ as a common factor.

$3^{500}$ $= (3^5)^{100} = 243^{100}$
$5^{300}$ $= (5^3)^{100} = 125^{100}$

Now they are comparable at a glance: same exponent, and $243 \gt 125$, so $3^{500}$ is the larger. It is larger by a factor of $(243/125)^{100}$, which works out at around $10^{29}$ — an enormous margin, from what had looked like a close-run thing.

The general recipe is worth keeping. To compare $a^m$ with $b^n$, take the highest common factor of $m$ and $n$, write both with that as the exponent, and compare the new bases. It converts an impossible arithmetic problem into one you can do in your head.

Those two solutions used the same idea from opposite ends. The first forced a shared base — powers of ten — so that the exponents could be compared. The second forced a shared exponent so that the bases could be. Which of the two is available depends on the numbers, and finding out costs a moment: look at whether the bases are powers of a common number, and look at whether the exponents have a common factor. One of the two nearly always works.
