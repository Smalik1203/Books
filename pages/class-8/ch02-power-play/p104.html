The chapter said $n^0 = 1$ for every $n$ except $0$. Work out what $0^n$ is for $n = 1, 2, 3$, and then say why $0^0$ is left undefined rather than given a value.

Two rules are pulling in opposite directions, and there is no way to satisfy both.

$n^0 = 1$ for every $n$ we have allowed — so this wants $0^0 = 1$
$0^n = 0$ since $0^1 = 0$, $0^2 = 0$, $0^3 = 0$ — so this wants $0^0 = 0$

Approach the corner along one road and the answer looks like $1$; approach it along the other and it looks like $0$. Since no single value keeps both patterns intact, mathematicians do not choose either, and $0^0$ is left undefined.

This is worth understanding rather than memorising, because it is how mathematics generally deals with a gap. Recall § 2.4: $n^0$ and $n^{-a}$ were not discovered, they were defined — and defined in the only way that let the laws we already had keep working. When there is such a way, the definition is made and nobody thinks of it as a choice. When there is no such way, as here, the case is left open. Leaving a thing undefined is not a failure; it is a refusal to write down something that would be wrong half the time.

Take any whole number, raise it to the fifth power, and compare the last digit of the answer with the last digit you started with. Try it on six different numbers, say what you find, and then explain it.

Try six of them and the pattern is clear.

$2^5 = 32$ends in $2$
$3^5 = 243$ends in $3$
$4^5 = 1024$ends in $4$
$7^5 = 16807$ends in $7$
$8^5 = 32768$ends in $8$
$9^5 = 59049$ends in $9$

Every time, the fifth power ends in the digit it started with. The reason is the cycle of last digits, met in the question on $7^{100}$. Every digit's last-digit cycle has length $1$, $2$ or $4$ — check them and see — and $5 - 1 = 4$ is a multiple of all three. So raising to the fifth power always lands exactly one step past a whole number of cycles, which is where it started.
