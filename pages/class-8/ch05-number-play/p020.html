Summary
  1. Whether a number is even or odd is its parity. Odd $\pm$ odd and even $\pm$ even are even, and odd $\pm$ even is odd. So $p \pm q \pm r \pm s$ has the same parity for every choice of signs. For example, $7 + 8 + 9 + 10 = 34$ is even, so all eight ways of placing signs between $7, 8, 9$ and $10$ give even values.
  2. A statement about numbers may be always true, sometimes true or never true. One counterexample shows that it is not always true. To show that it is always true, we need a reason, in algebra or in a picture, that covers every number.
  3. If $M$ and $N$ are multiples of $a$, then so are $M + N$ and $M - N$. If $A$ is divisible by $k$, then every multiple of $A$ is divisible by $k$, and $A$ is divisible by every factor of $k$.
  4. If a number is divisible by $k$ and by $m$, it is divisible by the LCM of $k$ and $m$. A number divisible by $5$ and $6$ is divisible by $30$, but a number divisible by $10$ and $4$ need only be divisible by $20$.
  5. The numbers that leave a remainder of $r$ when divided by $n$ have the form $nk + r$. For example, the numbers that leave $4$ when divided by $6$ are $6k + 4$: $4, 10, 16, \ldots$
  6. Writing a number with letters for its digits, as $1000d + 100c + 10b + a$, shows why the tests for divisibility work.
  7. A number is divisible by $9$, or by $3$, exactly when the sum of its digits is. It is divisible by $11$ exactly when adding and subtracting its digits in turn, from the units digit, gives $0$ or a multiple of $11$. Tests can be combined: $4$ and $9$ together test for $36$.
  8. Adding the digits of a number again and again until one digit is left gives its digital root. It is the remainder on division by $9$, except that multiples of $9$ have digital root $9$.
  9. In a cryptarithm, letters hide digits. Units digits and the number of digits in the answer narrow down the choices. For example, $\text{AB} \times 4 = \text{CA}$ gives $23 \times 4 = 92$.
