Picture $12m$ as $m$ rows of twelve. A factor of $12$ fills one row exactly, so it fills all $m$ rows exactly. So the factors of a multiple of $12$ include all the factors of $12$. For example, the factors of $24$ are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12$ and $24$. Statement 4 is always true.

Factors of a divisor

If $A$ is divisible by $k$, then $A$ is divisible by every factor of $k$.

Statement 5. If a number is divisible by $7$, then it is also divisible by any multiple of $7$.

A number divisible by $7$ is a multiple of $7$, say $7k$. A multiple of $7$ is $7m$. Now $7k$ is divisible by $7m$ only when $m$ is a factor of $k$. If $k = ym$, then

$7k \div 7m = 7ym \div 7m = y$

For example, $42 = 7 \times 6$ is divisible by $7$, but it is not divisible by $28 = 7 \times 4$. It is divisible by $14 = 7 \times 2$. So Statement 5 is only sometimes true.

Decide for yourself whether each of the next two statements is always true, sometimes true or never true.

Statement 6. If a number is divisible by both $9$ and $4$, it must be divisible by $36$.

Statement 7. If a number is divisible by both $6$ and $4$, it must be divisible by $24$.

Divisible by two numbers

If $A$ is divisible by $k$ and $A$ is also divisible by $m$, then $A$ is divisible by the LCM of $k$ and $m$. This is because $A$ is a multiple of $k$ and a multiple of $m$, so the prime factorisation of $A$ contains the prime factorisation of the LCM of $k$ and $m$.

Statement 8. When you add an odd number to an even number, you get a multiple of $6$.

All multiples of $6$ are even numbers. But the sum of an odd number and an even number is an odd number. So this statement is never true.

We can also show this with algebra. Write the even number as $2n$ and the odd number as $2m + 1$. Suppose their sum is a multiple of $6$, say $6j$:

$(2n) + (2m + 1) = 6j$
then $2n + 2m = 6j - 1$
so $2(n + m) = 6j - 1$

This says that $2(n + m)$, an even number, equals $6j - 1$, an odd number. That can never happen.
