Combining Tests

How can we test for $6$? Since $6 = 2 \times 3$, try the tests for $2$ and for $3$ on $54$, $111$, $244$ and $318$. Then divide each by $6$ to check.

Now try to test for $36$ in the same way. Is it enough to check $3$ and $12$, since $3 \times 12 = 36$? No. The number $24$ is divisible by both $3$ and $12$, but not by $36$. Checking $4$ and $9$ does work. Use prime factorisation to explain why $4$ and $9$ work for $36$, but $3$ and $12$ do not.

There are tests like these for many other numbers, some of them beyond $100$. You will meet more of them in later classes.

Digital Roots

Take a number and add its digits. Then add the digits of the answer, and keep going until one digit is left. That digit is called the digital root of the number. For example, the digital root of $758463$ is $6$:

$7 + 5 + 8 + 4 + 6 + 3 = 33 \qquad 3 + 3 = 6$

We met this when we built the test for $9$. The digital root is the remainder on division by $9$, except that a multiple of $9$ has digital root $9$. So $758463$ leaves a remainder of $6$ when divided by $9$.

Try these. Which numbers between $300$ and $400$ have digital root $4$? Which have digital root $8$? Write the digital roots of $15$ consecutive numbers, starting anywhere, and describe the pattern. Then find the digital roots of several consecutive multiples of $3$, of $6$ and of $5$. Explain the patterns you find.

Digital roots
  • A number and its digital root leave the same remainder on division by $9$.
  • The digital root of a multiple of $9$ (other than $0$) is always $9$.

  2. Shuffle the digits of $758463$ in any order. Why does the digital root stay the same?
  3. Why does the test for $11$ not work with a plain digit sum?

Adding digits again and again is an old idea. The Indian mathematician Aryabhata II, who lived around $950$ CE, described it in his work Mahāsiddhānta, and it was used to check the answers of long calculations.
