More on Divisibility Shortcuts

How can we find out whether a number is divisible by $6$? Will it work to check divisibility by its factors $2$ and $3$? Use the shortcuts for $2$ and $3$ on the numbers $38$, $225$, $186$ and $64$. Then divide each number by $6$ to check.

What about divisibility by $24$? Will it work to check divisibility by its factors $4$ and $6$? Why, or why not?

Checking divisibility by $4$ and by $6$ does not settle divisibility by $24$. For example, $12$ is divisible by both $4$ and $6$, but not by $24$. To check divisibility by $24$, check divisibility by $3$ and by $8$ instead.

Use prime factorisation to explain why checking $3$ and $8$ works for $24$, but checking $4$ and $6$ is not enough.

There are shortcuts like these for every number up to $100$, and for some numbers beyond $100$. You can learn how they work in higher classes.

Digital Roots

Take a number. Add its digits, and keep adding the digits of the result until you get a single-digit number. This single digit is called the digital root of the number. For example, the digital root of $489710$ is $2$:

$4 + 8 + 9 + 7 + 1 + 0 = 29 \qquad 2 + 9 = 11 \qquad 1 + 1 = 2$

What property do you think the digital root has? Recall that we did this when we found the shortcut for divisibility by $9$.

Between $600$ and $700$, which numbers have the digital root $5$? Which have the digital root $7$? Which have $3$?

Write down the digital roots of any $12$ consecutive numbers. What do you see?

We saw that the digital root of a multiple of $9$ is always $9$. Now find the digital roots of some consecutive multiples of $3$, of $4$ and of $6$.

What are the digital roots of numbers that are $1$ more than a multiple of $6$? What do you notice? Try to explain the patterns you find.

Think and Reflect

A riddle. Every digit of my number is the same, and it is the smallest odd digit. How many digits I have, the sum of my digits, and my digital root are all one number: the largest odd single-digit number. What is my number, and how do you say it?

Aryabhata II, who lived around $950$ CE, describes in his work Mahāsiddhānta how to find the digital root of a number by adding its digits again and again until one digit is left. The method is known to have been used to check the results of arithmetic.
