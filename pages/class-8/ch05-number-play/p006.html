What happens when you add a multiple of $4$ to an even number that is not a multiple of $4$? Is it like the parity of the sum of an even number and an odd number? Here is the algebra. Write the explanation and some examples yourself, and draw the rows.

$4p + (4q + 2) = 4p + 4q + 2 = 4(p + q) + 2$

Notice how algebra, and pictures of rows, let us prove a property of numbers for every case at once, and not only check it for a few.

Always, Sometimes, or Never

Here are some statements about factors and multiples. For each one we find out whether it is always true, sometimes true or never true. We already know that the sum of any two multiples of $2$ is a multiple of $2$.

Statement 1. If $8$ divides each of two numbers exactly, it divides their sum exactly.

Two numbers with $8$ as a factor are multiples of $8$, so we can write them as $8a$ and $8b$. A multiple of $8$ is made by adding $8$ again and again, so the sum of two multiples of $8$ is also a multiple of $8$:

$8a + 8b = 8(a + b)$

For example, take $8$ and $16$, $16$ and $56$, or $80$ and $120$: $8 + 16 = 8(1 + 2) = 24$, $16 + 56 = 72$ and $80 + 120 = 200$, and each sum is a multiple of $8$. Statement 1 is always true. Find out whether it is also true for the difference of the two numbers.
