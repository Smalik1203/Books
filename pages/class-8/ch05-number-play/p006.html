$6a + 6b = 6(a + b)$

In rows of six, $a$ rows and $b$ rows make $a + b$ rows, with nothing left over (Fig. 5.6).

For example, $12 + 30 = 6 \times (2 + 5) = 42$, $18 + 54 = 72$ and $60 + 90 = 150$, and each sum is a multiple of $6$. Statement 1 is always true. Check whether it is also true for the difference of the two numbers, such as $30 - 12$.

Sums and differences of multiples

If $M$ and $N$ are both multiples of $a$, then $M + N$ and $M - N$ are also multiples of $a$.

Statement 2. If a number is divisible by $6$, then any two numbers that add up to it are both divisible by $6$.

The number $54$ is divisible by $6$. It can be split as $24 + 30$, and both parts are multiples of $6$. But it can also be split as $25 + 29$, and neither part is a multiple of $6$. So Statement 2 is sometimes true. In fact the two parts always go together. If $54 = x + y$ and $x$ is a multiple of $6$, then $y = 54 - x$ is the difference of two multiples of $6$, so it is a multiple of $6$ too. Either both parts are multiples of $6$, or neither is.

Statement 3. If a number is divisible by $5$, then every multiple of that number is divisible by $5$ as well.

A number divisible by $5$ can be written as $5j$. A multiple of it is $5j \times m$. Picture $5j$ as $j$ rows of five. Then $m$ copies of it are $m$ blocks of $j$ rows, which is $mj$ rows of five (Fig. 5.7). So $5j \times m = 5 \times (jm)$, which is a multiple of $5$. The order of multiplying does not matter, so we may group the $5$ on its own.
