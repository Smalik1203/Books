4.3The Sierpiński Triangle

The same idea with a triangle instead of a square. Take an equilateral triangle, join the midpoints of its three sides, and remove the middle triangle that appears. Three triangles are left, each half the size of the original; do the same to each of them, and go on for ever.

Why do the midpoints cut the triangle into four identical triangles? Because each of the three corner pieces has two sides that are halves of the original's sides, with the same angle between them. So all three are copies of one another, and the middle one, made of the three midpoint-joining segments, matches them too.

The counting runs as before, with three where the carpet had eight. At step $n$ there are $3^{\,n}$ triangles left. And each step keeps three quarters of the area, since one of four identical triangles goes.

$$\text{area at step } n \;=\; \left(\frac34\right)^{\! n}.$$

4.4The Koch Snowflake

Helge von Koch described this one in 1904, and it works the other way round: it adds instead of taking away. Start again with an equilateral triangle. Divide every side into three equal parts, build a small equilateral triangle outwards on the middle part, and rub out the middle part itself. Each straight side has become a bump with four sides. Then do the same to every one of the new sides.
