Taking a Shape Apart

4.1Introduction

Look closely at a fern. A frond carries small leaves, and each leaf is shaped like the frond; each leaf carries smaller leaflets, and each of those is shaped like the leaf. The same form appears again at every scale, until the plant runs out of room to keep going.

Trees do it — trunk, limb, branch, twig. So do clouds, coastlines, mountain ridges and a fork of lightning. A shape built of smaller copies of itself is called a fractal, and the first half of this chapter is about three of them that can be drawn exactly.

The second half is about solid shapes, and it is the same question turned round. A fern is a shape you cannot draw in full because it never ends; a box is a shape you cannot draw in full because paper is flat. Both are handled the same way — by taking the shape apart into pieces you already understand, and reading it back from them.

4.2The Sierpiński Carpet

The Polish mathematician Wacław Sierpiński described this one in 1916. Start with a square. Divide it into nine equal squares and take the middle one away. Now do the same to each of the eight squares that are left, and then to each of the squares left after that, for ever.

Nobody can draw the carpet itself; every drawing is a step on the way to it. But the steps can be counted, and counting them is where the mathematics is.
