Holes are easier to miscount. Every remaining square makes exactly one new hole at the next step, and every hole already there stays. So $H_{n+1} = H_n + R_n$, and the holes pile up as a sum rather than a single power.

step 0 $R_0 = 1$,   $H_0 = 0$
step 1 $R_1 = 8$,   $H_1 = 1$
step 2 $R_2 = 64$,   $H_2 = 1 + 8 = 9$
step 3 $R_3 = 512$,   $H_3 = 1 + 8 + 64 = 73$

The more interesting quantity is how much paper is left. Take the first square's area to be $1$. At each step the square is cut into nine and one ninth is thrown away, so eight ninths of the area survives — and that happens at every step, to whatever is left.

$$\text{area at step } n \;=\; \left(\frac89\right)^{\! n}.$$

That is Chapter 1's compounding, downwards: a fixed fraction of what remains goes at every step. And it means the carpet has an area of nothing at all. Eight ninths of eight ninths of eight ninths, taken for ever, shrinks below any figure you care to name. At step $60$ it is under a thousandth, and at step $120$ under a millionth. Yet the eight-copies rule keeps the pattern visible however far you look in.

Example 1

A Sierpiński carpet is begun from a square of side $27$ cm. At step $3$, how big is each remaining square, how many are there, and what area is left?

Solution.

Step 1 side: $27 \to 9 \to 3 \to 1$ cm a third each step
Step 2 $R_3 = 8^3 = 512$ eight for each square
Step 3 $512 \times 1 = 512$ cm2 each square is 1 cm²
Answer 512 squares, each of side 1 cm, leaving 512 cm²

The formula agrees: $729 \times \left(\tfrac89\right)^3 = 729 \times \tfrac{512}{729} = 512$. Counting the little squares and taking eight ninths three times are the same calculation written two ways.
