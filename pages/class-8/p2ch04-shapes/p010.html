4.8The Shortest Way Across a Box

A net is not only for making boxes. It also answers a question that is very hard to think about on the box itself.

A beetle sits at one corner of a closed wooden box $9$ cm long, $5$ cm wide and $3$ cm high. A grain of sugar lies at the opposite corner. The beetle can walk on the outside of the box, but not through it or through the air. What is its shortest route?

On a flat floor, the shortest path between two points is a straight line. That is the only fact we need, because a net turns the outside of the box into a flat floor. A path on the box becomes a path of exactly the same length on the net, and a path on the net becomes one on the box. So we unfold the box, join the two points with a ruler, and fold it back up.

Chapter 2 finishes the job. The straight line is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose shorter sides are $9$ and $3 + 5 = 8$.

the theorem $d^2 = 9^2 + 8^2 = 81 + 64 = 145$
so $d = \sqrt{145} \approx 12.0$ cm

But we made a choice when we unfolded the box, and other choices give other answers. Go over the top and down the far end, and the triangle has sides $9 + 3 = 12$ and $5$. That gives $\sqrt{169} = 13$ cm. Go round the side first, and the sides are $9 + 5 = 14$ and $3$. That gives $\sqrt{205} \approx 14.3$ cm. All three are straight lines on their own nets, and all three are real routes on the box.
