Two solids, two rules, one number of edges

A prism has $3n$ edges and a pyramid $2n$, so the same edge count means different polygons.

the prism $3n = 30$, so $n = 10$ and it has $12$ faces
the pyramid $2n = 30$, so $n = 15$ and it has $16$ faces

Check both with faces $+$ vertices $-$ edges: $12 + 20 - 30 = 2$ and $16 + 16 - 30 = 2$. The pyramid needs a bigger base to reach the same number of edges, because each of its base vertices carries only two.

A cube unfolded

The rule from Section 4.8 names the winner before any arithmetic: set the longest dimension against the other two added together. On a cube all three are equal, so the pairing is $6$ against $6 + 6$.

the theorem $d^2 = 6^2 + 12^2 = 36 + 144 = 180$
so $d = \sqrt{180} \approx 13.4$ cm

Compare that with $18$ cm along three edges, and with the $\sqrt{108} \approx 10.4$ cm a fly could manage straight through the middle. The ant is stuck on the surface and pays about three centimetres for it.

Two views that leave a choice

A square pyramid does it: from above, the square base with the apex over its centre; from the front, a triangle. So does a triangular prism laid on a rectangular face, if that face is square — from above a square, from the end a triangle.

The two solids are quite different and share two of their three views, which is exactly the point of Section 4.9. The side views tell them apart: the pyramid gives a triangle, the prism a square.

A third answer is available if you are willing to be awkward about it. Stand a cube on the table and rest a triangular prism on top of it, with the prism's square face matching the cube's: from above you still see one square, and from the front a square with a triangle on it. Whether that counts depends on whether the front view was meant to be a triangle and nothing else — which is a fair question to ask of the question.
