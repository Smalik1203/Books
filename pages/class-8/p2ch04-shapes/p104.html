Three quarters, five times

Every step keeps three quarters of the area, so five steps keep $\left(\tfrac34\right)^5$ of it — not five times a quarter off, which is the slip this question is watching for.

area $\left(\tfrac34\right)^5 = \dfrac{243}{1024}$
as a decimal about $0.24$

So about a quarter of the triangle is left — comfortably more than a tenth. Compounding downwards is slower than it feels: five steps of losing a quarter leave far more than nothing.

The cubes you cannot see

There are $3^3 = 27$ small cubes. A cube is hidden only if it touches no face of the block, so peel one layer off every side: what is left is a $1 \times 1 \times 1$ block in the middle.

altogether $3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27$
hidden $1 \times 1 \times 1 = 1$

Just one. For a $4 \times 4 \times 4$ block the same argument gives $2^3 = 8$ hidden, and for $n \times n \times n$ it gives $(n-2)^3$ — a formula that came from peeling rather than from counting.

Six faces in three pairs

A cuboid's net is six rectangles, and they come in matching pairs — the two ends, the two sides, the top and the bottom. So the surface area is twice the sum of three different rectangles, and the net is really only three shapes drawn twice.

the three $2 \times 3$, $\ 3 \times 4$, $\ 4 \times 2$
so $2(6 + 12 + 8) = 52$ cm2

That is Part I's formula $2(lb + bh + hl)$, and drawing the net is where it comes from. Anyone who has laid the six rectangles out flat never has to remember the formula again.

The net also answers a question the formula cannot: how big a sheet of card is needed to make the box. That is not $52$ cm2, because the six rectangles have to be cut from one piece and the corners of the sheet go to waste. How much waste depends on which of the nets you choose, which is a good reason for a packaging designer to know all of them.
