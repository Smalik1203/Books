Why the other options are wrong

Set A, 2. Option (b), $16$, doubles the count at each step and option (d), $24$, trebles the eight. Neither is what the construction does: every remaining square becomes eight, so the count is multiplied by eight — $8$, then $64$, then $512$.

Set B, 3. Option (a), $\tfrac34$, is the area after one step, and option (b) is the same number written untidily. Two steps means three quarters taken twice: $\tfrac34 \times \tfrac34 = \tfrac{9}{16}$.

Set B, 5. Option (a), $6$, belongs to a prism with $18$ edges and option (b), $12$, to one with $36$. The rule is $3n$: each end polygon contributes $n$ edges and the joins between them contribute $n$ more, so $24$ edges means $n = 8$.

Set B, 9. Option (b), $\sqrt{193}$, is the answer to the $12 \times 4 \times 3$ box in the chapter, not to this one. The rule is the same but the numbers are not: here the pairing is $9$ against $4 + 3$, giving $81 + 49 = 130$.

Set B, 12. Option (a), angles, is the tempting one, because an isometric drawing looks so orderly. It is not true — a cube's right angles come out as $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ on the paper. What survives is length, which is why the drawing can be measured.

Set C, 6. Option (b), $\sqrt{200}$, is the diagonal of the $10 \times 10$ face, a route that does not reach the far corner at all. The three real unfoldings give $\sqrt{221}$, $\sqrt{221}$ again by symmetry, and $\sqrt{401}$ — and the balanced pairing $11$ and $10$ wins, as it should.

Set C, 9. A point is not a projection of a solid: looking down at any solid you see a flat region, never a single dot. The other three are all possible — a cone gives a circle, a square pyramid gives a square, and a tetrahedron standing on a face gives a triangle, and all three look like a triangle from the front.

Set C, 12. Options (b) and (c) each try to put the complexity somewhere: in the rule, or in the drawer's care. It is in neither. The rule is simple and the drawing is rough — the detail comes from feeding the rule its own output, again and again, which is the one idea the first half of this chapter was about.
