4.5Fractals People Built

Sierpiński and von Koch gave these shapes names and formulas, but builders and weavers were putting them into work a long time earlier.

The Kandariya Mahadev temple at Khajuraho, finished around 1025 CE, is the clearest example. Its tower is a mountain of stone whose outline is repeated in smaller towers set against it, and those carry smaller towers again, so that the whole silhouette appears at three or four scales at once. The same idea shapes temples at Madurai, Hampi, Rameswaram and Varanasi. A visitor sees the design of the building in every part of the building.

Fulani wedding blankets from Nigeria carry rows of diamonds with smaller diamonds inside them and smaller ones inside those. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher made the idea the whole subject of a print called Smaller and Smaller, in which the same lizard is repeated at scales that halve towards the centre until the drawing can go no further.

None of these is a fractal in the strict sense, because none of them goes on for ever — the stone runs out, and so does the thread and the ink. They are the first few steps, which is all anybody can ever draw of a fractal anyway.

Every fractal here was made by writing down one rule and obeying it over and over. Remove the middle ninth. Remove the middle quarter. Bump out the middle third.

Each rule is a sentence long, and no rule mentions the shape it produces. Where, then, does all that detail come from?

4.6Solids, and Their Flat Parts

A solid bounded by flat surfaces is described by three words. Its faces are those flat surfaces; its edges are the line segments where two faces meet; its vertices are the points where edges meet. A cube has six faces, twelve edges and eight vertices, and so has any cuboid.
