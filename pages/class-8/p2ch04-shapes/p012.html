What this chapter established
  1. A fractal is a shape built of smaller copies of itself. Ferns, trees, clouds and coastlines are rough ones; the three here are exact, and each comes from a single rule obeyed over and over.
  2. The Sierpiński carpet removes the middle ninth of every square: $8^n$ squares left at step $n$, and an area of $\left(\tfrac89\right)^n$ that falls below any figure you name. The Sierpiński triangle removes the middle quarter of every triangle: $3^n$ left, area $\left(\tfrac34\right)^n$.
  3. The Koch snowflake adds instead. Each side becomes four sides a third as long, giving $3 \times 4^n$ sides and a perimeter of $3\left(\tfrac43\right)^n$ — a boundary of endless length round an area that stays small.
  4. A solid with flat sides has faces, edges and vertices. An $n$-sided prism has $n+2$, $3n$ and $2n$ of them; an $n$-sided pyramid has $n+1$, $2n$ and $n+1$. In both, faces $+$ vertices $-$ edges $= 2$.
  5. A net is a solid unfolded flat. Most solids have several; a cube has eleven.
  6. Because a net turns a surface into a flat sheet, the shortest path across a solid is a straight line on some net. Comparing the few unfoldings gives the answer, and the winner adds the two smaller dimensions together — for a $12 \times 4 \times 3$ box, $\sqrt{193}$ cm.
  7. A projection is a solid seen flat from one direction, and one is never enough. The front, top and side views together fix the shape, and lengths on them are true lengths.
  8. Seen corner-on, a cube's twelve edges all come out equal — an isometric projection, drawn as a hexagon of three rhombuses, and the basis of isometric paper.
