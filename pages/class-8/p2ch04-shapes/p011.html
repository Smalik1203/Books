4.10Drawing a Solid Whole

Three views are exact and hard to picture. There is a way of drawing a solid in one picture that keeps most of the measuring, and it comes from a question worth asking: is there a direction to look at a cube from that treats all three of its directions alike?

There is. Balance a cube on one corner and look straight down at it. Every one of its twelve edges is now slanted the same amount away from you, so every one is shortened by the same factor, and all twelve come out the same length on the paper. That is called an isometric projection — iso-metric, equal measure.

Isometric paper is ruled with exactly those three directions. Drawing on it is counting: so many units along the length axis, so many along the depth, so many up. Every edge you draw is the true length, which is why a shape drawn this way can be read off as easily as it was drawn.

Isometric drawing keeps the lengths and loses something else: it cannot tell you which corner is nearer. Stare at a wireframe cube on isometric paper long enough and it will flip, so that the face you took for the front becomes the back. Shading one face settles it, and that is why engineers shade.
