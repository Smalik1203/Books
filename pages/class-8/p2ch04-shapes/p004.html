Each side becomes four sides a third as long. So if $S_n$ is the number of sides and the first triangle has sides of length $1$,

$$S_n = 3 \times 4^{\,n}, \qquad \text{perimeter} = 3 \times 4^{\,n} \times \frac{1}{3^{\,n}} = 3 \left(\frac43\right)^{\! n}.$$
Example 2

A Koch snowflake is begun from a triangle of side $9$ cm. How many sides has it at step $2$, and what is its perimeter?

sides $3 \times 4^2 = 48$
each side $9 \div 3 \div 3 = 1$ cm
perimeter $48 \times 1 = 48$ cm

It started at $27$ cm, so the perimeter is nearly twice what it was after only two steps — and $27 \times \left(\tfrac43\right)^2 = 48$, as the formula says.

Now look at what those two formulas say together, because it is one of the strangest facts in school mathematics.

An endless edge round a small patch

The perimeter is multiplied by $\tfrac43$ at every step, so it grows without limit — past a hundred, past a million, past any number you name. The area does not: every bump added is smaller than the last, and the whole snowflake fits comfortably inside a circle drawn round the first triangle.

So the Koch snowflake has a boundary of infinite length enclosing a finite area. You could never walk its edge; you could paint its inside with one tin.
