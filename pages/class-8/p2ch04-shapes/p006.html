Every fractal here was made by writing down one rule and obeying it over and over: remove the middle ninth; remove the middle quarter; bump out the middle third.

Each rule is a sentence long, and no rule mentions the shape it produces. Where, then, does all that detail come from?

4.6Solids, and Their Flat Parts

A solid bounded by flat surfaces is described by three words. Its faces are those flat surfaces; its edges are the line segments where two faces meet; its vertices are the points where edges meet. A cube has six faces, twelve edges and eight vertices, and so has any cuboid.

Two families of solid are worth naming, because their counts follow a rule.

A prism has two congruent polygons as opposite faces and parallelograms all round the sides. A pyramid has one polygon as its base and a point above it, joined to every vertex of the base. Both are named after that polygon: a triangular prism, a hexagonal pyramid. A triangular pyramid has another name too — a tetrahedron, four faces of which any one can be called the base.

Count the parts of a prism whose ends are $n$-sided and a pattern appears at once. There are two end faces and $n$ side faces; the $n$ vertices of each end make $2n$ in all; and the edges are the $n$ round each end plus the $n$ joining them.
