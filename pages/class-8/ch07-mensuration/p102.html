A well $7$ m across is dug $10$ m deep, and the earth taken out is spread evenly over a field $22$ m by $14$ m. How deep is the layer? Take $\pi = \tfrac{22}{7}$.

The well is a cylinder, and $7$ m across means a radius of $3.5$ m. The earth taken out fills that cylinder; spread over the field it becomes a very flat cuboid of the same volume.

the well $\tfrac{22}{7} \times 3.5^2 \times 10 = 385$ m3
the field $22 \times 14 = 308$ m2
depth $385 \div 308 = 1.25$ m

The diameter given rather than the radius is the trap, and it is set on purpose: using $7$ as the radius gives four times the volume and an answer of $5$ m, which no one would notice was wrong.

A stone is dropped into a tank $50$ cm by $40$ cm holding water, and the level rises by $2$ cm. Find the volume of the stone.

A stone has no length, breadth or height to measure. What it has is the water it pushed aside, and that water went somewhere — upwards, over the whole floor of the tank.

the extra layer $50 \times 40 \times 2$
so the stone $4000$ cm3

The layer is $2$ cm deep across the whole tank, not just where the stone is. Notice too what the question never needed: how deep the water was, and what shape the stone had.

The last three are questions where a volume is moved rather than measured — the wax, the well and the stone. In every case the trick is the same and worth saying once: the volume does not change when the shape does. Melting, digging, pouring and displacing all keep the amount of stuff the same, so the volume you work out at one end of the question is the volume you use at the other.

The stone is the only one where the volume is not measured directly at all. You cannot put a ruler to a stone. What you can measure is the water it pushed out of the way, and that is exactly as much as the stone.
