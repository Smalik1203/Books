The third is the second cut in half, and it is worth saying exactly which height is meant: the perpendicular height, measured square to the base, and not the length of a slanting side. A triangle leaning far over has the same area as one standing upright, provided the base and the perpendicular height are the same.

Neither route is the correct one. Choose whichever leaves you with the fewest pieces, and check the two agree if you have the time — an area found twice by different cuts is an area you can trust.

7.3The Trapezium

A trapezium has one pair of parallel sides — call them $a$ and $b$ — and a perpendicular height $h$ between them. It is the first figure whose area is not obvious, and there are two ways to see it. The second is the one worth keeping.

The first way is to cut. Drop a perpendicular from each end of the shorter parallel side and the trapezium falls into a rectangle with a triangle on each side. Add the three areas and, after some algebra, the answer appears.

The second way needs no cutting at all. Take a second copy of the trapezium, turn it upside down, and set it beside the first. The two fit together exactly — the slanting sides match because they are the same slant — and what they make is a parallelogram.
