The question of the three tins has an answer that may not be the one you expect. Work out all three surface areas before deciding what you think.

Each tin holds $616$ cm3. The surface areas are not alike at all.

$r = 3.5$, $h = 16$ $2 \times \tfrac{22}{7} \times 3.5 \times 19.5 = 429$ cm2
$r = 7$, $h = 4$ $2 \times \tfrac{22}{7} \times 7 \times 11 = 484$ cm2
$r = 14$, $h = 1$ $2 \times \tfrac{22}{7} \times 14 \times 15 = 1320$ cm2

The tallest of the three uses least, and the flattest more than three times as much for the same contents. Taller is not always better, though: a tin of radius $2$ cm would have to be $49$ cm tall and would need about $641$ cm2. The least metal goes on a tin that is neither very flat nor very thin. A tall thin packet is usually a decision about shelves rather than about metal.

Solved Examples

Type 1 · Area by cutting up

Example 1

A rectangular garden is $20$ m long and $15$ m broad. A path $2$ m wide runs all round it, on the inside. Find the area of the path.

Solution.

Step 1 $20 \times 15 = 300$ m2 the whole garden
Step 2 $(20 - 4) \times (15 - 4) = 16 \times 11 = 176$ m2 the part inside the path
Step 3 $300 - 176 = 124$ take it away
Answer 124 m2

The path takes $2$ m off each end, so each measurement loses $4$ m, not $2$ m.

Example 2

A wall is $5$ m long and $3$ m high. It has a door $1$ m by $2$ m and a window $1.5$ m by $1$ m. Find the area of the wall to be painted.

Solution.

Step 1 $5 \times 3 = 15$ m2 the whole wall
Step 2 $1 \times 2 = 2$ and $1.5 \times 1 = 1.5$ the door and window
Step 3 $15 - 2 - 1.5 = 11.5$ take both away
Answer 11.5 m2
