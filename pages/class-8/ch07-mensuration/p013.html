7.8Why the Two Do Not Grow Together

Take a cube and double every edge. What happens to its skin, and what happens to the space inside it? The two answers are different, and the difference explains more about the world than anything else in this chapter.

Each face was $1 \times 1$ and is now $2 \times 2$, so every face is four times as large and so is the whole surface. The inside took one cube and now takes eight. The lengths went up by a factor of $2$; the areas by $2^2$; the volumes by $2^3$. Those exponents are Chapter 2's, and they are not a coincidence — an area is two lengths multiplied and a volume is three.

lengths $\times k$
areas $\times k^2$
volumes $\times k^3$

Because volume outruns area, a thing that gets bigger gets bulkier relative to its skin, and a good deal follows from that.

A large cake takes far longer to bake than a small one, and not merely a little longer: the heat comes in through the surface and has to reach a volume that has grown faster. Crushed ice cools a drink faster than one block of the same weight, because breaking it up changes nothing about the volume and multiplies the surface. A mouse must eat several times its own weight in a week and an elephant need not, because a mouse loses heat through a skin that is enormous for the body behind it. And an elephant's legs are not a mouse's legs scaled up — its weight went up with the cube of its size while the strength of a leg goes up with the area of its cross-section, which is only the square.

A model made to a scale of $1 : 50$ has lengths fifty times smaller, areas $50^2 = 2500$ times smaller, and volumes $50^3 = 125\,000$ times smaller. A model car that is a hand's width long weighs a hundred-thousandth of the real thing, which is why it can be lifted with one finger.
