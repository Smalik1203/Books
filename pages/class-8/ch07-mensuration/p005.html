7.4.1 The rhombus, from the same rule

A rhombus is a quadrilateral, so the rule applies — and because of what Chapter 5 proved about its diagonals, it collapses into something much shorter.

Take one diagonal as the base, of length $d_1$. The other diagonal is perpendicular to it and is cut in half by it, so the two vertices off the base each stand exactly $\tfrac12 d_2$ away. Put that into the rule.

general rule $\tfrac12 d_1 (h_1 + h_2)$
here $h_1 = h_2 = \tfrac12 d_2$
so $\tfrac12 d_1 \big(\tfrac12 d_2 + \tfrac12 d_2\big) = \tfrac12 d_1 d_2$
$$\text{area of a rhombus} = \tfrac12 d_1 d_2$$

It is not a new formula so much as the old one with what is known about a rhombus put into it. The same is true of a square, whose diagonals are also perpendicular and equal: its area is $\tfrac12 d^2$, which is a genuinely useful thing to know when the diagonal is given and the side is not.

Example 3

A quadrilateral has a diagonal of $18$ cm, and the perpendiculars onto it from the other two vertices are $6$ cm and $9$ cm. Find its area.

Solution.

Step 1 $\tfrac12 \times 18 \times (6 + 9)$ half the diagonal, both heights
Step 2 $9 \times 15 = 135$
Answer 135 cm2

Where a shape is not one of these at all, go back to the only method there is: cut it into pieces you know. A field shaped like a house — a rectangle with a triangle on top — is a rectangle plus a triangle, and there is nothing more to it.
