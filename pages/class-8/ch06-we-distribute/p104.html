outer pair $n(n + 3) = n^2 + 3n$
inner pair $(n + 1)(n + 2) = n^2 + 3n + 2$

The two products differ by exactly $2$. Write $m$ for $n^2 + 3n$. The product of all four is $m(m + 2)$, and adding $1$ gives $m^2 + 2m + 1$, which is $(m + 1)^2$, that is, $(n^2 + 3n + 1)^2$. Check it at $n = 3$: $9 + 9 + 1 = 19$, and $19^2 = 361$. Paired the other way, the two products differ by $3$, and nothing tidy happens.

Two of these questions, the one about $x^2 - 6x + 10$ and the one about four consecutive numbers, ask you to show that something holds for every value, and no amount of trying numbers settles that. In both, the argument was an identity: a square that is never negative, and a product written as $m(m + 2)$. In the others, the numbers given were exactly the pieces of an identity, and nothing else was needed.

Example 1

The product $36 \times 45$ is changed to $37 \times 44$. How does the product change?

  1. it increases by 10
  2. it increases by 8
  3. it decreases by 8
  4. it does not change

Solution.

Step 1One number goes up by 1 and the other comes down by 1: $(a + 1)(b - 1) = ab + b - a - 1$, with $a = 36$ and $b = 45$.
Step 2The change is $45 - 36 - 1 = 8$, and it is positive, so the product increases by 8.
Answer(b) it increases by 8
Example 2

If $x + y = 8$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 34$, what is $xy$?

  1. 15
  2. 30
  3. 17
  4. 13

Solution.

Step 1$(x + y)^2 = x^2 + y^2 + 2xy$, so $64 = 34 + 2xy$.
Step 2$2xy = 30$ and $xy = 15$.
Check$3 + 5 = 8$, $\;9 + 25 = 34$ and $3 \times 5 = 15$.
Answer(a) 15
