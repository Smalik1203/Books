6.2Special Cases of the Distributive Property

6.2.1 The square of a sum or a difference

A square of side $60$ units has an area of $60^2 = 3600$ square units, and a square of side $5$ units has an area of $5^2 = 25$ square units. Can we use these two to find the area of a square of side $65$ units?

A square of side $65$ can be cut into four parts, as in Fig. 6.4: a square of side $60$, a square of side $5$, and two rectangles with sides $60$ and $5$. The area of the whole square is the sum of the areas of its four parts. Can you find the area of each part?

Adding the four areas gives

$65^2$ $= (60 + 5)^2 = 60^2 + 5^2 + 2 \times (60 \times 5)$
$= 3600 + 25 + 600 = 4225$ square units

Now multiply $(60 + 5) \times (60 + 5)$ using the distributive property:

$(60 + 5) \times (60 + 5)$ $= 60 \times 60 + 5 \times 60 + 60 \times 5 + 5 \times 5$
$= 60^2 + 2 \times (60 \times 5) + 5^2$

The four products are the four parts of the square. What happens if we write $65^2$ as $(30 + 35)^2$ or as $(52 + 13)^2$ instead? Draw the figures and check the area you get.
