Example 3

Expand $(x - 2)(x^2 + 3x - 1)$.

Solution.

Step 1 $(x - 2)(x^2 + 3x - 1) = x(x^2 + 3x - 1) - 2(x^2 + 3x - 1)$
Step 2 $= x^3 + 3x^2 - x - 2x^2 - 6x + 2$
Step 3 $3x^2 - 2x^2 = x^2$,   $-x - 6x = -7x$ like terms
Check At $x = 1$: $(1 - 2) \times (1 + 3 - 1) = -3$, and $1 + 1 - 7 + 2 = -3$.
Answer $(x - 2)(x^2 + 3x - 1) = x^3 + x^2 - 7x + 2$

In Step 2, why is $x \times x^2 = x^3$? Write out what each side means: $x \times x^2$ is $x \times (x \times x)$, which is three $x$s multiplied together.

Multiplying expressions
  • Multiply every term of one bracket by every term of the other.
  • Then add the like terms. Terms that are not alike stay apart.

A pinch of history

Mathematicians in many old civilisations used the distributive property in their calculations without stating it, among them those of Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, China and India. Euclid used it in geometry, and Āryabhaṭa in algebra. The first known statement of it in words is by Brahmagupta, in Verse 12.55 of his Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE). He called it khaṇḍa-guṇanam, multiplication by parts: break the multiplier into parts that add up to it, multiply by each part, and add the results. With two parts, that is $(a + b)c = ac + bc$. His next verse, 12.56, gives quick ways of multiplying that use the same property. We look at some of them next.
