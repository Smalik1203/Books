Can we use $60^2 = 3600$ and $5^2 = 25$ to find $(60 - 5)^2$, that is, $55^2$? Think of it in geometry: draw a square of side $55$ sitting inside a square of side $60$, as in Fig. 6.6. The area of the square of side $55$ is $(60 - 5)^2 = 55^2$.

Start from the square of side $60$ and take away the two rectangles with sides $60$ and $5$: that is $60^2 - (60 \times 5) - (5 \times 60)$. But the two rectangles overlap in the small square of side $5$, so this takes that small square away twice. What can we do to the expression to get the right area?

Add the area of the small square back once. Then it has been taken away only once, as it should be.

$(60 - 5)^2$ $= 60^2 - (60 \times 5) - (5 \times 60) + 5^2$
$= 3600 - 300 - 300 + 25 = 3025$

The area of the square of side $55$ is $3025$ square units.

We have seen what $(a + b)^2$ gives when it is expanded. What is the expansion of $(a - b)^2$? Using the distributive property,

$(a - b)^2$ $= (a - b) \times (a - b)$
$= (a)^2 - ba - ab + (b)^2$
$= a^2 - 2ab + b^2$

We can also get it from the expansion of $(a + b)^2$. Think how, before reading on. The hint is that $(a - b)^2 = \big(a + (-b)\big)^2$, so the expansion of $(a + b)^2$ can be used directly, with $-b$ in place of $b$:

$\big(a + (-b)\big)^2 = (a)^2 + (-b)^2 + 2 \times (a) \times (-b)$
