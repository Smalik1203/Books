triangle $180 - 360 \div 3 = 60$
hexagon $180 - 360 \div 6 = 120$
left over $360 - 60 - 120 = 180$
each of the two $180 \div 2 = 90$

An angle of $90^\circ$ leaves an exterior angle of $90^\circ$, and $360 \div 90 = 4$, so the third polygon is the square. Taken round the point in the order triangle, square, hexagon, square, the angles are $60 + 90 + 120 + 90 = 360$, and that pattern, repeated, can cover a whole floor.

For how many values of $n$ is each interior angle of a regular $n$-gon a whole number of degrees?

Each interior angle is $180^\circ - \dfrac{360^\circ}{n}$. The $180$ is already whole, so the whole question is when $\dfrac{360}{n}$ is — that is, when $n$ divides $360$.

in pairs $1 \times 360,\ 2 \times 180,\ 3 \times 120,\ 4 \times 90,\ 5 \times 72,\ 6 \times 60$
$8 \times 45,\ 9 \times 40,\ 10 \times 36,\ 12 \times 30,\ 15 \times 24,\ 18 \times 20$
divisors $12$ pairs, so $24$ of them
drop $n = 1, 2$ $22$ values

Numbers such as $7$ and $16$ are missing because they do not divide $360$. Only $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ have to go, since no polygon has fewer than three sides.

Listing the divisors in pairs is the move worth keeping. Each small divisor brings a large partner, and the list is finished when the two meet — after $18 \times 20$ the next divisor is $20$, already there — so none is missed and none is counted twice.

The fourth and fifth questions are the two that catch people out, and for opposite reasons. The fourth looks as though both halves must have the same answer, and they do not. The fifth looks as though the proof has broken, and it has not — one diagonal fails, and a quadrilateral has two.

For the rest, the habit worth forming is to ask which of the two angle sums makes the question easier. The interior sum grows with $n$ and is the one to use when you are told a total. The exterior sum is fixed at $360^\circ$, so it is the one to use when you are told about a single angle of a regular figure, or when you need to know whether a figure can exist at all.
