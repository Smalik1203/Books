triangle $ABC$ $\angle B + \angle BAC + \angle BCA = 180^\circ$
triangle $ACD$ $\angle D + \angle DAC + \angle DCA = 180^\circ$
add them $\angle A + \angle B + \angle C + \angle D = 360^\circ$

This argument did not use the lengths of the sides or the sizes of the angles. So it works for every quadrilateral. That is why the four strips of card always give $360^\circ$, however far they lean.

4.3.2 Any number of sides

The same idea works for a polygon with more sides. Choose one vertex and draw every diagonal from it. The polygon is cut into triangles that all meet at that vertex. The angle at the chosen vertex is split into parts, one part in each triangle. The angles at the two vertices next to it each belong to one triangle. Every other angle is split into two parts. So, again, the angles of the triangles together make up the angles of the polygon, with nothing left over.

An $n$-gon is cut into $n - 2$ triangles, and each triangle has angles adding to $180^\circ$.

$$\text{angle sum of an } n\text{-gon} = (n-2) \times 180^\circ$$

Check it for shapes you know. A triangle gives $(3 - 2) \times 180^\circ = 180^\circ$, and a quadrilateral gives $2 \times 180^\circ = 360^\circ$.
