Quadrilaterals

4.1Introduction

Take four strips of card and punch a hole near each end. Join them into a ring with four pins. You now have a quadrilateral. Push one corner and the whole shape leans. The sides keep their lengths, but the shape changes. A square becomes a rhombus, and it can go on flattening until the strips almost lie on top of one another.

Now do the same with three strips. This time nothing moves. A triangle made of three pinned strips cannot be pushed into any other shape. This is why bridges, roofs and bicycle frames are built from triangles. It is also why a wooden gate often has a bar nailed across it from corner to corner. We come back to this at the end of the chapter.

One thing does not change when the quadrilateral leans. One angle opens and another closes, but if you measure all four angles and add them, you always get $360^\circ$. You get it before the push, after the push, and at every stage in between.

The chapter begins with shapes of any number of sides, because the reason for $360^\circ$ works for all of them. Then it turns to the quadrilaterals that have names: the trapezium, the kite, the parallelogram, the rhombus, the rectangle and the square. These are not six separate shapes. A square is a special rectangle, and a rectangle is a special parallelogram. So a fact proved for every parallelogram is true for every square as well.

What this chapter is about
  • The four angles of any quadrilateral add to $360^\circ$.
  • The named quadrilaterals form one family, and each shares the properties of those above it.
