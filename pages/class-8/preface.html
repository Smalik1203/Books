Preface

A number can be looked at, not only counted with. Nine dots make a square and eight make a cube; a rectangle cut in two says why multiplying distributes over adding; a quadrilateral holds its angle sum however it is pushed about. This book is built on that habit — draw the thing, and the rule stops being a rule to remember.

Each chapter states an idea, works through it beside the theory rather than after it, and ends with practice graded from the straightforward to the genuinely hard. Where a result can be settled in three lines, it is settled. Where it cannot be settled yet, the book says so, and says what would be needed.

Numbers were written down long before they were written down well. Egypt repeated a symbol until it had enough of them; Rome subtracted one letter from the next; the Maya counted in twenties and China in rods. What we use instead came from India — ten symbols, a place that means ten times the one beside it, and a zero to hold a place that is empty. Āryabhaṭa was setting problems with it by 499 CE, Brahmagupta gave zero its rules in 628, and the Bakhshali manuscript carries a dot for it that may be older than either. Fibonacci met the system in North Africa and spent a book persuading Europe to give up its counting board. A student who knows that arithmetic has a history is less likely to believe it fell from the sky.

Two things are worth knowing about how to read it. Do not skip the worked examples: they carry as much of the argument as the paragraphs around them. And when a question is put to you in a panel — Think and Reflect — stop and try it before reading on. The answer is usually a few lines further down, and it is worth much less once you have seen it.

Every chapter then goes ten pages further, in a section called Beyond the Book, which takes the same ideas somewhere new without adding a line to the syllabus. None of it is examinable. It is there because a subject met only in an examination is a subject that will not be remembered.
