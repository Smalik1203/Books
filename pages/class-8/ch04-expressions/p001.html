A Rectangle, Cut in Two

4.1Introduction

A rectangle is $a$ tall and $b + c$ wide. Its area is $a(b+c)$, because that is what area is: height times width. Now draw a line down it, separating the $b$ part from the $c$ part. Nothing has been added and nothing taken away, so the two pieces together must still be the whole thing — and the two pieces are rectangles of area $ab$ and $ac$.

$$a(b+c) = ab + ac$$

That is the distributive law, and the picture is the proof. You will use it more than any other rule in this chapter, and by the end of the book you will have used it more than any other rule in mathematics.

What is worth noticing straight away is how the picture proved it. Nobody was told what $a$, $b$ and $c$ are. The argument works for a rectangle $3$ by $5+2$ and for one $17$ by $200+1$, and it would work if somebody handed you three numbers tomorrow that nobody has thought of yet. A letter is not a mystery to be solved here. It is a number you have simply not been told.

You have been doing this for two years already, without the picture. When you write the perimeter of a square of side $s$ as $4s$, or the cost of $n$ pens at ₹$12$ each as $12n$, you are writing an expression: a recipe for a number, with the number itself left out. This chapter is about handling those recipes — taking them apart, adding them, multiplying them, and above all recognising when two that look quite different are the same recipe written twice.

What an expression is

An algebraic expression is numbers and letters combined by adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing. The letters are variables; they stand for numbers nobody has fixed yet. Fix them, and the expression becomes an ordinary number.
