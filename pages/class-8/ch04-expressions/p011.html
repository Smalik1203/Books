4.5Cut Both Ways

One cut across a rectangle gave the distributive law; cut it the other way as well and you have the rest of the chapter. A rectangle $(a+b)$ tall and $(c+d)$ wide, cut once each way, falls into four pieces, and their areas — $ac$, $ad$, $bc$ and $bd$ — are together the whole thing.

$$(a+b)(c+d) = ac + ad + bc + bd$$

Read the picture again as an instruction and it says: every term of the first bracket multiplies every term of the second. Two terms times two terms makes four products. Two times three would make six. If you ever finish an expansion with fewer products than that, you have missed one.

Counting the products before starting is worth the two seconds. Three terms times four terms must give twelve, so an answer built from ten of them is wrong before it is simplified. That is a far easier thing to notice than a single missing $ab$ buried in a line of algebra.

Example 6

Expand $(x + 4)(x + 7)$.

Two terms in each bracket, so four products.

Solution.

Step 1 $x \times x = x^2$ first times first
Step 2 $x \times 7 = 7x$ first times second
Step 3 $4 \times x = 4x$ second times first
Step 4 $4 \times 7 = 28$ second times second
Answer $x^2 + 11x + 28$

The two middle products were like terms and joined. They usually are, and that is why a product of two binomials so often has three terms rather than four.
