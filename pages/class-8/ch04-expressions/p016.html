4.6.3 A difference of two squares

The third result is the one that looks least likely and turns out to be used most. Start with a square of side $a$ and cut a square of side $b$ out of one corner. What is left has area $a^2 - b^2$, which is obvious and is not on its own very useful. What is useful is that the L-shaped piece left behind can be cut once and laid out again as a plain rectangle — and the two sides of that rectangle can be read straight off the drawing.

Check the two sides of the rectangle before believing it. The lower piece is $a$ long and $a - b$ tall. The upper piece is $a - b$ long and $b$ tall; stood on end it is $b$ wide and $a - b$ tall, so it matches the height exactly and adds $b$ to the width. The rectangle is therefore $(a+b)$ by $(a-b)$.

$$(a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - b^2$$

Expanding it confirms the picture in one line: $(a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - ab + ab - b^2$, and the two middle products cancel — they do so because one bracket has a plus where the other has a minus, which is the whole reason this product is short and the others are not. Here are the three together, worth reading aloud a few times; a result you can say is a result you will notice.

square of a sum $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$
square of a difference $(a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$
difference of squares $(a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - b^2$

The letters $a$ and $b$ stand for whole terms. In $(3x + 5y)^2$ the $a$ is $3x$ and the $b$ is $5y$, so $a^2$ is $9x^2$ and $2ab$ is $30xy$. Writing down what $a$ and $b$ are before starting is the difference between using an identity and misremembering one.
