6 The middle term is $2 \times 9 \times x = 18x$. (b) leaves out the two rectangles; (c) forgets to double; (d) doubles the $9$ in the last term as well.
9 $38 \times 42 = (40 - 2)(40 + 2) = 1600 - 4$. (a) forgets the $4$; (b) adds it; (c) takes away $14$.
11 $a^2 + b^2 = (a + b)^2 - 2ab = 25 - 12$. (a) stops at $(a + b)^2$; (b) takes away $ab$ once; (d) adds $2ab$ instead of taking it away.
12 $(x - y)^2 = x^2 + y^2 - 2xy = 29 - 20$. (a) is $(x + y)^2$; (b) takes away $xy$ once; (c) is $x - y$, not its square.
13 $x^2 - 8x + 19 = (x - 4)^2 + 3$, and a square is never negative, so the smallest value is $3$, at $x = 4$. (c) is that $x$, not the value; (d) is the value at $x = 0$.
15 The last term is $5k = -15$, so $k = -3$; then $5 + k = 2$ agrees. (a) drops the sign; (c) takes the last term itself for $k$; (d) takes the middle coefficient.
17 Both are true, but $999 \times 1001 = 1000^2 - 1$ is the difference of two squares, not the square of a sum. So the answer is (b).
18 The square is on the whole term, coefficient included: $(3x)^2 = 3x \times 3x$. So A is true and R is false, and the answer is (c).

The mathematics of this chapter fits on one line: every term of one bracket multiplies every term of the other, and three particular cases of that are worth knowing without working them out. Everything else — the terms, the degrees, the names — was vocabulary for saying it.

Three habits are worth more than the results. The first is to look at what you have been given before deciding what to do: a question that hands you $a+b$ and $ab$ has handed you the two pieces of a square, and is not asking for $a$ and $b$. The second is to notice when an expression is nearly a square and make it into one, as $x^2 - 6x + 10$ became $(x-3)^2 + 1$. Once a square is visible, the fact that a square is never negative settles questions that no amount of trying numbers could. The third is the cheapest of the three — put a number in and see whether both sides agree. It cannot prove an expansion right, and it catches almost every slip.

There is also something to notice about the pictures. Every identity here was argued from an area, and an area cannot be negative, so strictly the drawings only cover the case where all the letters are positive lengths. The algebra covers every case. That is not a flaw in the pictures. It is the moment algebra starts outrunning the diagram that suggested it — and from here on, that is mostly what it does.
