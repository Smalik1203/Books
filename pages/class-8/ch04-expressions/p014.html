4.6Three Results Worth Knowing

Everything from here on is the last section applied to three particular cases. They are not new mathematics, since you could expand any of them from Fig. 4.2 in half a minute. They are worth learning by heart anyway, because they turn up so often that recognising one is faster than doing it.

A statement that is true for every value of its letters is called an identity. That is a stronger claim than an equation like $2x = 6$, which is true for one value of $x$ and false for all the others. The three results below are identities, and the pictures are what make them so: an area argument never asks what $a$ and $b$ happen to be.

All three could be got from § 4.5 by multiplying out, and none of them is a new rule. They are worth learning by name anyway, for the reason that a much-used result always is: recognising one on sight saves the multiplying, and — more usefully — lets you run it backwards, which multiplying out will never do.

4.6.1 The square of a sum

Take a square of side $a + b$ and cut it once each way, at the point where $a$ ends. The four pieces are a square $a$ by $a$, two rectangles $a$ by $b$, and a square $b$ by $b$.

$$(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$
