What this chapter established
  1. An expression is a recipe for a number with the number left out, and its letters are variables. It breaks at its $+$ and $-$ signs into terms, each keeping the sign in front of it; a term's numerical factor is its coefficient and its degree is the sum of the powers of its letters.
  2. Expressions are named by their number of terms. A polynomial is one in which every power is a whole number, so nothing sits in a denominator or under a root.
  3. Only like terms — those whose letter parts match exactly — can be gathered, and gathering them is the distributive law read backwards: $5xy - 2xy = (5-2)xy$.
  4. Subtracting a bracket reverses the sign of every term inside it. To multiply term by term, multiply the coefficients and add the exponents; the degrees add too, which is a free check.
  5. The distributive law $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ is one rectangle's area counted two ways. Cut the rectangle both ways and every term of one bracket multiplies every term of the other — two terms times three give six products.
  6. An identity holds for every value of its letters; an equation only for particular ones. The three to know: $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$,   $(a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$,   $(a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - b^2$.
  7. Each is a picture: a square cut into four, a square with two overlapping strips taken off, a cut corner rearranged into a rectangle. $(a+b)^2$ is not $a^2 + b^2$, and Fig. 4.3 shows what is missing. The letters stand for whole terms, so in $(3x + 5y)^2$ the $a$ is $3x$.
  8. $(x+a)(x+b) = x^2 + (a+b)x + ab$: the middle coefficient is the sum of the two numbers, the last term their product.
  9. Read backwards, the identities turn sums into products: $x^2 + 10x + 25 = (x+5)^2$ and $m^2 - 81 = (m+9)(m-9)$. Read as arithmetic, they give $103 \times 97 = 10000 - 9$, and $a^2 + b^2$ from $a+b$ and $ab$ without ever finding $a$ and $b$.
