The identities also run backwards. If an expression is $a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ for some $a$ and $b$, it can be written as $(a+b)^2$ — and a product is far easier to work with than a sum. That is the whole of Class 9's work on factorisation, and it begins with noticing that $x^2 + 10x + 25$ has a first term that is a square, a last term that is a square, and a middle term that is twice the product of their roots.

$x^2 + 10x + 25$ $= x^2 + 2(x)(5) + 5^2 = (x+5)^2$
$m^2 - 81$ $= m^2 - 9^2 = (m+9)(m-9)$

Not every trinomial is a square, and checking the middle term is what tells you: $x^2 + 9x + 25$ has the same two squares at its ends, but $9$ is not $2 \times 1 \times 5$, so it is not $(x+5)^2$ and there is nothing to be done with it here.
