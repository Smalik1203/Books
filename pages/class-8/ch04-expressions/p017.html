Example 8

Expand $(3x + 5y)^2$.

here $a = 3x$,   $b = 5y$
$a^2$ $= (3x)^2 = 9x^2$
$2ab$ $= 2 \times 3x \times 5y = 30xy$
$b^2$ $= (5y)^2 = 25y^2$
answer $9x^2 + 30xy + 25y^2$

Note $(3x)^2 = 9x^2$ and not $3x^2$: the square is on the whole term, coefficient included.

Nothing changes when the sign does. Take $a = 7p$ and $b = 2q$, so that $a^2 = 49p^2$ and $b^2 = 4q^2$ in every one of the three results, and only the middle term can differ.

$(7p - 2q)^2$ $= 49p^2 - 28pq + 4q^2$
$(7p - 2q)(7p + 2q)$ $= 49p^2 - 4q^2$

The second has no middle term at all, and that is the whole point of it: two brackets differing in one sign give two terms, not three.

The identities also run backwards. If an expression is $a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ for some $a$ and $b$, it can be written as $(a+b)^2$ — and a product is far easier to work with than a sum. That is the whole of Class 9's work on factorisation, and it begins with noticing that $x^2 + 10x + 25$ has a first term that is a square, a last term that is a square, and a middle term that is twice the product of their roots.

$x^2 + 10x + 25$ $= x^2 + 2(x)(5) + 5^2 = (x+5)^2$
$m^2 - 81$ $= m^2 - 9^2 = (m+9)(m-9)$

Not every trinomial is a square, and checking the middle term is what tells you: $x^2 + 9x + 25$ has the same two squares at its ends, but $9$ is not $2 \times 1 \times 5$, so it is not $(x+5)^2$ and there is nothing to be done with it here.

Reading an identity backwards like this is a different skill from reading it forwards, and a harder one. Forwards you are told what to do; backwards you must first recognise that there is anything to do at all, and the recognising is done on the shape of the expression rather than on its numbers.
