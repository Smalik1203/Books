C1 $\frac{2}{x}$ is $2x^{-1}$, and $-1$ is not a whole number. The plain number $5$ in (b) is a polynomial: it is $5x^0$, of degree nought.
C3 $n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1)$, so it is a product, and a product is prime only when one factor is $1$. That needs $n = 2$, giving $3$. Option (b) gives $24$ and (c) gives $80$; (d) misses that $n=2$ is the one case that survives.
C5 The last term is $3k$, and $3k = 18$ gives $k = 6$; the middle coefficient $3 + 6 = 9$ then confirms it. Option (d) reads the last term itself as $k$.
C6 The second statement is right. The first is not: the identity fails for most pairs but holds whenever $ab = 0$ — take $a = 0$, or $b = 0$. False for every pair is a stronger claim than not true for all pairs, and only the weaker one is correct.
C7 With $a = 2x$ and $b = 5$, the middle term is $2ab = 20x$. Imran multiplied the two without doubling, which is option (b)'s reasoning as well as its answer; (d) forgets that $(-5)^2$ is positive.
C9 $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab = 41 + 40 = 81$. Option (d) stops at $81$ without taking the square root; (c) adds $ab$ once rather than twice.
C10 A square is never negative, so $(x-4)^2$ qualifies. Each of the others is negative for some $x$: $x^2 - 4$ at $x = 0$, $x - 4$ at $x = 0$, and $4 - x^2$ at $x = 3$.
C11 $(a-b)^2 + 4ab = a^2 - 2ab + b^2 + 4ab = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$. Option (c) adds only two of the four $ab$s back; (b) assumes the $4ab$ does nothing at all.
C12 $x^2 - (x-2)^2 = (x + x - 2)(x - x + 2) = 2(2x-2)$. Option (a) is the square of the amount taken off, which is only the corner of Fig. 4.4; (c) forgets that the corner has been removed twice and put back once.
