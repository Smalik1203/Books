4.4Multiplying

Adding expressions asked whether two terms were alike. Multiplying never asks: any term can be multiplied by any other, and the answer is always a single term. That is why multiplication turns out to be the easier of the two operations, and also why it produces so much more.

4.4.1 One term by one term

Multiply the coefficients, and multiply the letters — which, since a repeated letter is a power, means adding the exponents. That last step is the index law $x^m \times x^n = x^{m+n}$ from Chapter 2, doing its work here for the first time.

$3x \times 5x$ $= (3 \times 5)(x \times x) = 15x^2$
$2a^2b \times 4ab^3$ $= (2 \times 4)(a^2 \times a)(b \times b^3) = 8a^3b^4$
$-6p \times \tfrac12 pq$ $= -3p^2q$

The degrees add too, and that is a check worth having: a term of degree $3$ times a term of degree $4$ must give degree $7$, whatever the letters are.

4.4.2 One term by a whole expression

Here is the rectangle from the first page, drawn properly. A rectangle $a$ tall and $b + c$ wide is cut by one vertical line into a rectangle $a$ by $b$ and a rectangle $a$ by $c$.

So to multiply an expression by a single term, multiply every term of it by that term. The picture only shows two pieces, but nothing stops you cutting the rectangle three times or ten, and the argument does not change.

$$a(b + c + d + \cdots) = ab + ac + ad + \cdots$$
