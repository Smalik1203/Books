Each interior angle is $180^\circ - \dfrac{360^\circ}{n}$. The $180$ is already whole, so the whole question is when $\dfrac{360}{n}$ is — that is, when $n$ divides $360$.

$360$ $= 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5$
divisors $4 \times 3 \times 2 = 24$ of them
drop $n = 1, 2$ $22$ values

The count of divisors comes from Chapter 2: each prime can appear from none of it up to all of it, which is $3+1$ choices for the $2$s, $2+1$ for the $3$s and $1+1$ for the $5$. Only $n = 1$ and $n = 2$ have to go, since no polygon has fewer than three sides.

Counting the divisors rather than listing them is the move worth keeping. A question that asks how many, rather than which, almost never wants the list — and the factorisation of the number is usually where the count is hiding.

The fourth and fifth questions are the two that catch people out, and for opposite reasons. The fourth looks as though both halves must have the same answer, and they do not. The fifth looks as though the proof has broken, and it has not — one diagonal fails, and a quadrilateral has two.

For the rest, the habit worth forming is to ask which of the two angle sums makes the question easier. The interior sum grows with $n$ and is the one to use when you are told a total. The exterior sum is fixed at $360^\circ$, so it is the one to use when you are told about a single angle of a regular figure, or when you need to know whether a figure can exist at all.

Type 1 · Polygons and their diagonals

Example 1

A polygon has $44$ diagonals. How many sides has it?

Solution.

Step 1 $\dfrac{n(n-3)}{2} = 44$ the diagonal count
Step 2 $n(n-3) = 88$ double both sides
Step 3 $88 = 11 \times 8$ two numbers three apart
Step 4 $\dfrac{11 \times 8}{2} = 44$ check
Answer $n = 11$: the polygon has 11 sides
