Using What You Know

Four facts came out of this chapter and nothing below needs a fifth. The interior angles of an $n$-gon add to $(n-2) \times 180^\circ$. The exterior angles add to $360^\circ$ whatever $n$ is. A diagonal turns a quadrilateral into two triangles. And the quadrilateral names are conditions stacked on one another.

What is different here is that several of the questions ask whether something is possible rather than what its value is, and a question of that kind is never answered by a measurement. Try each one before reading what follows it.

The last question has a short answer and a long way of reaching it. If you find yourself testing values of $n$ one at a time, stop and write down what has to divide what.

Find the interior angle sum of a $20$-gon using only the fact that the exterior angles add to $360^\circ$ — that is, without the $(n-2) \times 180^\circ$ formula.

At every vertex the two angles add to $180^\circ$, so a $20$-gon's forty angles come to $20 \times 180^\circ$ altogether.

all the angles $20 \times 180^\circ = 3600^\circ$
less the outside $3600^\circ - 360^\circ = 3240^\circ$

With $n$ for $20$ that reads $180n - 360 = (n-2) \times 180$ — the chapter's formula again, proved without a single diagonal.

Is there a polygon whose interior angles add to exactly the same total as its exterior angles? If so, which one; if not, say why not.

The exterior sum is $360^\circ$ and never changes, so the question is which $n$ makes the interior sum $360^\circ$ as well.

set equal $(n-2) \times 180 = 360$
divide $n - 2 = 2$,   $n = 4$

The quadrilateral, and only the quadrilateral. Below it the interior sum is smaller and above it larger, because the interior sum climbs by $180^\circ$ a side while the exterior sum stands still. So the two totals meet once, and they meet at four.
