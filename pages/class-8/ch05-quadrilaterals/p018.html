5.8Why Three Rods Hold and Four Do Not

Back to the strips of card at the start of the chapter. Three of them, pinned into a triangle, cannot be pushed into any other shape at all. Four of them can be pushed into infinitely many. The reason has nothing to do with the card, or with how tight the pins are, or with friction; a triangle of steel and a triangle of paper behave the same way. It is about how much three lengths decide, and how much four lengths do not.

Give somebody three lengths and ask them to build a triangle. There is only one triangle they can build — they may lay it down turned round or flipped over, but its angles are settled before they start. Give somebody four lengths and the question has no single answer: the four rods make a whole family of quadrilaterals, and pushing one corner walks you along that family.

That is also the cure, and it is the diagonal again. Nail one across a gate and the gate becomes two triangles; neither can change shape, so neither can the gate. Look at a roof truss, a pylon, a crane jib or the frame of a bicycle and you will find the same thing: they are built of triangles, or of quadrilaterals with a diagonal put in.

So the diagonal has done three jobs in this chapter. It cut the quadrilateral into two triangles and gave the angle sum. It made the congruent triangles that proved everything about the parallelogram. And it is what a builder nails across a frame to stop it leaning. They are the same fact seen three times — that a triangle is decided by its sides, and a quadrilateral is not.
