What this chapter established
  1. A polygon is a closed figure of straight sides that meet only at their ends. It has as many vertices as sides, and is named by that number.
  2. A polygon is convex when every segment joining two interior points stays inside it, and concave otherwise — which happens exactly when one of its angles exceeds $180^\circ$.
  3. A diagonal joins two vertices that are not neighbours. Each vertex reaches $n-3$ others, and every diagonal is counted twice, so a polygon has $\dfrac{n(n-3)}{2}$ of them.
  4. A polygon is regular when its sides are all equal and its angles are all equal. A rhombus fails the second test and a rectangle the first.
  5. One diagonal cuts a quadrilateral into two triangles, and their angles are exactly the quadrilateral's four. So the angles of a quadrilateral add to $360^\circ$ — whatever its shape, since the argument never mentions one.
  6. Fanning out from one vertex cuts an $n$-gon into $n-2$ triangles, so its angles add to $(n-2) \times 180^\circ$. Each angle of a regular $n$-gon is that total divided by $n$.
  7. An exterior angle is what a straight angle has left after the interior angle, so the two add to $180^\circ$ at every vertex.
  8. Walking once round any polygon turns you through one revolution, so the exterior angles add to $360^\circ$ however many sides there are. In a regular $n$-gon each is $\dfrac{360^\circ}{n}$ — which must therefore divide $360^\circ$ exactly, and most numbers do not.
  9. The quadrilaterals are a family of conditions, not a list of shapes. Every square is a rectangle and a rhombus; every rectangle and rhombus is a parallelogram; every parallelogram is a trapezium. A kite stands aside, meeting the parallelograms only at the rhombus.
  10. A parallelogram is defined by parallel sides alone, and gets three properties free: opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, adjacent angles adding to $180^\circ$. Its diagonals bisect each other.
  11. The extra property of each special parallelogram is about the diagonals: in a rhombus they cross at right angles, in a rectangle they are equal, in a square both. Each test runs backwards and identifies the figure.
  12. Three lengths settle a triangle; four do not settle a quadrilateral. That is why a frame of four rods leans and a frame of three does not, and why a diagonal braces a gate.
