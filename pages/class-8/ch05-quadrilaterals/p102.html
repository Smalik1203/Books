You have four rods, of lengths $3$, $4$, $5$ and $20$ centimetres. Show that they cannot be pinned into a quadrilateral, using nothing but the rule for three rods: each side of a triangle is shorter than the other two together.

Suppose they could. Call the quadrilateral $ABCD$, with the $20$ cm rod as $DA$, and draw the diagonal $BD$. It cuts the figure into two triangles, and the three-rod rule holds in each.

triangle $ABD$ $20 < AB + BD$
triangle $BCD$ $BD < BC + CD$
together $20 < AB + BC + CD = 3 + 4 + 5 = 12$

Twenty is not less than twelve, so no such quadrilateral exists, whichever way round the three short rods go. The diagonal did the work again: it turned a question about four rods into two questions about three.

A quadrilateral has three right angles. Must the fourth be one too? A pentagon has four right angles. Must the fifth be one?

The two halves look alike and are not, and the angle sum is what tells them apart.

quadrilateral $360 - 3 \times 90 = 90$
pentagon $540 - 4 \times 90 = 180$

So a quadrilateral with three right angles has no choice: the fourth is $90^\circ$ and the figure is a rectangle. A pentagon with four right angles needs its fifth angle to be $180^\circ$ — which is not an angle of a polygon at all, but a point sitting in the middle of a straight side. No such pentagon exists, and what you would draw if you tried is a rectangle with an extra dot marked on one edge.

The angle-sum proof used a diagonal that lay inside the figure. In a concave quadrilateral one diagonal falls outside. Show that the sum is still $360^\circ$.

A concave quadrilateral has one vertex whose angle is more than $180^\circ$. One of its diagonals falls outside the figure, and the proof cannot use it. But a quadrilateral has two diagonals, and the other one lies inside.

Use that one. It cuts the figure into two triangles exactly as before, the angles at its two ends are each divided into two pieces by it, and the angles of the two triangles are once again precisely the four angles of the quadrilateral.

two triangles $180^\circ + 180^\circ = 360^\circ$

Nothing else changes. What the concave case shows is that the proof needed a diagonal inside the figure, not any particular one — and every quadrilateral, concave or not, has at least one.
