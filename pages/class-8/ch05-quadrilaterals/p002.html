5.2Polygons

A polygon is a closed figure made of straight sides. Three conditions, all of them doing work: the sides are straight, so a circle is not one; the figure is closed, so a path that stops short is not one; and the sides meet only at their ends, so a figure whose sides cross each other is not one either.

The points where two sides meet are the vertices, and there are always exactly as many vertices as sides. Polygons are named by that number, and the names are Greek: gonia is a corner, so a pentagon has five of them.

Three sides is a triangle, four a quadrilateral, five a pentagon and six a hexagon; then seven a heptagon, eight an octagon, nine a nonagon and ten a decagon.

Past ten the names are more trouble than they are worth, and a figure with seventeen sides is called a $17$-gon by everybody, including the people who know the Greek for it.

5.2.1 Convex and concave

Polygons come in two kinds, and the difference matters for almost everything that follows. Pick any two points inside a polygon and join them. In a convex polygon the joining segment stays inside, wherever the two points were. In a concave one you can find two points whose segment leaves the figure and comes back.

Everything in this chapter is about convex polygons unless it says otherwise. That is not because the concave ones misbehave — most of the results survive — but because the pictures are easier to read, and a reader who has to keep checking whether a corner points inwards is not thinking about the mathematics.
