So add the two triangles' angle sums and you have added the quadrilateral's four angles and nothing else.

triangle $ABC$ $\angle B + \angle BAC + \angle BCA = 180^\circ$
triangle $ACD$ $\angle D + \angle DAC + \angle DCA = 180^\circ$
add them $\angle A + \angle B + \angle C + \angle D = 360^\circ$

Nowhere did that argument use the lengths of the sides, or where the corners were, or whether the figure was leaning. It used one diagonal and the triangle sum. That is why the four rods of the first page can be pushed into any shape they like and still add to $360^\circ$: the proof does not know they have moved.

5.3.2 Any number of sides

The same trick works on a bigger figure, and it is worth doing on paper before reading on. Choose one vertex and join it to every other vertex it is not already joined to. The polygon falls into a fan of triangles, all of them meeting at the vertex you chose. And as with the quadrilateral, the triangles' angles between them account for the polygon's own angles exactly — the chosen vertex's angle arrives in pieces, the two beside it whole, and the rest in two pieces each, with no angle used twice and none left over.
