5.2.2 Diagonals

A diagonal is a segment joining two vertices that are not next to each other. A triangle has none, because every pair of its vertices is already joined by a side. A quadrilateral has two, and they are the reason this chapter works at all: draw one diagonal and a quadrilateral becomes two triangles.

Counting them is a piece of Chapter 2's arithmetic. Each of the $n$ vertices can be joined to $n - 3$ others — every vertex except itself and the two beside it — which gives $n(n-3)$ joins. But each diagonal has been counted at both ends, so halve it.

$$\text{number of diagonals} = \frac{n(n-3)}{2}$$

For a quadrilateral that is $\dfrac{4 \times 1}{2} = 2$, and for a pentagon $\dfrac{5 \times 2}{2} = 5$, which is what Fig. 5.2 shows. A twenty-sided figure has $170$ of them, which is worth knowing before you try to draw one.

5.2.3 Regular polygons

A polygon is regular when all its sides are equal and all its angles are equal. Both conditions are needed, and it is easy to believe otherwise. A rhombus has four equal sides and is not regular; a rectangle has four equal angles and is not regular. Only when a quadrilateral has both is it a square.
