When the two sums agree

The exterior sum is $360^\circ$ and never changes, so the question is which $n$ makes the interior sum $360^\circ$ as well.

set equal $(n-2) \times 180 = 360$
divide $n - 2 = 2$,   $n = 4$

The quadrilateral, and only the quadrilateral. Below it the interior sum is smaller and above it larger, because the interior sum climbs by $180^\circ$ a side while the exterior sum stands still. So the two curves cross once, and they cross at four.

Four rods that will not close

Lay the three short rods end to end and they reach $3 + 4 + 5 = 12$ cm. The fourth rod is $20$ cm long, so even fully stretched the three cannot bring its far end back to its near end. The ring will not close.

three of them $3 + 4 + 5 = 12$
the fourth $20 > 12$

The rule, then, is that each length must be less than the sum of the other three — and it is enough to check the longest, since if the longest passes the test the others cannot fail it. It is the triangle inequality with one more rod, and the reason is the same: the short way between two points is the straight one.

Three right angles, and four

The two halves look alike and are not, and the angle sum is what tells them apart.

quadrilateral $360 - 3 \times 90 = 90$
pentagon $540 - 4 \times 90 = 180$

So a quadrilateral with three right angles has no choice: the fourth is $90^\circ$ and the figure is a rectangle. A pentagon with four right angles needs its fifth angle to be $180^\circ$ — which is not an angle of a polygon at all, but a point sitting in the middle of a straight side. No such pentagon exists, and what you would draw if you tried is a rectangle with an extra dot marked on one edge.
