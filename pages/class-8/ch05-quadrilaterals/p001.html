Four Sides, One Sum

5.1Introduction

Take four strips of card, punch a hole near each end, and join them into a ring with four pins. You now have a quadrilateral, and the first thing you will notice is that it does not hold still. Push one corner and the whole thing leans; the sides keep their lengths, but the shape does not keep anything at all. A square becomes a rhombus, and goes on flattening until the four rods nearly lie on top of one another.

Do the same with three strips and nothing happens. A triangle made of three pinned rods cannot be pushed into another triangle, which is why bridges and roofs and bicycle frames are made of triangles and gates have a diagonal nailed across them. That difference between three rods and four is the last section of this chapter, and it is worth carrying the question there.

Meanwhile, something in the pushed quadrilateral does not change. The four angles are all different from what they were — one has opened, another has closed — and yet if you measure all four and add them, you get $360^\circ$ before the push and $360^\circ$ after it, and $360^\circ$ at every stage in between.

That is the shape of the chapter. It opens with figures of any number of sides, because the argument that settles four settles all of them; it spends its middle on the quadrilaterals, which are the ones with names and a family; and it comes back at the end to the four pinned rods, and to why a gate has a diagonal nailed across it.

What this chapter is about

A quadrilateral has almost nothing fixed about it. The one thing it cannot escape is that its four angles add to $360^\circ$ — and the reason, once you see it, also gives the angle sum of a figure with five sides, or twelve, or a hundred.
