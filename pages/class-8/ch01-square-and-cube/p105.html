A side that is neither a whole number nor a fraction

The side is $\sqrt{2}$, and the first half is quick. If the side were a whole number $n$ then $n^2 = 2$, and $1^2 = 1$ is too small while $2^2 = 4$ is too big. There is no whole number between $1$ and $2$, so there is no such $n$.

The second half is the interesting one, and it is the pairing argument used once more. Suppose the side were a fraction $\tfrac{a}{b}$, written in lowest terms. Squaring gives

$\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^2 = 2$ so $a^2 = 2b^2$

Now count how many $2$s each side contains. On the left, $a^2$ is a square, so the prime $2$ appears in it an even number of times — that is exactly what being a square means. On the right, $b^2$ likewise has an even number of $2$s, and then one more $2$ is bolted on, making the count odd.

One number cannot contain the prime $2$ an even number of times and an odd number of times at the same moment. So no such fraction exists, and the side of that sheet of cardboard cannot be written as a fraction at all.

This is worth more than the question that produced it. Numbers of this kind are called irrational, and finding the first of them was a genuine shock — a length you can draw with a ruler, and cannot write down. You will meet them properly in a chapter of their own; the argument above is the whole proof, and you have just followed it.

Odd squares and the number eight

Check first, then explain. $1, 9, 25, 49, 81$ divided by $8$ leave $1, 1, 1, 1, 1$. It is not a coincidence and the reason is one line of algebra.

Every odd number can be written $2k + 1$. Square it:

$(2k+1)^2$ $= 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 4k(k+1) + 1$

Now $k$ and $k+1$ are consecutive, so one of them is even, so $k(k+1)$ is even. Write it as $2m$. Then the square is $8m + 1$ — a multiple of $8$, plus one.

The useful part is the test it gives you. A number that leaves any remainder but $1$ on division by $8$ cannot be an odd square: $2019$ leaves $3$, so it is out, and no factorisation was needed to say so.
