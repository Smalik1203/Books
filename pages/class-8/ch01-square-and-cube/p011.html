Take any number and break it into primes. Now square it: every prime that appeared once now appears twice, every prime that appeared three times now appears six. Squaring doubles how many times each prime appears — so the prime factorisation of a perfect square has every prime an even number of times, and the primes can be dealt out into two identical piles.

It is worth seeing that this is a rule about the primes and not about the number's appearance. Nothing on the outside of $1024$ suggests a square: it does not look like one, and no digit of it helps. But $1024 = 2^{10}$, ten $2$s, which deal into two piles of five, so it is $32^2$, and it could not have been anything else. Meanwhile $1000$ looks tidy and is not a square at all: $2^3 \times 5^3$ leaves an odd count of each. Once you can see inside a number, the way it looks no longer matters.

Example 4

Is $784$ a perfect square? If so, what is its square root?

Solution.

Step 1 $784 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 7 \times 7$ divide repeatedly
Step 2 $(2 \times 2)$ and $(2 \times 2)$ and $(7 \times 7)$ every prime pairs
Step 3 $784 = (2 \times 2 \times 7) \times (2 \times 2 \times 7) = 28^2$ two identical piles
Answer Yes. $\sqrt{784} = 28$

Notice that the method hands over the root as part of the answer. Take one member from each pair and multiply: $2 \times 2 \times 7 = 28$.

Example 5

Is $294$ a perfect square?

Solution.

Step 1 $294 = 2 \times 3 \times 7 \times 7$
Step 2 the two $7$s pair, but $2$ and $3$ are each alone no two identical piles
Answer No. $294$ is not a perfect square

The last digit would not have caught this one: $294$ ends in $4$, which is a good ending for a square. The factorisation settles it.

The unpaired primes are worth a close look, because they say exactly what is missing. In $294$ the strays are $2$ and $3$. Multiply by $2 \times 3 = 6$ and the result, $1764$, is a square: it is $42^2$. That turns a test into a construction.
