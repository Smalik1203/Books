Since it would be tiresome to write both every time, the symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{n}}$ is defined to mean the positive one:

$\sqrt{64} = 8, \qquad \sqrt{100} = 10, \qquad \sqrt{441} = 21$

When the negative root is wanted it is written $-\sqrt{64} = -8$, and when both are wanted, $\pm\sqrt{64} = \pm 8$. In this chapter we shall want the positive one throughout — a side length of $-8$ m is not a side length — so from here on the square root means the positive square root.

This is why $\sqrt{x^2}$ is not simply $x$. If $x = -5$ then $x^2 = 25$ and $\sqrt{25} = 5$, which is $-x$, not $x$. The square root symbol never returns a negative number.

So much for what a root is. Given a number such as $576$, two questions remain: is it a perfect square at all, and if it is, what is its root? We have met two methods already and they both work.

Listing. Square $20, 21, 22, \ldots$ until you reach $576$ or pass it: $400, 441, 484, 529, 576$. It works, and it is honest, and for a six-digit number it is hopeless.

Subtracting the odd numbers. From § 1.2.2 we know $576$ is a square exactly when subtracting $1, 3, 5, \ldots$ lands on zero. For $576$ that is twenty-four subtractions. Also honest, also slow.

The third method is the one to keep, and it comes from asking what a square looks like on the inside.

Take any number and break it into primes. Now square it: every prime that appeared once now appears twice, every prime that appeared three times now appears six. Squaring doubles every exponent — so the prime factorisation of a perfect square has every prime an even number of times, and the primes can be dealt out into two identical piles.

It is worth seeing that this is a rule about the primes and not about the number's appearance. Nothing on the outside of $1024$ suggests a square — it does not look like one, and no digit of it helps — but $1024 = 2^{10}$, ten $2$s, which deal into two piles of five, so it is $32^2$ and nothing else it could have been. Meanwhile $1000$ looks tidy and is not a square at all: $2^3 \times 5^3$ leaves an odd count of each. Once you can see inside a number, its appearance stops mattering.
