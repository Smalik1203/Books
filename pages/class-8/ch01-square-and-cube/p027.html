What this chapter established
  1. A square number is a number multiplied by itself, written $n^2$; the squares of the natural numbers are the perfect squares. A cube is a number multiplied by itself three times, written $n^3$.
  2. The squares are the only numbers with an odd count of factors, because every factor has a partner and only a square is its own partner.
  3. A perfect square ends in $0, 1, 4, 5, 6$ or $9$. Ending in $2, 3, 7$ or $8$ rules a number out; ending in anything else proves nothing.
  4. A perfect square has an even number of trailing zeros; a perfect cube has a multiple of three.
  5. $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1) = n^2$. Every square is a running total of the odd numbers, and each new odd number is the L-shaped border that grows one square into the next.
  6. Two consecutive triangular numbers add to a square, because a square array of dots cut along its diagonal falls into exactly those two triangles.
  7. Between $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$ there are $2n$ numbers, so squares thin out as they grow.
  8. Every perfect square has two square roots, one positive and one negative. The symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{n}}$ always means the positive one. A cube has a single cube root, which is negative when the cube is.
  9. In the prime factorisation of a perfect square every prime appears an even number of times; of a perfect cube, a multiple of three times. The primes deal out into two identical piles or three, and one pile is the root.
  10. The unpaired primes say what is missing, which turns the test into a repair: multiply by them and the result is a square, or a cube.
  11. A root can be trapped between two squares you know and then narrowed with the last-digit rule — often in two lines, and without factorising anything.
  12. The last digit of a cube names the last digit of its root exactly, which the last digit of a square never does.
  13. Differencing a list repeatedly flattens the squares after two rounds and the cubes after three, and the constants they settle on are $2$ and $6$.
  14. Varga was the square figure and the second power in one word, and ghana the solid and the third. A root is a mūla, the root of a plant — the thing the visible thing grew from — and that image survives in Arabic jidhr, Latin radix, and the English word you use every day.
