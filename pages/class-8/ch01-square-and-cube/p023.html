1.6Where the Words Came From

The oldest tables of squares and cubes that we know of were made in Babylon, on clay tablets, around 1700 BCE. A scribe who had to lay out a field of a given area, or work out how much earth a canal would move, could read the answer from a table faster than he could work it out himself.

In Sanskrit mathematics, the word varga meant a square figure, its area, and the act of multiplying a number by itself, all three at once. Āryabhaṭa, writing in 499 CE, uses it in both senses: a four-sided figure with equal sides is a varga, and so is the product of two equal numbers. The fourth power was varga-varga, the square of the square. For solids the word was ghana, meaning solid or compact. It named both the cube you could hold and a number multiplied by itself three times.

One word, one picture

Varga: a square, its area, and the second power. Ghana: a solid, and the third power. The arithmetic and the geometry were never separated, because nobody thought they were separate.

Undoing the operation got a picture of its own. The Sanskrit word mūla means the root of a plant, and so a base or an origin. A square root was the varga-mūla, the origin of the square, and a cube root was the ghana-mūla. Brahmagupta, writing in 628 CE, also used pada, a foot or a base, in the same way.

The picture travelled. Arabic translators of Sanskrit mathematics used jidhr, the root of a plant, and Latin translators of Arabic mathematics used radix. From radix come the English words root and radical. So when you write $\sqrt{49} = 7$, you are saying that $49$ grew from $7$. Taking a root is working back to where a number came from.
