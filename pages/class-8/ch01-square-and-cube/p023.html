1.5.2 Differencing until it stops changing

Section 1.2.2 took the differences of the squares once and found the odd numbers. There is no reason to stop after one round. Take the differences of the differences:

squares $1 \qquad 4 \qquad 9 \qquad 16 \qquad 25 \qquad 36$
first $3 \quad\ \ 5 \quad\ \ 7 \quad\ \ \ 9 \quad\ \ 11$
second $2 \quad\ \ 2 \quad\ \ 2 \quad\ \ \ 2$

After two rounds the squares have gone flat. Try the same on the cubes and see how many rounds they take.

Three rounds, and the constant is $6$. The squares took two rounds and settled on $2$. That is not a coincidence: the squares are $n^2$ and $2 = 1 \times 2$; the cubes are $n^3$ and $6 = 1 \times 2 \times 3$. Difference the fourth powers four times and they settle on $1 \times 2 \times 3 \times 4 = 24$ — which you can check from $1, 16, 81, 256, 625, 1296$ if you have the patience.

The count of rounds is the useful part. A list that goes flat after three rounds is behaving like a cube; one that goes flat after two is behaving like a square. Given a run of numbers and no rule, differencing is the first thing to try — and this is a trick you will meet again whenever a sequence has to be identified rather than remembered.
