C2 $3675 = 3 \times 5^2 \times 7^2$. Only the $3$ is unpaired, so dividing by $3$ leaves $1225 = 35^2$. Dividing by (b) or (c) breaks a pair that was fine, and (d) removes the $3$ and a $7$ — more than was needed.
C3 Only $7^3 = 343$ ends in $3$, so the root's last digit is forced. (d) is the square habit carried over by mistake: for squares two roots share an ending, but for cubes the ten endings are all different.
C5 (a) $36$ is a square and not a cube; (c) $216$ is a cube and not a square; (d) $729$ genuinely is both — it is $27^2$ and $9^3$ — but $64$ comes first. Both are sixth powers, $2^6$ and $3^6$.
C7 $2^6 \times 3^3$: the $6$ is a multiple of both $2$ and $3$, but the $3$ is a multiple only of $3$. So it is a cube and not a square. To be both, every exponent would have to be a multiple of $6$.
C8 Every odd square is $8m + 1$, so a number leaving $5$ is not one. (d) is false: $125$ leaves $5$ and is $5^3$. Cubes, unlike odd squares, can leave any remainder at all.
C10 $99^2 = 9801$ is below $10\,000$ and $100^2$ is exactly $10\,000$, so the squares run $1^2$ to $99^2$. (c) and (d) count $10\,000$ itself, which the word below excludes.

What to carry forward

Three habits from this section outlast the questions that carried them. The first is to look inside a number rather than at it: the factorisation answered nearly every hard question here, and the digits answered almost none. The second is that an impossibility needs an argument and not a search — $n^2 + n$ and $\sqrt{2}$ were both settled in three lines, and no amount of checking would have settled either. The third is that two conditions imposed at once are often one condition in disguise, as square-and-cube turned out to mean sixth power.
