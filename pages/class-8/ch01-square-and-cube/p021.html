Example 11

Is $3375$ a perfect cube? Is $500$?

$3375$ $= 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5$

Three $3$s and three $5$s. Deal them into three piles and each pile is $3 \times 5 = 15$, so

$3375$ $= 15 \times 15 \times 15 = 15^3$ $\sqrt[3]{3375} = 15$

Now $500$.

$500$ $= 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5$

The three $5$s deal out one to each pile, but the two $2$s cannot be shared three ways. So $500$ is not a perfect cube. Its two trailing zeros said as much before we started.

As with squares, the leftovers say what is missing. In $500$ there are two $2$s and a third is wanted, so $500 \times 2 = 1000 = 10^3$. In $1323 = 3^3 \times 7^2$ one more $7$ is wanted, and $1323 \times 7 = 9261 = 21^3$.

A word of warning about the trailing-zero rule, since it is the one most often mangled. It says a cube has a multiple of three zeros at the end — none, three, six, and so on. It does not say that a number with three zeros is a cube: $2000$ ends in three zeros and $2000 = 2^4 \times 5^3$, where the four $2$s cannot be shared three ways. As with the last digit of a square, the rule is a rejection and not a proof.

Set the two tests side by side, because the difference between them is only a number:

The last row is the one to remember. For squares the last digit is a quick rejection and a poor clue to the root; for cubes it rejects nothing and names the root's last digit exactly.
