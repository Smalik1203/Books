Why $n^2 + n$ is always trapped

No calculation will settle this, because there are infinitely many cases. What settles it is showing that $n^2 + n$ always falls in a place where no square can be.

Take any natural number $n$ and put $n^2 + n$ between the two squares nearest it. Since $n \ge 1$, it is certainly bigger than $n^2$. And it is smaller than the next square up:

$(n+1)^2$ $= n^2 + 2n + 1$
so $n^2 \;\lt\; n^2 + n \;\lt\; n^2 + 2n + 1$

So $n^2 + n$ sits strictly between two consecutive squares — and § 1.2.2 established that there is nothing but ordinary numbers in that gap. A square would have to be one of the two ends, and it is neither.

Try it once: $n = 12$ gives $156$, which lies between $144$ and $169$. The argument says that will happen every time, without a further case checked.

Numbers that are a square and a cube at once

Ask what such a number looks like in its prime factorisation. To be a square, every prime must appear an even number of times. To be a cube, a multiple of three times. To be both, a multiple of $6$ times — and a number where every prime appears a multiple of six times is a sixth power.

$1^6$$= 1$
$2^6$$= 64 = 8^2 = 4^3$
$3^6$$= 729 = 27^2 = 9^3$
$4^6$$= 4096$ the next one

Three of them below $1000$, then a long wait. Notice how the answer came: not by testing numbers one at a time, but by asking what the two conditions together demanded, and finding they demanded a single thing. Two conditions that combine into one are always worth looking for.

Both of these last two solutions did their real work before any arithmetic started. One turned a question about squares into a question about where a number sits between two others. The other turned a question about two properties into a question about how often each prime appears. In each case the arithmetic that followed was easy, which is usually a sign that the new way of looking at it was the right one.
