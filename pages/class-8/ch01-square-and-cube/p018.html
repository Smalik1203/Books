$\left(\tfrac46\right)^3$ $= \tfrac46 \times \tfrac46 \times \tfrac46 = \tfrac{64}{216}$
$(-6)^3$ $= -6 \times -6 \times -6 = -216$

That second line is a real difference between squares and cubes and is worth pausing on. A square is never negative, because the two minus signs cancel. A cube keeps a third minus sign that has nothing to cancel with, so the cube of a negative number is negative. Cubes run from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ where squares only ever go one way.

1.4.1 The number on the taxi

Srinivasa Ramanujan grew up in Kumbakonam, taught himself mathematics from a borrowed book of formulas, and in 1913 sent a letter full of unproved results to G. H. Hardy in Cambridge. Hardy, who received a good deal of unpromising post, read this one twice and brought him to England.

Some years later Ramanujan was ill in hospital and Hardy went to visit. Casting about for something to say, Hardy remarked that he had come in taxicab number $1729$, and that it seemed a rather dull number — he hoped it was not a bad omen.

Ramanujan answered at once that it was not dull in the least: it is the smallest number that can be written as a sum of two cubes in two different ways.

$1729 = 1^3 + 12^3 = 1 + 1728$

$1729 = 9^3 + 10^3 = 729 + 1000$

Check both lines. Neither is a trick; they are two genuinely different pairs of cubes adding to the same total, and no number below $1729$ has such a pair. It has been called the Hardy–Ramanujan number ever since, and numbers with this property are called taxicab numbers.

What is worth taking from the story is not the number but the habit. Ramanujan knew $1729$ the way a musician knows a tune — not by calculating on the spot, but because he had spent years turning numbers over. His colleague J. E. Littlewood said afterwards that every positive integer was one of Ramanujan's personal friends. The table of cubes on the previous page is a small beginning on the same acquaintance.

The word doing the work in his answer is smallest. Saying that $1729$ can be written as a sum of two cubes in two ways takes two lines to check; saying it is the first number that can is a claim about all $1728$ numbers below it, and that is a much larger thing to know. Ramanujan was not searching when he said it. He already knew.
