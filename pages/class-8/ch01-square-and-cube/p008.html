That single fact does two jobs. The first is a test. If every square is $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots$, then subtracting the odd numbers in order from a square must land exactly on zero — and from anything else it must overshoot.

Example 2

Is $25$ a perfect square? Is $38$?

Subtract $1$, then $3$, then $5$, and keep going.

from 25 $24,\ 21,\ 16,\ 9,\ 0$ $5$ steps
from 38 $37,\ 34,\ 29,\ 22,\ 13,\ 2,\ -11$ overshot

Twenty-five reaches zero after five subtractions, so $25 = 1+3+5+7+9$ and $25 = 5^2$. The count of steps is the root, which is a useful extra: the method does not merely say yes, it says five.

Thirty-eight steps over zero, from $2$ to $-11$, so it is not a square. Between $6^2 = 36$ and $7^2 = 49$ there is no square, and $38$ is stuck between them.

The second job is arithmetic. If you already know one square, the next one is a single addition away.

Example 3

Given that $35^2 = 1225$, find $36^2$ without multiplying.

$1225$ is the sum of the first $35$ odd numbers. To reach the sum of the first $36$, add the $36$th odd number.

36th odd number $2 \times 36 - 1 = 71$
$36^2$ $1225 + 71$ $1296$

The same step run the other way is just as useful: $34^2 = 1225 - 69$, since the $35$th odd number is $69$.

The gap between neighbouring squares also settles a question that looks harder than it is: how many numbers lie strictly between two consecutive squares? The gap from $n^2$ to $(n+1)^2$ is $2n+1$, and the two ends are not counted, so there are $2n$ numbers in between. Between $16^2$ and $17^2$ there are $32$; between $99^2$ and $100^2$ there are $198$; and the count grows steadily, which is why large squares are thinly scattered.
