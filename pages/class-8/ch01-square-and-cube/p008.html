The second job is arithmetic. If you already know one square, the next one is a single addition away.

Example 3

Given that $47^2 = 2209$, find $48^2$ without multiplying.

Solution.

Step 1 $2209$ is the sum of the first $47$ odd numbers
Step 2 48th odd number $= 2 \times 48 - 1 = 95$
Step 3 $48^2 = 2209 + 95$ sum of the first $48$
Answer $48^2 = 2304$

Run backwards: the $47$th odd number is $93$, so $46^2 = 2209 - 93 = 2116$, one step down.

The gap between neighbouring squares also settles a question that looks harder than it is: how many numbers lie strictly between two consecutive squares? The gap from $n^2$ to $(n+1)^2$ is $2n+1$, and the two ends are not counted, so there are $2n$ numbers in between. Between $24^2$ and $25^2$ there are $48$. Between $60^2$ and $61^2$ there are $120$. The count grows steadily, which is why large squares are thinly scattered.

Look back at the board of switches in Fig. 1.2 with that in mind. The lights that were on were $1, 4, 9, 16, 25$, and the gaps between them were $3, 5, 7, 9$. These are the odd numbers again: the same fact, seen from the side. Squares are not spread evenly along the number line and they never were. They start close together and get further and further apart. That is why the last-digit test matters so much for large numbers. By the time you reach five figures, a number picked at random has almost no chance of being a square, and a test that rules four endings out in a glance is worth having.

1.2.3 Two triangles make a square

You have met the triangular numbers before: $1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \ldots$, the counts of dots in a growing triangle, each got from the one before by adding a row. They have nothing obvious to do with squares. Add two neighbours together and see what happens.

$1 + 3$$= 4 = 2^2$
$3 + 6$$= 9 = 3^2$
$6 + 10$$= 16 = 4^2$
