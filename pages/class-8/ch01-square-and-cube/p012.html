Example 4

Is $324$ a perfect square? If so, what is its square root?

Break it into primes by dividing repeatedly.

$324$ $= 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3$

Now pair them off: $(2 \times 2)$ and $(3 \times 3)$ and $(3 \times 3)$. Every prime is used an even number of times, so $324$ is a perfect square, and the two identical piles are

$324$ $= (2 \times 3 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3 \times 3) = 18^2$ $\sqrt{324} = 18$

Notice that the method hands over the root as part of the answer. Take one member from each pair and multiply: $2 \times 3 \times 3 = 18$.

Example 5

Is $156$ a perfect square?

$156$ $= 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 13$

The two $2$s pair, but $3$ and $13$ are each alone. There is no way to split these four primes into two identical piles, so $156$ is not a perfect square.

The last digit would not have caught this one: $156$ ends in $6$, which is a perfectly respectable ending for a square. The factorisation settles it.

The unpaired primes are worth staring at, because they say exactly what is missing. In $156$ the strays are $3$ and $13$; multiply by $3 \times 13 = 39$ and the result, $6084$, is a square — it is $78^2$. That turns a test into a construction.

It is worth being clear about why this third method displaces the other two rather than sitting beside them. Listing and subtracting both do work proportional to the size of the number: testing a six-digit number by subtraction takes about a thousand subtractions. Factorising does work proportional to the size of the factors, which is a different and usually much smaller thing — and it answers questions the other two cannot answer at all, as the next two examples show.

There is also a habit here that will serve you long after this chapter. Neither example below is solved by remembering a procedure. Both are solved by writing the number in a form where the answer is visible — and choosing that form is the whole of the work. A number written as $9408$ tells you nothing; the same number written as $2^6 \times 3 \times 7^2$ tells you everything the question was asking.
