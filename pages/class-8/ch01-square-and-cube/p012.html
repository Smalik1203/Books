Why does this third method replace the other two? Listing and subtracting take more work the bigger the number is: testing a six-digit number by subtraction takes about a thousand subtractions. Factorising takes more work only when the factors are large. It also answers questions the other two cannot, as the next two examples show. In both, the work is to write the number in a form where the answer can be seen. $3920$ tells you nothing, but $2^4 \times 5 \times 7^2$ tells you everything the question asks.

Example 6

Find the smallest number by which $3920$ must be multiplied to give a perfect square, and find the square root of the product.

Factorise, then look for the primes that have no partner.

Solution.

Step 1 $3920 = 2^4 \times 5 \times 7^2$
Step 2 the $2$s and $7$s pair off; the $5$ is alone multiply by $5$
Step 3 $3920 \times 5 = 2^4 \times 5^2 \times 7^2 = (2^2 \times 5 \times 7)^2$
Step 4 $4 \times 5 \times 7 = 140$ one of each pair
Answer Multiply by 5. $19\,600 = 140^2$, so the root is 140

Nothing smaller than $5$ will do, because that $5$ has to be paired somehow.

Example 7

Find the smallest perfect square divisible by $8$, $9$ and $15$.

Solution.

Step 1 it must be a multiple of the LCM, $2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 360$
Step 2 one $2$ and the $5$ are unpaired, so $360$ is not a square multiply by $10$
Step 3 $360 \times 10 = 2^4 \times 3^2 \times 5^2 = 60^2$
Answer $3600$

Any square multiple of $360$ must contain four $2$s and two $5$s, so multiplying by $10$ is the smallest way. Examples 6 and 7 are the same move: factorise, find the primes standing alone, and supply their partners. Whether the question asks you to test a number or to repair one, the unpaired primes are what you are looking for.
