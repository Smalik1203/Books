Example 7

Find the smallest perfect square divisible by $4$, $9$ and $10$.

It must be a multiple of all three, so it must be a multiple of their lowest common multiple.

Solution.

Step 1 LCM $= 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 180$
Step 2 the $5$ is unpaired, so $180$ is not a square multiply by $5$
Step 3 $180 \times 5 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5^2 = 30^2$
Answer $900$

Every multiple of $180$ that is a square must contain that $5$ twice, so multiplying by $5$ is the smallest way to arrange it.

Both examples are the same move: factorise, find the primes standing alone, and supply their partners. Whether the question asks you to test a number or to repair one, the unpaired primes are what you are looking for.

Is the answer the same number? Should you expect it to be?

One more thing follows from the factorisation, and it is worth noticing even though nothing here depends on it. If a number is not a perfect square then its root is not a whole number — that much is now obvious, since a stray prime cannot be paired. What is far less obvious is that the root is not a fraction either. There is no fraction whose square is $2$, and the reason is the pairing argument run once more. That is a fact about the number line rather than a gap in our methods, and it has a chapter of its own further on. For now it explains why estimating a root well is a real skill and not second best: for most numbers an estimate is the only answer there is.
