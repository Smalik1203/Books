A number with exactly three factors

Three is an odd number of factors, so by the chapter's first result the number must be a square. That alone cuts the field to $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \ldots$

Now look at which squares have exactly three. A square $n^2$ always has $1$, $n$ and $n^2$ among its factors, so three is the fewest it could have — and it has exactly three when $n$ brings nothing else in with it, which is to say when $n$ is prime.

$4 = 2^2$ $1, 2, 4$
$9 = 3^2$ $1, 3, 9$
$25 = 5^2$ $1, 5, 25$
$49 = 7^2$ $1, 7, 49$ the four smallest

Check that $16$ is properly excluded: $16 = 2^4$ has factors $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ — five of them, because $4$ is not prime and drags $2$ along. So the answer is the square of a prime, and nothing else.

Adding fifty odd numbers by subtracting two squares

The sum runs over odd numbers, so reach for $1 + 3 + \cdots + (2n-1) = n^2$. It does not start at $1$, but that is easily arranged: add the missing front and take it away again.

$1 + 3 + \cdots + 99$ $= 50^2 = 2500$ since $99 = 2 \times 50 - 1$
$1 + 3 + \cdots + 49$ $= 25^2 = 625$ since $49 = 2 \times 25 - 1$
the difference $= 2500 - 625$ $1875$

Twenty-five numbers added by subtracting two you already knew. The general move is worth keeping: a run that starts in the wrong place becomes two runs that both start in the right one.

Notice that the second solution never added a single one of the twenty-five numbers it was asked about. It found a formula that covered a longer run, another that covered the unwanted front of it, and subtracted. That manoeuvre — solve a bigger problem you can handle, then remove the part you did not want — turns up everywhere in mathematics, and it is worth recognising as a move rather than meeting it fresh each time.
