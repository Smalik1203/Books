the $2$s $a + 1$ is even, and $a$ is a multiple of $3$ $a = 3$
the $3$s $b$ is even, and $b + 1$ is a multiple of $3$ $b = 2$
$n$ $= 2^3 \times 3^2 = 72$

Check both: $2n = 144 = 12^2$ and $3n = 216 = 6^3$. Notice how the answer came: not by testing numbers one at a time, but by asking what the two conditions demanded of each prime separately. Two conditions on one number are often easiest to read one prime at a time.

Both of these last two solutions did their real work before any arithmetic started. One turned a question about squares into a question about where a number sits between two others. The other turned a question about two properties into a question about how often each prime appears. In each case the arithmetic that followed was easy, which is usually a sign that the new way of looking at it was the right one.

A square sheet of cardboard has an area of exactly $2$ m². Show that its side is not a whole number of metres. Then show that it is not a fraction of a metre either.

The side is $\sqrt{2}$, and the first half is quick. If the side were a whole number $n$ then $n^2 = 2$, and $1^2 = 1$ is too small while $2^2 = 4$ is too big. There is no whole number between $1$ and $2$, so there is no such $n$.

The second half is the interesting one, and it is the pairing argument used once more. Suppose the side were a fraction $\tfrac{a}{b}$, written in lowest terms. Squaring gives

$\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^2 = 2$ so $a^2 = 2b^2$

Now count how many $2$s each side contains. On the left, $a^2$ is a square, so the prime $2$ appears in it an even number of times — that is exactly what being a square means. On the right, $b^2$ likewise has an even number of $2$s, and then one more $2$ is added on, making the count odd.

One number cannot contain the prime $2$ an even number of times and an odd number of times at the same time. So no such fraction exists, and the side of that sheet of cardboard cannot be written as a fraction at all.

This is worth more than the question that produced it. Numbers of this kind are called irrational, and finding the first of them was a genuine shock — a length you can draw with a ruler, and cannot write down. You will meet them properly in a chapter of their own; the argument above is the whole proof, and you have just followed it.

Square five odd numbers of your choosing and divide each answer by $8$. The remainder is always $1$. Explain why it must be.

Check first, then explain. $1, 9, 25, 49, 81$ divided by $8$ leave $1, 1, 1, 1, 1$. It is not a coincidence and the reason is one line of algebra.
