All ten digits appear, and each appears exactly once. So the last-digit test is useless for ruling a cube out — every ending is possible — but it is far more powerful in the other direction. The last digit of a cube tells you the last digit of its root, with no ambiguity at all. A cube ending in $7$ came from a number ending in $3$; a cube ending in $2$ came from a number ending in $8$. Only $0, 1, 4, 5, 6$ and $9$ end their cubes in themselves.

Squares hide their roots; cubes do not

A square ending in $6$ might come from a number ending in $4$ or in $6$. A cube ending in $6$ can only have come from a number ending in $6$. That single difference makes cube roots of large numbers easier to guess than square roots.

Two smaller habits follow the same way as they did for squares. Cubing a number that ends in a zero triples the zeros: $10^3 = 1000$, $20^3 = 8000$, $200^3 = 8\,000\,000$. So a perfect cube has a number of trailing zeros that is a multiple of three, and a number ending in exactly two zeros — $500$, or $1200$ — cannot be a cube. And cubing keeps a number odd or even, as squaring did: odd cubed is odd, even cubed is even.

Cubing is not limited to whole numbers either.

$\left(\tfrac46\right)^3$ $= \tfrac46 \times \tfrac46 \times \tfrac46 = \tfrac{64}{216}$
$(-6)^3$ $= -6 \times -6 \times -6 = -216$

That second line is a real difference between squares and cubes and is worth pausing on. A square is never negative, because the two minus signs cancel. A cube keeps a third minus sign that has nothing to cancel with, so the cube of a negative number is negative. So cubes run both ways, far into the negative numbers and far into the positive, where squares only ever go one way.

1.4.1 The number on the taxi

Srinivasa Ramanujan grew up in Kumbakonam, taught himself mathematics from a borrowed book of formulas, and in 1913 sent a letter full of unproved results to G. H. Hardy in Cambridge. Hardy, who got plenty of letters that led nowhere, read this one twice and brought him to England.

Some years later Ramanujan was ill in hospital and Hardy went to visit. Short of something to say, Hardy said that he had come in taxicab number $1729$, and that it seemed a rather dull number. He hoped it was not a bad sign.

Ramanujan answered at once that it was not dull in the least: it is the smallest number that can be written as a sum of two cubes in two different ways.

$1729 = 1^3 + 12^3 = 1 + 1728$

$1729 = 9^3 + 10^3 = 729 + 1000$

Check both lines. Neither is a trick; they are two genuinely different pairs of cubes adding to the same total, and no number below $1729$ has such a pair. It has been called the Hardy–Ramanujan number ever since, and numbers with this property are called taxicab numbers.
