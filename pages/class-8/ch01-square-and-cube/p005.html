Now be careful about what this buys you, because it is a one-way test. It says that a number ending in $2, 3, 7$ or $8$ is certainly not a square: $1027$ is out at a glance, and so is $2048$. It does not say that a number ending in $0, 1, 4, 5, 6$ or $9$ is one. Both $16$ and $26$ end in $6$; only the first is a square.

A test that only says no

If a number ends in $2$, $3$, $7$ or $8$, it is not a perfect square. If it ends in anything else, the test has told you nothing and you must look further.

Notice too that the second row is a mirror: $1, 4, 9, 6, 5$ and then $6, 9, 4, 1$ back again. That is because $n$ and $10 - n$ end their squares the same way — $4^2 = 16$ and $6^2 = 36$ both end in $6$, and $1^2$ and $9^2$ both end in $1$. So a square ending in $6$ came from a number ending in $4$ or $6$, and a square ending in $1$ came from a number ending in $1$ or $9$. That will be useful when we start hunting for square roots.

Example 1

Which of $2032$, $2048$, $1027$ and $1089$ are certainly not perfect squares?

Read the last digit of each and check it against the six that are possible.

$2032$ ends in $2$ — impossible
$2048$ ends in $8$ — impossible
$1027$ ends in $7$ — impossible
$1089$ ends in $9$ — possible, so look further

Three are settled without a calculation. The fourth is not settled at all; as it happens $1089 = 33^2$, but the last digit could never have told us that.

Two more habits of squares are worth collecting here, since both come from the same place and both save work later.

Zeros come in pairs. Squaring $10$ gives $100$: one zero becomes two. Squaring $200$ gives $40\,000$: two zeros become four.

$10^2$$= 100$
$200^2$$= 40\,000$
$900^2$$= 810\,000$
