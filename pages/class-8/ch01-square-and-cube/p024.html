1. $64$ is both a perfect square and a perfect cube. Using prime factorisation, say what a number must look like on the inside for that to happen, and find the next two numbers after $64$ with the same property.
  1. A cubical tank holds $216$ m³. Find the length of an edge, the area of the base, and the total area of the five faces that must be lined — every face but the open top.
  1. This chapter wrote $n^2$ for two copies of $n$ multiplied together and $n^3$ for three. Guess what $n^4$ and $n^5$ ought to mean; work out $2^4$, $2^5$ and $3^4$; and say how many zeros $10^7$ ends in. The next chapter takes the idea up properly.
  1. The first five square numbers are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 and the first five cubes are 1, 8, 27, 64, 125. Add each square to the cube in the same position and take differences of the five answers you get. How many rounds does it take to go flat, and why should you have expected that?
  1. Write down a number that the last-digit test cannot rule out as a square but which is not one, and a number that no test in this chapter can settle without factorising. Explain your choices.
