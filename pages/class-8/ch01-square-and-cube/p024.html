1.6Where the Words Came From

The oldest lists of squares and cubes we have are Babylonian, pressed into clay around 1700 BCE. They are not curiosities: a scribe who had to work out how much earth a canal would displace, or lay out a field of a given area, wanted the answer quickly, and a table on the desk was quicker than a calculation. Tables of squares and cubes are among the earliest mathematical objects anybody made, and they were made because somebody needed them.

The words we use are older than the notation and they are worth unpicking, because each of them is a picture.

In Sanskrit mathematical writing — in use from at least the third century BCE — the word varga meant a square figure, and the area of that figure, and the operation of multiplying a number by itself. All three, with no attempt to keep them apart. Āryabhaṭa, writing in 499 CE, defines it in exactly that double sense: a four-sided figure with equal sides is a varga, and so is the product of two equal quantities. The fourth power was varga-varga, the square of the square.

For solids the word was ghana, meaning thick or solid or compact — again both the cube you could hold and the number you got by multiplying something by itself three times.

So the title of this chapter is not a metaphor anyone invented for it. For a very long time a squared number and a square figure were the same word, because they were felt to be the same thing.

One word, one picture

Varga: a square, its area, and the second power. Ghana: a solid, and the third power. The arithmetic and the geometry were never separated because nobody thought they were separate.

Undoing the operation got a picture of its own, and a stranger one. The Sanskrit word mūla means the root of a plant, and from there its basis, cause or origin. A square root was the varga-mūla — the origin of the square — and a cube root the ghana-mūla. The usage is attested from at least the first century BCE. Another word used the same way was pada, a foot or a base; Brahmagupta, writing in 628 CE, explains that the pada of a square is the thing it is the square of.
