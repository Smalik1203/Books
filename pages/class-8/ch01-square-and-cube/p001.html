The Shape of a Number

1.1Introduction

Some numbers have a shape. Sixteen dots can be laid out as a square with four to a side, and twenty-seven small cubes can be stacked into a larger cube three along each edge. Fifteen dots cannot be made into a square, however they are arranged, and twenty-six cubes will not stack into a cube.

That is not a decorative fact. Numbers with a shape behave differently from numbers without one, and this chapter is about the difference — how to recognise such a number, what patterns it obeys, and how to work backwards from the number to the side of the square or the edge of the cube that made it.

Here is a puzzle that turns entirely on it.

Think and Reflect

A corridor has $100$ lockers, numbered $1$ to $100$, and all of them are shut. A hundred students walk down it in turn.

Student $1$ changes every locker — opens each shut one. Student $2$ changes every second locker: $2, 4, 6, \ldots$ Student $3$ changes every third: $3, 6, 9, \ldots$ and so on, until student $100$ has changed locker $100$ alone.

When all hundred have gone past, which lockers are open?

Guessing is no use here, and neither is opening and shutting a hundred lockers on paper. What settles it is one small question: how many times is any one locker touched?

Locker $12$ is touched by student $1$, by student $2$, by student $3$, by student $4$, by student $6$ and by student $12$ — and by nobody else, because student $5$ skips it and so does student $7$. Those six students are exactly the six factors of $12$.

That is the whole of it. A locker is touched once for each factor of its number. It starts shut, and every touch changes it, so it ends open when the number of factors is odd and shut when the number is even.
