Each trailing zero of $n$ is a factor of $10$, and squaring uses it twice — so the square has exactly twice as many. A number with three zeros at the end has a square with six. Read backwards: a perfect square can only end in an even number of zeros, so $2000$ is not a square and neither is any number ending in an odd run of them.

Squaring keeps parity. An even number is $2k$, and $(2k)^2 = 4k^2$, which is even. An odd number is $2k+1$, and its square is $4k^2 + 4k + 1$, which is odd. So a square is even exactly when the number it came from is even — $6^2 = 36$, $7^2 = 49$ — and no square is ever the wrong parity for its root.

1.2.2 Every square is a sum of odd numbers

Set the squares in a row and take the difference of each pair of neighbours:

squares $1 \qquad 4 \qquad 9 \qquad 16 \qquad 25 \qquad 36$
differences $3 \quad\ \ 5 \quad\ \ 7 \quad\ \ \ 9 \quad\ \ 11$

The odd numbers, in order, and no exception anywhere down the list. Turned round, that says every square is what you get by adding up odd numbers from $1$: stop after one and you have $1$, stop after two and you have $4$, and the running total is a square at every step and never at any other moment.
