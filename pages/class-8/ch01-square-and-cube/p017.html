Two smaller habits follow the same way as they did for squares. Cubing a number that ends in a zero triples the zeros: $10^3 = 1000$, $20^3 = 8000$, $200^3 = 8\,000\,000$. So a perfect cube has a number of trailing zeros that is a multiple of three, and a number ending in exactly two zeros — $500$, or $1200$ — cannot be a cube. And cubing keeps a number odd or even, as squaring did: odd cubed is odd, even cubed is even.

Cubing is not limited to whole numbers either.

$\left(\tfrac25\right)^3$ $= \tfrac25 \times \tfrac25 \times \tfrac25 = \tfrac{8}{125}$
$(-4)^3$ $= (-4) \times (-4) \times (-4) = -64$

That second line is a real difference between squares and cubes and is worth pausing on. A square is never negative, because the two minus signs cancel. A cube keeps a third minus sign that has nothing to cancel with, so the cube of a negative number is negative. So cubes run both ways, into the negative numbers and into the positive, where squares only ever go one way.

1.4.1 The number on the taxi

Srinivasa Ramanujan grew up in Kumbakonam in Tamil Nadu. He taught himself mathematics from a borrowed book of formulas. In 1913 he sent a letter full of new results to G. H. Hardy in Cambridge. Hardy read it twice, and invited him to England.

Some years later Ramanujan was ill, and Hardy went to see him. Hardy mentioned that his taxi had the number $1729$, which seemed to him a dull number.

Ramanujan replied at once that it was not dull at all. It is the smallest number that can be written as a sum of two cubes in two different ways.

$1729 = 1^3 + 12^3 = 1 + 1728$

$1729 = 9^3 + 10^3 = 729 + 1000$

Check both lines. They are two different pairs of cubes with the same total, and no number below $1729$ has two such pairs. It has been called the Hardy–Ramanujan number ever since. Numbers like it, each a sum of two cubes in two different ways, are now called taxicab numbers.

What is worth taking from the story is the habit, not the number. Ramanujan knew $1729$ because he had spent years turning numbers over, not because he worked it out on the spot. His colleague J. E. Littlewood said that every positive integer was one of Ramanujan's personal friends. The table of cubes in §1.4 is a small start on the same kind of friendship.
