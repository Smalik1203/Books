1.5.2 Taking differences again and again

Section 1.2.2 took the differences of the squares once and found the odd numbers. There is no reason to stop after one round. Take the differences of the differences:

squares $1 \qquad 4 \qquad 9 \qquad 16 \qquad 25 \qquad 36$
first $3 \quad\ \ 5 \quad\ \ 7 \quad\ \ \ 9 \quad\ \ 11$
second $2 \quad\ \ 2 \quad\ \ 2 \quad\ \ \ 2$

After two rounds the squares have gone flat. Try the same on the cubes and see how many rounds they take.

Three rounds, and the constant is $6$. The squares took two rounds and settled on $2$. That is not a coincidence: the squares are $n^2$ and $2 = 1 \times 2$; the cubes are $n^3$ and $6 = 1 \times 2 \times 3$. Take differences of the fourth powers four times and they settle on $1 \times 2 \times 3 \times 4 = 24$. You can check this from $1, 16, 81, 256, 625, 1296$.

The count of rounds is the useful part. A list that goes flat after three rounds is behaving like a cube; one that goes flat after two is behaving like a square. For example, $2, 6, 12, 20, 30$ has differences $4, 6, 8, 10$ and then $2, 2, 2$. It goes flat after two rounds, so it behaves like a square, and indeed it is $1^2 + 1$, $2^2 + 2$, $3^2 + 3$, and so on. Given a run of numbers and no rule, taking differences is the first thing to try.
