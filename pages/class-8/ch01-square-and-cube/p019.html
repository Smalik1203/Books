Both statements are the same statement with a different number in it, and it is worth pausing on why the number is the one it is. Squaring uses each prime twice, so the count of each prime doubles and every count comes out even. Cubing uses each prime three times, so the count triples and every count comes out a multiple of three. The test is that same arithmetic read backwards, which is why you do not have to remember it once you have seen it.

Example 11

Is $3375$ a perfect cube? Is $500$?

Solution.

Step 1 $3375 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5$
Step 2 three piles, each $3 \times 5 = 15$ three $3$s, three $5$s
Step 3 $3375 = 15 \times 15 \times 15 = 15^3$
Step 4 $500 = 2 \times 2 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5$
Step 5 the three $5$s deal out, but the two $2$s cannot be shared three ways
Answer $\sqrt[3]{3375} = 15$; $500$ is not a perfect cube

The two trailing zeros of $500$ said so before we started.

As with squares, the leftovers say what is missing. In $500$ there are two $2$s and a third is wanted, so $500 \times 2 = 1000 = 10^3$. In $1323 = 3^3 \times 7^2$ one more $7$ is wanted, and $1323 \times 7 = 9261 = 21^3$.

A word of warning about the trailing-zero rule, since it is the one most often got wrong. It says a cube has a multiple of three zeros at the end — none, three, six, and so on. It does not say that a number with three zeros is a cube: $2000$ ends in three zeros and $2000 = 2^4 \times 5^3$, where the four $2$s cannot be shared three ways. As with the last digit of a square, the rule is a rejection and not a proof.

Set the two tests side by side, because the difference between them is only a number:

The last row is the one to remember. For squares the last digit is a quick rejection and a poor clue to the root; for cubes it rejects nothing and names the root's last digit exactly.
