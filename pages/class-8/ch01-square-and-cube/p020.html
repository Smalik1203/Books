As with squares, the leftovers say what is missing. In $1375 = 5^3 \times 11$ two more $11$s are wanted, and $1375 \times 121 = 166\,375 = 55^3$. In $3087 = 3^2 \times 7^3$ one more $3$ is wanted, and $3087 \times 3 = 9261$, which is $21^3$.

A word of warning about the trailing-zero rule, since it is often got wrong. It says a cube has a multiple of three zeros at the end — none, three, six, and so on. It does not say that a number with three zeros is a cube: $2000$ ends in three zeros and $2000 = 2^4 \times 5^3$, where the four $2$s cannot be shared three ways. As with the last digit of a square, the rule is a rejection and not a proof.

Testing for a cube
  • A number is a perfect cube when every prime in it appears a multiple of three times.
  • Its cube root takes one prime from each group of three: $\sqrt[3]{5832} = 18$.

Set the two tests side by side, because the difference between them is only a number:

The last row is the one to remember. For squares the last digit is a quick rejection and a poor clue to the root; for cubes it rejects nothing and names the root's last digit exactly. A cube ending in $9$ has a root ending in $9$; a cube ending in $3$ has a root ending in $7$.

1.5.1 Reading a cube root off the digits

For a cube of up to six digits there is a method that needs no factorising at all, and it uses the two facts we have. It works because a two-digit root has a cube of at most six digits, so you can find the two digits one at a time — the units digit from the end of the number, the tens digit from the front. The units digit comes from the table of last digits above, and the tens digit from the table of cubes in §1.4.
