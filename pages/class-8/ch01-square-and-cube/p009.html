Look back at the corridor of lockers in Fig. 1.2 with that in mind. The open ones were $1, 4, 9, 16, 25$, and the gaps between them were $3, 5, 7, 9$ — the odd numbers again, which is exactly the same fact seen from the side. Squares are not spread evenly along the number line and they never were; they start close together and draw apart for ever. That is why the last-digit test matters so much for large numbers: by the time you reach five figures, a number picked at random has almost no chance of being a square, and a test that rules four endings out in a glance is worth having.

1.2.3 Two triangles make a square

You have met the triangular numbers before: $1, 3, 6, 10, 15, \ldots$, the counts of dots in a growing triangle, each got from the one before by adding a row. They have nothing obvious to do with squares. Add two neighbours together and see what happens.

$1 + 3$$= 4 = 2^2$
$3 + 6$$= 9 = 3^2$
$6 + 10$$= 16 = 4^2$

Cut a square array of dots along its diagonal. The half that keeps the diagonal is a triangle of $1 + 2 + \cdots + n$ dots; the half that loses it is a triangle one row shorter. Between them they account for every dot, so two consecutive triangular numbers always add to a square.

Both facts in this section came from the same manoeuvre, which is worth naming because you will want it again. A number was counted twice — once as a total and once as a shape — and the two counts were set equal. Nothing was calculated and nothing was checked case by case; the argument was finished the moment the picture was drawn correctly. Mathematicians call this counting two ways, and it settles a surprising number of questions that look as though they need algebra.
