Example 6

Find the smallest number by which $9408$ must be multiplied to give a perfect square, and find the square root of the product.

Factorise, then look for the primes that have no partner.

$9408$ $= 2^6 \times 3 \times 7^2$

The six $2$s make three pairs and the two $7$s make one. The single $3$ is the only stray, so multiplying by $3$ is enough — and nothing smaller will do, because that $3$ has to be paired somehow.

$9408 \times 3$ $= 2^6 \times 3^2 \times 7^2 = (2^3 \times 3 \times 7)^2$ $28224 = 168^2$

The root is read straight off: one member of each pair, $8 \times 3 \times 7 = 168$.

Example 7

Find the smallest perfect square divisible by $4$, $9$ and $10$.

It must be a multiple of all three, so it must be a multiple of their lowest common multiple.

LCM $= 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5 = 180$

$180$ is not itself a square — the $5$ is unpaired — and every multiple of $180$ that is a square must contain that $5$ twice. The smallest way to arrange it is to multiply by $5$.

$180 \times 5$ $= 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5^2 = 30^2$ $900$

Both examples are the same move: factorise, find the primes standing alone, and supply their partners. Whether the question asks you to test a number or to repair one, the unpaired primes are what you are looking for.
