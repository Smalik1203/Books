1.3.1 Estimating a root you do not know

Factorising a large number takes time, and often it is not needed. Two facts you already have will trap a root between two whole numbers in a few lines: the squares you know, and the last-digit rule.

Example 8

Find $\sqrt{1936}$ without factorising.

Solution.

Step 1 $40^2 = 1600$ and $50^2 = 2500$, so the root of $1936$ is between $40$ and $50$ trap it
Step 2 $1936$ ends in $6$, so the root ends in $4$ or $6$: $44$ or $46$ use the last digit
Step 3 $45^2 = (40 + 5)(40 + 5) = 1600 + 400 + 25 = 2025$ split the difference
Step 4 $2025$ is more than $1936$, so the root is below $45$: $44$
Step 5 $44^2 = 1936$ check
Answer $\sqrt{1936} = 44$

Three lines and no factorisation. Notice what each line contributed: the first narrowed fifty candidates to ten, the second narrowed ten to two, and the third chose between the two. Each step threw away most of what was left, which is what makes the method quick — and it is a kind of argument you will meet whenever a search has to be made short.

The same trapping works when there is no exact answer to find, and then it is the only thing that works. Most numbers are not perfect squares, so most of the roots you will ever want cannot be written down exactly at all — and a question about the real world hardly ever hands you a perfect square.
