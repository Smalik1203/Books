One more thing follows from the factorisation, and it is worth noticing even though nothing here depends on it. If a number is not a perfect square then its root is not a whole number — that much is now obvious, since a stray prime cannot be paired. What is far less obvious is that the root is not a fraction either. There is no fraction whose square is $2$, and the reason is the pairing argument run once more. That is a fact about the number line rather than a gap in our methods, and it has a chapter of its own further on. For now it explains why estimating a root well is a skill and not a consolation prize: for most numbers an estimate is the only answer there is.

1.3.1 Estimating a root you do not know

Factorising a large number takes time, and often it is not needed. Two facts already established will corner a root between two whole numbers in a few lines: the squares you know, and the last-digit rule.

Example 8

Find $\sqrt{1936}$ without factorising.

Trap it between two squares you know. $40^2 = 1600$ and $50^2 = 2500$, and $1936$ lies between them, so the root lies between $40$ and $50$.

Use the last digit. $1936$ ends in $6$, and a square ends in $6$ only when its root ends in $4$ or $6$. The only candidates left are $44$ and $46$.

Split the difference. Which side of $45$ is it? We need $45^2$, and it can be had without long multiplication:

$45^2$ $= (40 + 5)(40 + 5) = 1600 + 400 + 25 = 2025$

$2025$ is more than $1936$, so the root is below $45$ — and of the two candidates only $44$ is. Check: $44^2 = 1936$.

$\sqrt{1936}$ $44$
