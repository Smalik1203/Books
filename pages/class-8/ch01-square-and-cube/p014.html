No factorisation was needed. Notice what each step did. The first narrowed the candidates to ten, the second narrowed ten to two, and the third chose between the two. Each step threw away most of what was left, which is what makes the method quick.

The same trapping works when there is no exact answer to find, and then it is the only thing that works. Most numbers are not perfect squares, so most of the roots you will ever want cannot be written down exactly at all — and a question about the real world hardly ever hands you a perfect square.

Example 9

A square sheet of card has an area of $150$ cm². Can a square tile of side $13$ cm be cut from it? What is the largest square tile with a whole-number side that can be cut from it?

Solution.

Step 1 a side of $13$ cm needs $13^2 = 169$ cm², and there is only $150$ so no
Step 2 $12^2 = 144$ is less than $150$ fits, $6$ cm² to spare
Answer No. The largest whole-number side is $12$ cm

Sometimes only the nearest whole number is wanted. To estimate $\sqrt{200}$: it lies between $\sqrt{196} = 14$ and $\sqrt{225} = 15$. Since $200$ is much closer to $196$ than to $225$, the root is a little over $14$. That is a good answer to a question that has no exact one, because $200$ is not a perfect square.

The same trap can be closed further. Square numbers just above $14$, one decimal place at a time, and compare:

$14.1^2$ $= 198.81$ less than $200$
$14.2^2$ $= 201.64$ more than $200$

So $\sqrt{200}$ lies between $14.1$ and $14.2$. Each round of this kind gives one more decimal place, and the trapping can go on for as long as you need.
