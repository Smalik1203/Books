Square five odd numbers of your choosing and divide each answer by $8$. The remainder is always $1$. Explain why it must be.

How many digits can the cube of a two-digit number have? Give an example of each length that is possible.

$1729$ is the smallest number that is a sum of two cubes in two different ways. What is the smallest number that is a sum of two squares in two different ways? Searching by hand is quite feasible if you organise it.

A word about the third and the fifth, because they are a different kind of question from the rest. Both ask you to show that something is impossible, and you cannot do that by checking cases — there are infinitely many and you would never finish. What settles an impossibility is an argument that covers every case at once, and in both of these the argument is short. Finding it is the work; writing it down takes three lines.

The others are more ordinary, but each has a lever in it. The first turns on counting factors rather than listing them. The second is a subtraction of two squares in disguise. The fourth and the seventh both ask what a number must look like in its factorised form, which is the chapter's central move applied twice over. And the last is a search, so the question is not whether you can do it but whether you can organise it so that it ends.

If a question resists, write out the first ten cases by hand. A pattern that is invisible in the statement is often obvious in a list, and the list costs a minute. Every solution overleaf begins that way, even the ones that end in algebra.

One further remark, since it applies to half the list. Several of these questions are about what a number looks like once it has been taken apart into primes, and none of them can be answered by staring at the digits. If you find yourself squinting at a number hoping for inspiration, stop and factorise it instead. That is not a last resort; it is the first move.

A last piece of advice on how to use this section. Do not read the solutions one after another like a chapter. Take a question, work at it until you either finish it or run out of ideas, and only then turn over — and when you do, read only the one you were working on. The value of a worked solution is almost entirely in the gap between what you tried and what it did, and there is no gap to look at if you have not tried.
