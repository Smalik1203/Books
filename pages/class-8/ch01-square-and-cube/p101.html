Using What You Know

The chapter left you with a short list, and it is shorter than it looks. Factors come in partner pairs, and a square is the one number with a factor that is its own partner. A square is a running total of the odd numbers. Squaring doubles how many times each prime appears, and cubing triples it, so a perfect square has every prime an even number of times and a perfect cube a multiple of three. And a root that cannot be found exactly can nearly always be trapped between two you know.

Every question below can be answered from that list. None of them says which item to reach for, and two can be settled with no arithmetic at all if you see what is being asked. Try each before reading what follows it.

If you cannot get started on a question, write out the first ten cases by hand. A pattern that is invisible in the statement is often obvious in a list, and the list costs a minute. Every explanation below begins that way, even the ones that end in algebra.

A last piece of advice on how to use this section. Do not read the explanations one after another like a chapter. Take a question, work at it until you either finish it or run out of ideas, and only then read on — and when you do, read only the explanation of the one you were working on. The value of a worked solution is almost entirely in the gap between what you tried and what it did. If you have not tried, there is no gap to look at.

A number has exactly five factors. What must such a number look like? Give the two smallest.

Five is an odd number of factors, so by the chapter's first result the number must be a square. That alone cuts the field to $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \ldots$

Now ask what can be inside it. If two different primes $p$ and $q$ divide a square, each appears at least twice, so $1$, $p$, $q$, $p^2$, $q^2$ and $pq$ are all factors — six already, which is too many. So only one prime is inside it, and the number is a power of that prime. A power $p^k$ has the factors $1, p, p^2, \ldots, p^k$, which is $k + 1$ of them, so five factors means $k = 4$.

$16 = 2^4$ $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$
$81 = 3^4$ $1, 3, 9, 27, 81$ the two smallest

Check a square of the other kind: $100 = 2^2 \times 5^2$ has nine factors, because two primes are inside it. So the answer is the fourth power of a prime, and nothing else.
