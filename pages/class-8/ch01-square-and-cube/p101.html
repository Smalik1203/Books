Using What You Know
The chapter's ideas, in questions that do not say which one to use.

The chapter left you with a short list, and it is shorter than it looks. Factors come in partner pairs, and a square is the one number that is its own partner. A square is a running total of the odd numbers. Squaring doubles every exponent in a prime factorisation and cubing trebles them, so a perfect square has every prime an even number of times and a perfect cube a multiple of three. And a root that cannot be found exactly can nearly always be trapped between two you know.

Every question below can be answered from that list. None of them says which item to reach for, and three can be settled with no arithmetic at all if you see what is being asked. Try each before turning the page.

A number has exactly three factors. What must such a number look like? Give the four smallest.

Find $51 + 53 + 55 + \cdots + 99$ without adding more than two numbers together.

Show that $n^2 + n$ is never a perfect square, for any natural number $n$.

Which numbers below $1000$ are a perfect square and a perfect cube? Say what such a number must look like on the inside, and then write down the next one after the last of them.

A square sheet of cardboard has an area of exactly $2$ m². Show that its side is not a whole number of metres. Then show that it is not a fraction of a metre either.
