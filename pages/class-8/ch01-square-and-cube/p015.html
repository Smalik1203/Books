Three lines and no factorisation. Notice what each line contributed: the first narrowed fifty candidates to ten, the second narrowed ten to two, and the third chose between the two. Each step threw away most of what was left, which is what makes the method quick — and it is a shape of argument you will meet whenever a search has to be made short.

The same trapping works when there is no exact answer to find, and then it is the only thing that works. Most numbers are not perfect squares, so most of the roots you will ever want cannot be written down exactly at all — and a question about the real world hardly ever obliges by handing you a perfect square.

Example 9

A square of cloth has an area of $125$ cm². Can a square handkerchief of side $15$ cm be cut from it? What is the largest square handkerchief with a whole-number side that can be?

A side of $15$ cm needs $15^2 = 225$ cm² of cloth, and there is only $125$. So no — and notice that the question was answered by squaring rather than by taking a root.

For the second part, find the whole numbers on either side of $\sqrt{125}$.

$11^2$$= 121$ — fits, with $4$ cm² to spare
$12^2$$= 144$ — too big

So the largest whole-number side is $11$ cm.

Sometimes only the nearest whole number is wanted. To estimate $\sqrt{250}$: it lies between $\sqrt{225} = 15$ and $\sqrt{256} = 16$, and since $250$ is much closer to $256$ than to $225$, the root is a little under $16$. That is a good enough answer to a question that has no exact one — $250$ is not a perfect square, and no whole number will ever be its root.
