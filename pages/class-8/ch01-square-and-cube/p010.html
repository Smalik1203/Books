1.3Square Roots

A square courtyard has an area of $81$ m². How long is its side?

The question runs the other way from everything so far. We have been given a side and asked for an area; now we are given the area and asked for the side. Since $9 \times 9 = 81$, the side is $9$ m, and we call $9$ a square root of $81$.

In general, if $y = x^2$ then $x$ is a square root of $y$. Undoing a squaring is the whole business of this section, and there is one wrinkle in it to settle first.

What is the square root of $64$? Eight, because $8 \times 8 = 64$. But $(-8) \times (-8) = 64$ as well, so $-8$ has an equal claim:

$8^2 = 64 \qquad \text{and} \qquad (-8)^2 = 64$

Every perfect square has two square roots, one positive and one negative, and they differ only in sign. Zero is the single exception: its only square root is itself.

Writing both every time would be slow, so the symbol $\sqrt{\phantom{n}}$ is taken to mean the positive one:

$\sqrt{64} = 8, \qquad \sqrt{100} = 10, \qquad \sqrt{441} = 21$

When the negative root is wanted it is written $-\sqrt{64} = -8$, and when both are wanted, $\pm\sqrt{64} = \pm 8$. In this chapter we want the positive one all through, since a side length of $-8$ m is not a side length. So from here on the square root means the positive square root.

This is why $\sqrt{x^2}$ is not simply $x$. If $x = -5$ then $x^2 = 25$ and $\sqrt{25} = 5$, which is $-x$, not $x$. The square root symbol never returns a negative number.

That is what a root is. Given a number such as $529$, two questions are left: is it a perfect square at all, and if it is, what is its root? We have already met two methods that work.

Listing. Square $20, 21, 22, \ldots$ until you reach $529$ or pass it: $400, 441, 484, 529$. It works, but for a six-digit number it is hopeless.

Subtracting the odd numbers. From § 1.2.2 we know $529$ is a square exactly when subtracting $1, 3, 5, \ldots$ lands on zero. For $529$ that is twenty-three subtractions. It works too, and it is also slow.

The third method is the one to keep, and it comes from asking what a square looks like on the inside.
