To grow a square of side $n$ into one of side $n+1$, you add a column of $n$ down one side, a row of $n$ along another, and a single tile at the corner where they meet: $n + n + 1 = 2n + 1$ tiles, which is odd whatever $n$ is. That is the argument, and the picture is the argument — nothing has been left out of it.

The sum of the odd numbers

The first $n$ odd numbers add up to $n^2$: $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1) = n^2$. The $n$th odd number is $2n - 1$.

Keep both the picture and the formula. The picture is what convinces; the formula is what calculates. Asked for $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + 59$, you do not want to draw anything. You want to notice that $59 = 2 \times 30 - 1$, so this is the first thirty odd numbers, and the answer is $30^2 = 900$. Thirty numbers are added in one line, because you saw the shape of the sum instead of doing the sum.

That single fact does two jobs. The first is a test. If every square is $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots$, then subtracting the odd numbers in order from a square must land exactly on zero — and from anything else it must overshoot.

Example 2

Is $36$ a perfect square? Is $45$?

Solution.

Step 1 from 36: $35,\ 32,\ 27,\ 20,\ 11,\ 0$ zero after $6$ steps
Step 2 from 45: $44,\ 41,\ 36,\ 29,\ 20,\ 9,\ -4$ overshot, from $9$ to $-4$
Answer $36 = 6^2$ is a perfect square; $45$ is not

The count of steps is the root: the method does not only say yes, it says six. And $45$ is stuck between $6^2 = 36$ and $7^2 = 49$, with no square between them.
