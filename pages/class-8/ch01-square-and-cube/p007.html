Taking differences is a habit worth acquiring, and it will earn its keep well beyond this chapter. Faced with a list you do not understand, the useful question is often not what are these numbers? but by how much does each one exceed the last? A list that looks arbitrary can have differences that are perfectly orderly, and the differences are then the thing to explain. Here the squares — which grow in a lumpy, accelerating way — turn out to be built from a list as plain as the odd numbers.

$1 = 1$

$1 + 3 = 4$

$1 + 3 + 5 = 9$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25$

You could check another twenty lines and they would all work, and you would still not know why. The reason is best seen rather than argued.

To grow a square of side $n$ into one of side $n+1$, you add a column of $n$ down one side, a row of $n$ along another, and a single tile at the corner where they meet: $n + n + 1 = 2n + 1$ tiles, which is odd whatever $n$ is. That is the argument, and the picture is the argument — nothing has been left out of it.

The sum of the odd numbers

The first $n$ odd numbers add up to $n^2$: $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1) = n^2$. The $n$th odd number is $2n - 1$.

The picture and the formula are worth keeping both. The picture is what convinces; the formula is what calculates. Asked for $1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + 99$, you do not want to draw anything — you want to notice that $99 = 2 \times 50 - 1$, so this is the first fifty odd numbers, so the answer is $50^2 = 2500$. Fifty numbers added in one line, because the shape of the sum was recognised rather than the sum performed.
