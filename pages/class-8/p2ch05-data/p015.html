There are three other ways the same thing is done, and all three break a rule the chapter has already stated. Bars of different widths: the eye compares area, not height, so a bar twice as wide looks like twice as much before its height is considered at all. A line graph whose horizontal axis puts $1990$, $2000$ and $2005$ at equal spaces: ten years and five are then drawn the same length, and every slope on it is wrong. A pie chart whose slices come to less than a circle: the parts must be the whole, or the picture is not of a whole at all.

There is nothing clever about spotting any of them. Look at the axis before looking at the bars — where it starts, and whether its steps are even. It takes a few seconds, and it is the difference between reading a graph and being told what to think by one.

A cut axis is not always dishonest. A graph of body temperature through a fever would be a flat line if drawn from zero, and the interesting range is two degrees wide. The rule is that a bar's length must be proportional to its value, and a bar is what carries that promise — so cut the axis of a line graph when you must, and say so, but never of a bar graph.
