Bars would be wrong here. Six bars would say that the temperature stood at $22^\circ$ for three hours, then at $26^\circ$ for three hours, then jumped again — six flat stretches and five leaps, which is not what a morning does.

Because the line is unbroken it can be read at places where nothing was measured. At half past ten it stands a little over $28^\circ$, and that is a reasonable estimate of the temperature then. It is an estimate and not a measurement: the real temperature wandered about between the readings, and a straight segment from one dot to the next is the simplest guess, not the truth.

The same graph also settles what a table cannot. The steepest climb is between $9$ am and noon, and the eye reads a rate off a slope without subtracting anything. A table of the same six numbers holds that information and hides it.

Example 6

Using Fig. 5.4, find the mean of the six readings, and say why the hottest reading being at $3$ pm does not mean the day was hottest at $3$ pm.

Solution.

Step 1 the six readings: $22, 26, 31, 34, 30, 25$ read off the dots
Step 2 $168 \div 6 = 28^\circ$ the readings add to 168
Answer Mean $28^\circ$. The largest reading was at $3$ pm; the hottest moment is unknown

And the peak is a peak among the six readings only. Nobody looked between $12$ and $3$ pm, or between $3$ and $6$; the true maximum lies somewhere in that stretch and could be at any moment in it. A graph of six readings can say where the largest reading was, and no more.

Readings taken closer together give a line with more in it. Take one every quarter of an hour, and the segments joined at corners start to look like a curve. Nothing about the method changed. Only the number of dots did.

5.9Reading a Graph Honestly

A graph is quicker to look at than a table and slower to read properly, and the gap between those two is where mistakes live. It is worth doing deliberately, in two steps that are easy to run together and should not be.

First what is given, then what follows

Step one: see how the graph is organised. What is on each axis, in what unit, at what scale. Where the axis begins. Which points were actually measured and which were joined. Step two: and only then, say what the shape shows — where it rises, where it is steepest, which lines cross. Anything said in step two is only as good as what was noticed in step one.
