5.6When the Data Comes in a Table

The $30$ students of a class each say how many times they visited the school library last month. Their answers are written down in the order they are given.

$$3 \quad 2 \quad 4 \quad 1 \quad 3 \quad 5 \quad 2 \quad 3 \quad 6 \quad 4 \quad 3 \quad 1 \quad 2 \quad 4 \quad 3$$
$$5 \quad 2 \quad 3 \quad 4 \quad 1 \quad 3 \quad 6 \quad 2 \quad 4 \quad 5 \quad 3 \quad 1 \quad 4 \quad 2 \quad 5$$

Only six different answers appear, so we count them in one pass. For each answer, put a stroke in its row, and draw every fifth stroke across the four before it.

The number of times a value occurs is its frequency. A table of values and their frequencies is a frequency table. It keeps everything in the list except the order of the answers, which we do not need.

Now find the mean number of visits. One quick answer is to add the six different values and divide by six: $\frac{1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6}{6} = 3.5$. This is wrong. The mean is the sum of all thirty answers divided by thirty. The answer $3$ was given by eight students, so it must be counted eight times in the sum.

the sum $(1 \times 4) + (2 \times 6) + (3 \times 8) + (4 \times 6) + (5 \times 4) + (6 \times 2)$
which is $4 + 12 + 24 + 24 + 20 + 12 = 96$
the mean $96 \div 30 = 3.2$ visits

Each value is multiplied by its frequency before the values are added. Then we divide by the total of the frequencies, not by the number of rows. Forgetting either step is the most common mistake with a frequency table.

The median needs no multiplying, and we do not have to write the thirty answers in order. With thirty values, the median is the mean of the 15th and the 16th. Add up the frequencies from the top until those places are reached.
