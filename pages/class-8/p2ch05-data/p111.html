Set C, 6. Option (c), still $22$, assumes the readings all fall in one half of the class, which may happen but need not. Option (d) is impossible: narrowing a class can only share its readings out, never add to them.

Set C, 7. Options (a) and (b) are the two ways of getting this wrong, and they are opposite ways. One expects probability to control sixty particular throws; the other decides it says nothing at all. It says what to expect on average, and it says the average firmly enough to be worth trusting — over thousands of throws, not sixty.

What to carry forward

Every rule here runs backwards. A scale turns people into centimetres, and just as readily turns centimetres into people. An angle is a fraction of $360^\circ$, and a fraction of $360^\circ$ is an angle. A frequency table's counts add to the total, which is a check going forwards and an equation going backwards. Almost every question in this division was one of those rules read the other way, and the first move each time was to write the rule down in its familiar direction and then put the unknown where the answer usually stands.

Ask what the picture has thrown away. A frequency table has lost the order; a grouped table has lost the individual measurements; a pie chart has lost the total; a line graph between two readings has only a guess. None of these is a defect, and every one of them is a limit on what the picture can be asked. When a question about a graph looks unanswerable, check first whether the answer was thrown away on purpose — how many students are exactly $142$ cm has no answer, and saying so is the correct answer.

Read the axis before the bars. Where it starts, and whether its steps are even. It costs a couple of seconds and it is the single habit in this chapter most likely to be useful outside it, because a graph in a newspaper or an advertisement is drawn by somebody who would like you to reach a particular conclusion.

The chapter began with thirty numbers nobody could read and ended with a statement about a slip of paper not yet drawn out of a box. The road between them was one idea used over and over: give up some of what you know, in a chosen and stated way, and what remains becomes visible. A tally gives up the order. A class interval gives up the exact measurement. A probability gives up any claim about the next throw, and keeps a claim about the next ten thousand. Each of them is a fair trade, and knowing what was traded is what tells you when to believe the answer.
