Example 1

A die is thrown twenty times and the faces come up in this order. Make a frequency table.

$$3 \quad 6 \quad 1 \quad 4 \quad 3 \quad 2 \quad 6 \quad 5 \quad 3 \quad 1 \quad 4 \quad 6 \quad 2 \quad 3 \quad 5 \quad 4 \quad 6 \quad 3 \quad 1 \quad 2$$
Face123456
Times it came up335324

The counts add to $20$, so nothing has been dropped. They are not equal, and a reader may be tempted to conclude that the die favours a three. Twenty throws are far too few to conclude anything of the kind — which is a matter the last section of this chapter takes up properly.

5.3Bars You Can Compare

The frequency table is a great improvement on the raw list, but it still has to be read: to see that houses of five are commonest you must compare six numbers, one pair at a time. A bar graph does that comparison for you by turning each count into a length. Nine is taller than seven before you have thought about it at all.

Three decisions were made in drawing it, and none of them is free.

The bars are all the same width. Width carries no meaning here, so it must not vary; a wider bar looks like more even when it is no taller. What the reader is asked to compare is height, and only height.
