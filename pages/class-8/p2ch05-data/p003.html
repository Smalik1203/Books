Example 1

Check by balancing that the mean of $4$, $7$, $8$, $8$ and $18$ is $9$.

below 9 $5 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 9$
above 9 $9$

They agree, so $9$ is the mean — and no adding up of the five values and dividing by five was needed. The check works as a check because balance and mean are the same condition written two ways.

One thing the picture makes plain that the arithmetic hides: a single distant value can hold up a great many near ones. In Fig. 5.1 three dots huddled near $11$ are balanced by one dot at $17$, and if that one were dragged out to $37$ the mean would have to move nearly five places to keep up. A mean is not a promise that most of the data is near it.

5.3What Moves the Mean

Add a new value to a collection. Does the mean go up or down? The fair-share description gives no quick answer — the total changes and so does the number of shares, and which effect wins is not obvious. The balance picture answers it in a sentence.

A new dot to the right of the fulcrum adds to the right-hand total and nothing to the left, so the row tips that way and the fulcrum must slide right to restore it. A new dot to the left does the opposite. So including a value bigger than the mean raises the mean, and including one smaller lowers it — every time, whatever the rest of the data is doing.

A value exactly equal to the mean is the interesting case. It sits on the fulcrum, contributes nothing to either total, and leaves the balance where it was. Adding the mean to a collection changes the mean not at all. That is worth remembering, because it looks as though it should change something.

It has a use. A collection can be padded out to any size you like without disturbing its mean, simply by adding copies of the mean. That is how you check that a rule about means really is about the values, and not about how many of them there happen to be.

Two things the balance picture does not say, and both are worth having straight. The mean need not be one of the values: none of the four in Fig. 5.1 is $12$. It need not even be a value the situation allows, which is how a survey ends up reporting $2.4$ children per household without anybody owning four tenths of a child.
