5.2What Moves the Mean

Suppose a new value joins a set of numbers. Does the mean go up or down? The fair share does not answer this quickly. The total changes, and so does the number of shares. The balance point answers it at once.

A new dot to the right of the support adds to the right-hand total and adds nothing to the left. The row tips to the right, so the support has to move right to balance it again. A new dot to the left does the opposite. So a new value larger than the mean raises the mean, and a new value smaller than the mean lowers it. This is true every time, whatever the other values are.

A new value exactly equal to the mean sits on the support. It adds nothing to either total, so the balance does not change. Including a value equal to the mean leaves the mean unchanged.

Example 2

The mean of $6$, $6$, $7$ and $13$ is $8$. Which single value can be added without changing the mean? Which pairs of values can be added?

Solution.

Step 1 One new value must sit on the support
Step 2 So the single value is $8$, and no other
Step 3 A pair must balance itself at $8$: its own mean is $8$, so its sum is $16$
Answer The single value $8$; or any pair adding to $16$, such as $5$ and $11$

The pair $5$ and $11$ is $3$ below and $3$ above $8$, so it adds the same amount to each side. The single value is the case of this rule with one dot instead of two. Three new values would need a sum of $24$.

Removing a value works the other way round. Remove a value above the mean and the mean falls. Remove a value below the mean and the mean rises. Remove a value equal to the mean and the mean stays where it is. In each case, ask which side of the support the value was on.

The mean need not be one of the values. None of the four values in Fig. 5.1 is $8$. The mean need not even be a value that is possible. A survey can find that the homes in a village have $1.8$ bicycles each, although no home has $0.8$ of a bicycle.
