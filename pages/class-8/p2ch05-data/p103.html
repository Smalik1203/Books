There are fifteen values in all, so the sum must be $7 \times 15$. Writing the sum the frequency-table way puts the unknown in it.

the sum $(4 \times 6) + (x \times 9) = 24 + 9x$
and it must be $7 \times 15 = 105$
so $9x = 81$, giving $x = 9$

Nine values of $9$ and six of $4$ — and the mean of $7$ sits nearer the nine than the four, as it should, because there are more of them. A mean is pulled by how many as well as by how far away.

The twelve runners of Example 3 (section 5.3) had a true mean time of $60$ seconds. Two more runners are timed with a correct stopwatch, at $59$ s and $68$ s. What is the new mean time of all fourteen runners?

The correction has to be applied before the new runners are let in, because their times were taken correctly and the recorded mean was not.

the true sum $12 \times 60 = 720$ s
with the newcomers $720 + 59 + 68 = 847$
the new mean $847 \div 14 = 60.5$ s

One newcomer is below the old mean and one above, and between them they lift it by half a second. Their own mean is $63.5$, which is above $60$, so the rise was to be expected — and the rule from section 5.2 would have predicted its direction without any of this arithmetic.

The same monthly rainfall is drawn twice as a line graph: once with the vertical axis running from $0$ to $400$ mm, and once from $100$ to $400$ mm. What is the same in the two pictures and what is different? Which of the twelve months cannot be shown at all on the second graph?

Nothing about the data changes and nothing about the readings changes: every month still sits against its correct label, and a reader who looks at the numbers gets the same twelve either way. What changes is the look of the rises and falls. The second graph gives the same vertical space to a range of $300$ mm that the first gave to $400$, so every slope on it is a third steeper than it was.

And any month with under $100$ mm of rain falls off the bottom of the second graph altogether — which is the sharper problem, because a missing point is easier to overlook than an exaggerated slope.
