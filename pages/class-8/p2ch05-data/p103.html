The twenty-four students of Example 3 (section 5.4) had a true mean height of $149.2$ cm. Two more join the class and are measured barefoot at $149$ cm and $152$ cm. What is the new mean height?

The correction has to be applied before the new students are let in, because their heights have no shoes in them and the recorded average did.

the true sum $24 \times 149.2 = 3580.8$ cm
with the newcomers $3580.8 + 149 + 152 = 3881.8$
the new mean $3881.8 \div 26 = 149.3$ cm

One newcomer is below the old mean and one above, and between them they lift it by a tenth of a centimetre. Their own mean is $150.5$, which is above $149.2$, so the rise was to be expected — and the rule from section 5.3 would have predicted its direction without any of this arithmetic.

The same monthly rainfall is drawn twice as a line graph: once with the vertical axis running from $0$ to $400$ mm, and once from $100$ to $400$ mm. What is the same in the two pictures and what is different? Which of the twelve months cannot be shown at all on the second?

Nothing about the data changes and nothing about the readings changes: every month still sits against its correct label, and a reader who looks at the numbers gets the same twelve either way. What changes is the look of the rises and falls. The second graph gives the same vertical space to a range of $300$ mm that the first gave to $400$, so every slope on it is a third steeper than it was.

And any month with under $100$ mm of rain falls off the bottom of the second graph altogether — which is the sharper problem, because a missing point is easier to overlook than an exaggerated slope.

A shopkeeper's takings over seven days average ₹$4{,}000$. He then finds that Wednesday's figure was entered as ₹$2{,}400$ when it should have been ₹$4{,}500$. What is the corrected average?

Only one figure was wrong, so only the total needs mending — and the total is what the average was hiding.

the recorded total $7 \times 4{,}000 = 28{,}000$ rupees
the shortfall $4{,}500 - 2{,}400 = 2{,}100$ rupees
the true average $30{,}100 \div 7 = 4{,}300$ rupees

Compare it with Example 3 in section 5.4, where the error was in every reading and came off the mean whole. Here the error was in one reading out of seven, so it arrives at the mean divided by seven: ₹$2{,}100$ shared out is ₹$300$, which is exactly the distance from ₹$4{,}000$ to ₹$4{,}300$.
