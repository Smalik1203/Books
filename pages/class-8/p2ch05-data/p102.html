Why $18$ and not $13$? Because the newcomer has two jobs. It must bring its own share of $13$, and it must also bring enough to lift the five that were already there by one apiece — another $5$. A newcomer that brought only $13$ would have left the mean at $12$, which is the rule from section 5.2 about adding a value equal to the mean.

A dot plot has dots at $6$, $7$, $7$ and $9$, and one more dot that has been rubbed out. The mean of all five is $8$. Where was the missing dot?

The sum of five values with a mean of $8$ is $40$, and the four visible dots come to $29$, so the missing one is at $11$. The balance argument reaches it without any adding at all.

below 8 $6$ is $2$ below, and each $7$ is $1$ below — $4$ in all
above 8 $9$ is $1$ above, so $3$ more is needed
so the dot sits $3$ above $8$, at $11$

The second method is the one to reach for when the numbers are awkward. It never asks you to add up the whole collection, only to look at how far each dot is from a point you already have.

Nine values have a median of $20$ and a mean of $20$. Two of the values below $20$ are increased, one by $4$ and the other by $5$, and both are still below $20$ afterwards. What happens to the median, and what happens to the mean?

Nine values put the median in the fifth position. The two values that changed were below $20$, so they sat somewhere in the first four places. After the change they are still below $20$, so they still sit there. Nothing has crossed the middle, the fifth value is still $20$, and the median does not move.

the old sum $9 \times 20 = 180$
the new sum $180 + 4 + 5 = 189$
the new mean $189 \div 9 = 21$

The mean felt every unit of the change and rose by $1$. The median felt none of it, because it asks each value only which side of the middle it is on, and neither value changed sides.
