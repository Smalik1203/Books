5.4When There Are Too Many Values

The household data had six different answers for thirty students, so counting them into six rows was worth doing. Now measure the heights of forty students to the nearest centimetre. They run from $131$ cm to $154$ cm, and a frequency table with a row for every height would have twenty-four rows, most of them saying $1$ and several saying $0$. Such a table is longer than the raw list and no easier to read.

The trouble is that height does not come in six kinds. It varies continuously, and no two students need share a value at all, so counting equal values is counting almost nothing. What is worth counting is how many fall in each stretch of the range — and that means choosing the stretches first.

Grouping trades detail for shape

A class interval is a stretch of the range, and its length is the class size. Counting how many values fall in each gives a grouped frequency table. The individual measurements are gone from it — the table cannot say that anyone was $143$ cm — and in exchange the shape of the whole set becomes visible.
