5.7Choosing the Picture

Four kinds of graph have now appeared, counting the pie chart of Chapter 3. Which to use is decided by the question being asked, not by the data — the same numbers may deserve any of them.

The questionThe picture
Which of these separate things is biggest?bar graph
How do two sets compare, item by item?double bar graph
How is one measurement spread out?histogram
What share of the whole is each part?pie chart
How did one quantity change over time?line graph

The forty heights make the point. Asked how the class is spread, draw the histogram of Fig. 5.4. Asked what share of the class is under $145$ cm, a pie chart of two slices — $23$ against $17$ — answers better, and it is the same forty measurements. Asked how the tallest student's height changed over five years, no graph here will do, because the data does not contain it.

That last case is the one to watch. A picture cannot show what was never measured, and a graph drawn from data that does not answer the question will still look convincing.

Two of the five carry a warning with them. A pie chart shows shares and hides totals — the same chart serves a class of forty and a city of forty lakh, which is why Chapter 3 insisted that one be labelled with how many it counts. And a bar graph of two bars is a table with the numbers taken out: there is nothing to compare that the two numbers did not already say, and the reader now has to measure them off an axis to get them back.

That second warning generalises into a useful test. Before drawing anything, ask whether a reader could answer the question faster from the table. With three or four numbers the answer is usually yes, and then the table is the better picture: it is exact, it is quicker to make, and it does not ask anyone to measure a length to recover a figure that was written down in the first place. A graph earns its place when there are enough numbers that reading them one at a time has stopped working.

Whichever is chosen, the labelling rule of section 5.3 holds for all of them: a heading saying what is being shown, both axes named with their units, a stated scale, and — on a pie chart — the total the circle stands for. A picture with any of those missing is not a lighter picture. It is one the reader cannot check.
