The gaps are equal, and there are gaps. A house has three members or four and never three and a half, so the six answers are six separate things standing apart. The gap is what says so. Section 5.5 draws bars with no gaps at all, and the difference is exactly this.

The vertical axis starts at zero. A bar means what it means because its length is proportional to its count: a bar twice as tall stands for twice as many. Begin the axis anywhere but zero and that stops being true, and the graph will mislead a reader who is doing nothing wrong. Section 5.8 is about what happens then.

The remaining choice is the scale — how much of the page one student is worth. It is decided by the largest value and the space available, and then written on the axis, because a graph whose scale is not stated cannot be read at all.

Example 2

Four villages have populations $1{,}240$, $2{,}860$, $4{,}050$ and $3{,}310$. A bar graph is to be drawn in a space $9$ cm tall. What scale should be used?

tallest bar $4{,}050$ people, and it must fit in $9$ cm
so at least $4{,}050 \div 9 = 450$ people to the centimetre
take $1$ cm $= 500$ people, giving a tallest bar of $8.1$ cm

The exact answer, $450$, is the wrong scale to use even though it fits. A reader has to convert lengths back into people, and doing that in five-hundreds is easy while doing it in four-hundred-and-fifties is not. Round the scale up to a friendly number and lose a little height.

Two labels finish the job, and a graph without them is not finished. Each axis is named with what it counts and in what unit — students, rainfall in millimetres — and the graph is given a heading saying what the whole of it is about. A reader who comes to a graph cold has only what is printed on it, and bars alone say some things are bigger than other things.

One awkward case is worth naming. If one value dwarfs the rest — four towns of a few thousand and a fifth of two lakh — then a scale that fits the tallest bar flattens the other four into nothing, and there is no scale that serves both. The honest answer is that the five do not belong on one graph. Draw the four, and give the fifth in words.

Bars are sometimes laid on their sides, running from a vertical axis at the left. Nothing about the graph changes — it is the same lengths against the same scale — and the reason is practical: the names of eight districts will not fit side by side under eight upright bars, and they sit comfortably one under another at the left.
