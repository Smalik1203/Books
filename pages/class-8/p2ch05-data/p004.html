Removing values is the same argument run backwards. Take away a value above the mean and the mean falls. Take away one below it and the mean rises. Take away one equal to the mean and nothing moves.

The fair share gives the same answers once it is put the right way round. A new value above the mean brings in more than one share's worth of stuff while bringing in only one more person to share it, so there is a little over to go round and everybody's share rises. Below the mean, it brings in less than a share and everybody's falls. Exactly a share, and nothing changes hands.

How far the mean moves is worth knowing too, because it is usually less than people expect. Thirty households averaging $5.07$ are joined by one of $8$. The new total is $152 + 8 = 160$ over $31$ households, which is $5.16$ — the mean has moved about a tenth, not three. The excess of $8$ over the old mean is shared among all thirty-one, so a single value moves the mean by its own distance from the mean divided by the new count.

Which is a caution as well as a shortcut. The mean went up never means the values went up: not one of the original thirty households changed at all.

5.4Shifting Every Value

Now change every value at once. The five numbers $3$, $5$, $7$, $9$, $11$ have a mean of $7$. Add ten to each of them and they become $13$, $15$, $17$, $19$, $21$. Their mean is $17$ — and adding up the new five to find that out is doing more work than the question deserves.

Nothing about the arrangement was disturbed. Every dot moved ten places to the right, so every distance from every dot to every other dot is what it was, and the point that balanced them still balances them — ten places along.

The picture is convincing and algebra makes it certain. Let the values be $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$, with mean $a$, so that

$$\frac{x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}{n} = a.$$
