Two consequences come straight out of the fraction. The top can never exceed the bottom, so $P(E)$ is never more than $1$; and it cannot be negative, so it is never less than $0$. An event that must happen has every outcome in it and probability $1$; an event that cannot happen has none and probability $0$. Everything real lives between.

Example 6

A fair die is thrown once. Find the probability that the face is even, that it is more than $4$, that it is $7$, and that it is less than $7$.

even $2, 4, 6$ — three outcomes, so $\dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac12$
more than 4 $5, 6$ — two outcomes, so $\dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac13$
a seven no outcome at all, so $\dfrac{0}{6} = 0$
less than 7 all six outcomes, so $\dfrac{6}{6} = 1$

Every one of these was answered by counting faces. Nothing was thrown, and nothing needed to be — the six outcomes were equally likely by the shape of the die, and that is all the formula asks for.

An event and its opposite between them use up every outcome, so their probabilities add to $1$. A bag holds $3$ red marbles, $2$ blue and $5$ green; $P(\text{red}) = \tfrac{3}{10}$, and the probability of drawing something that is not red is $\tfrac{7}{10}$, which is $1 - \tfrac{3}{10}$. When an event is awkward to count and its opposite is easy, that subtraction is the shorter road.

Now look again at Example 1, where twenty throws of a die gave five threes and only two fives. Each face has probability $\tfrac16$, so about three or four of each were to be expected, and what turned up was neither. That is not evidence of a loaded die. Twenty throws are far too few, and a fair die is under no obligation to be tidy over twenty throws — or over twenty thousand, though by then the six counts will be very close to equal indeed.

This is the honest relation between the two halves of the chapter. Data records what happened; probability says what to expect; and the two agree in the long run and need not agree at all in the short one. A record of twenty throws is a fact about those twenty throws, and reading a verdict on the die out of it is the same mistake as reading a temperature off a graph at a time nobody measured.
