The mean is the balance point

Add up how far each value below the mean is from it. Add up how far each value above the mean is from it. The two totals are always equal.

Could another point balance the row as well? Try $9$. The two sixes are now $3$ away each and the seven is $2$ away, so the total on the left is $8$. The thirteen is only $4$ away. The totals do not match, and the row tips. Moving the point to the right always makes the left total bigger and the right total smaller. Moving it to the left does the opposite. So the totals are equal at one point only. The mean is the only balance point.

A line of algebra shows that the fair share and the balance point are the same number. Let the values be $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$. The balance holds at a point $m$ when the differences $x - m$, some positive and some negative, add up to zero. Their total is $(x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n) - nm$. This is zero exactly when $nm$ equals the sum of the values, that is, when $m$ is the sum divided by $n$.

Example 1

Check by balancing that the mean of $3$, $6$, $7$, $7$ and $17$ is $8$.

Solution.

Step 1 Below $8$: $5 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 9$
Step 2 Above $8$: $17 - 8 = 9$
Answer The two totals are equal, so the mean is $8$

We did not add the five values and divide by five. Balancing and dividing give the same answer, so either one can be used to check the other.

The picture shows something the arithmetic hides. One value far away can balance many values close together. In Fig. 5.1, three dots near $6$ are balanced by one dot at $13$. If that one dot moved out to $33$, the mean would move $5$ places, from $8$ to $13$. So a mean does not tell us that most of the values are close to it.
