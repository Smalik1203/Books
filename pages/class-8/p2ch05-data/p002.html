Measure how far each dot stands from $12$. The two tens are $2$ away each and the eleven is $1$ away, so the dots on the left are $2 + 2 + 1 = 5$ away in total. The seventeen, alone on the right, is $5$ away. The two totals match — and that is the sense in which $12$ is the centre.

The mean is where the distances balance

Add up how far every value below the mean falls short of it, and add up how far every value above it overshoots. The two totals are always equal. That is a second description of the same number the fair share gave, and for most of the questions in this chapter it is the more useful one.

Could there be a second such point? Suppose someone proposes $13$ instead. Every dot on the left is now further away than before — the two tens by one more each, the eleven by one more — so the total below has grown from $5$ to $8$. And the seventeen is now only $4$ away, so the total above has shrunk. The two no longer match, and the row tips.

The same thing happens for any point above $12$, and the mirror image for any point below it: moving left grows the right-hand total and shrinks the left. So the totals can agree in one place only. The mean is not merely a balancing point; it is the only one there is.

The two descriptions are the same statement in different clothes, and a line of algebra shows it. To say the balance holds at $m$ is to say that the overshoots and the shortfalls cancel — that adding up $x - m$ over every value gives nothing at all. Since there are $n$ values, that total is $(x_1 + \cdots + x_n) - nm$, so it is zero exactly when $nm$ is the sum, which is exactly when $m$ is the sum divided by $n$.

Example 1

Check by balancing that the mean of $4$, $7$, $8$, $8$ and $18$ is $9$.

Solution.

Step 1 below 9: $5 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 9$ four values fall short
Step 2 above 9: $18$ overshoots by $9$ one value above
Answer The two totals agree, so the mean is $9$

No adding up of the five values and dividing by five was needed. The check works as a check because balance and mean are the same condition written two ways.

One thing the picture makes plain that the arithmetic hides: a single distant value can hold up a great many near ones. In Fig. 5.1 three dots huddled near $11$ are balanced by one dot at $17$, and if that one were dragged out to $37$ the mean would have to move five places to keep up. A mean is not a promise that most of the data is near it.
