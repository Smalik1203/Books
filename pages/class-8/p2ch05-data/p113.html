7 (a) averages $12$ and $5$, the two middle values of the list as printed; the list has to be sorted first. (c) and (d) are single values from the list, but six values have no single middle.
8 (a) assumes a new value leaves the mean alone; only a value equal to the mean does. (c) averages the old mean with the new value as though each counted once. $17$ is $7$ above the mean, and $7$ shared among seven values is $1$.
12 A is true: the values left add up to the mean times the new count. R is false: $3, 6, 8, 9, 11, 14$ has a median of $8.5$, which is not one of them. So the answer is (c).
13 Both are true: the middle of the five is $3$, and the mean is $110 \div 5 = 22$. But the mean has no say in where the middle is, so R does not explain A: (b).
14 $4$ is the mean of the two values. Counted with their frequencies the sum is $10 + 6 = 16$ over six values, which is less than $3$. A is false and R is true: (d).

Three habits from this division will last longer than its questions. An average is a total in disguise: multiply the mean by the count and the sum comes back, and a missing value, a wrong entry or a newcomer becomes an equation with one unknown. A median is a position: sort first, count to the middle, and ask only which side a change falls on. And a graph offers what was measured, joined by a line, so between two readings the honest answer is often not settled.
