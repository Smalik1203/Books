How often a value occurs is its frequency, and the table of those counts is a frequency table. It is a shorter thing than the raw list and holds everything the raw list holds, except the order the answers arrived in — which nobody wanted.

Now find the average household size. There is a tempting short answer — add the six different sizes and divide by six, giving $\tfrac{33}{6} = 5.5$ — and it is wrong. The mean is the sum of all thirty answers divided by thirty, and a household of five was given as an answer nine times over, so it must count nine times.

the sum $(3 \times 4) + (4 \times 7) + (5 \times 9) + (6 \times 5) + (7 \times 3) + (8 \times 2)$
which is $12 + 28 + 45 + 30 + 21 + 16 = 152$
the mean $152 \div 30 = 5.07$, to two places

Each value is multiplied by its own frequency before anything is added, and the divisor is the total of the frequencies rather than the number of rows. That is the whole adjustment, and forgetting it is the most common mistake made with a frequency table.

The median needs no multiplying at all, and it does not need the thirty answers written out in order either. There are thirty values, so the median is the average of the fifteenth and sixteenth. Add the frequencies from the top until those positions are reached.

Household size345678
Students479532
Running total41120252830

The running total reaches $11$ after the fours and $20$ after the fives, so positions $12$ to $20$ in the sorted list are all fives. The fifteenth and sixteenth are among them, and the median is $5$.

The running total is worth building whenever a median is wanted from a table. It answers what is the value in position $n$? for any $n$ at once, so the same column gives the median, the quarter marks, or anything else that is defined by a position rather than by a total.
