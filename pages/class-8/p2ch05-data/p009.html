The same idea joins two groups into one. The mean of the whole group is not always the mean of the two means.

Example 7

In a test, the $24$ students of Class 8A have a mean mark of $60$, and the $26$ students of Class 8B have a mean mark of $65$. Find the mean mark of all $50$ students.

Solution.

Step 1 Class 8A: $24 \times 60 = 1440$ marks. Class 8B: $26 \times 65 = 1690$ marks
Step 2 All $50$ students: $1440 + 1690 = 3130$ marks
Step 3 $3130 \div 50 = 62.6$
Check $62.6$ lies between the two class means, $60$ and $65$, as it must
Answer The mean mark of all $50$ students is $62.6$

The mean of the two means is $62.5$, which is a little too low. Class 8B has more students, so it pulls the mean of the whole group a little closer to its own mean of $65$. The mean of the two means is right only when the two groups are the same size.

The same method works for three or more groups. Find the total for each group, add the totals, and divide by the number of values in all the groups together. The mean of the means will not do.

The mean carries the total

The mean multiplied by the number of values gives the total. From the total we can find a missing value, correct a wrong one, or join two groups into one.
