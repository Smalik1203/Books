What this chapter established
  1. Raw data is a list in the order it arrived, and nothing can be seen in it. Every step in this chapter throws something away in order to show something else.
  2. A frequency table counts how often each answer appears. Tally the list in one pass, in bundles of five, and check that the counts add back to the number of readings.
  3. A bar graph turns counts into lengths. Equal widths, equal gaps, a stated scale, and an axis that starts at zero.
  4. A double bar graph puts two sets on one pair of axes. The bars of a pair touch and the pairs stand apart, so spacing says what belongs with what.
  5. When a measurement varies continuously, count it into class intervals instead. A class takes its lower end and not its upper, so $135$–$140$ means $135 \le h < 140$ and every value belongs to exactly one class.
  6. A histogram draws a grouped table. Its bars touch because the classes do, its axis is a number line, and its bars cannot be reordered — which a bar graph's can.
  7. A line graph joins readings taken over time, and the line may be read between them. That reading is an estimate; on a linear graph, where the rate is fixed, it is exact. A straight line through the origin means direct proportion; a straight line that misses the origin does not.
  8. The question decides the picture: separate amounts take a bar graph, a spread takes a histogram, shares of one whole take a pie chart, and change over time takes a line graph.
  9. A bar's length is proportional to its value, so an axis that does not start at zero, or bars of unequal width, will mislead a reader who has done nothing wrong. Read the axis first.
  10. When outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes in it divided by the total. It lies between $0$ and $1$, and an event and its opposite have probabilities adding to $1$.

Data and probability answer different questions and are constantly mistaken for each other. A table says what happened; a probability says what to expect. They come together only over a great many trials, and a small record that disagrees with a probability is not evidence against it.
