5

Dots, and the Lines Between Them

5.1Introduction

Last year the mean was met as a fair share. Thirty rupees among five children is six each, and the mean is what everyone would get if the pile were levelled out. That is a true description and a useful one, and this chapter begins by looking at the same number from a completely different side.

Lay the values out as dots on a number line and the mean turns out to be the point where the row balances. It is the place where the pull of everything on the left exactly matches the pull of everything on the right. Nothing about fair shares suggests that, and once it is seen, a whole set of questions become easy that were not easy before. What happens to the mean if one more value is added? If every value goes up by ten? If somebody's measurement was wrong by a centimetre?

The second half of the chapter takes the dots and joins them. When the thing being measured changes over time — a temperature through a day, a price over ten years — the gaps between the readings mean something, and a line drawn through them says what. Reading such a line is a skill in itself, and so is knowing when it is saying less than it appears to.

5.2The Point That Balances

Start with two numbers. The mean of $3$ and $7$ is $5$; the mean of $8$ and $9$ is $8.5$. Both answers sit exactly halfway between the two values, and it is worth asking why. Halving the sum is halving the total, and for two numbers that is the same as standing midway.

Three or more numbers spoil that description at once. The mean of $10$, $10$, $11$ and $17$ is

$$\frac{10 + 10 + 11 + 17}{4} = \frac{48}{4} = 12,$$

and $12$ is not halfway between the smallest and the largest, which would be $13.5$. Nor is it one of the values. So in what sense is it the centre of anything?
