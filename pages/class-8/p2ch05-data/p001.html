5

Tales by Dots and Lines

5.1The Point That Balances

In earlier classes you met the mean as a fair share. Five friends have ₹40 between them. If they share it equally, each gets ₹8, so the mean is ₹8. In this chapter we look at the mean in a second way. We also meet a second kind of average, and then we learn to draw and read graphs of things that change over time.

Start with two numbers. The mean of $4$ and $10$ is $7$, and $7$ is exactly halfway between them. The mean of $5$ and $6$ is $5.5$, again halfway. For two numbers, the mean is always the point halfway between them.

With three or more numbers this is no longer true. The mean of $6$, $6$, $7$ and $13$ is

$$\frac{6 + 6 + 7 + 13}{4} = \frac{32}{4} = 8.$$

Halfway between the smallest value, $6$, and the largest, $13$, is $9.5$, not $8$. And $8$ is not one of the four values. So in what way is $8$ the centre of these numbers?

Think of each value as a dot of the same weight on a number line, as in Fig. 5.1. Now measure how far each dot is from $8$. The two sixes are $2$ away each and the seven is $1$ away. So the dots on the left are $2 + 2 + 1 = 5$ away in all. The thirteen, alone on the right, is also $5$ away. The two totals are equal. This is the sense in which $8$ is the centre: the row of dots balances there.
