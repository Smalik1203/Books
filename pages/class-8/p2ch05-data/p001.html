5

Reading a Thousand Numbers

5.1Introduction

A teacher asks each of her thirty students how many people live in their house, and writes the answers down in the order they are called out.

$$5 \quad 4 \quad 6 \quad 5 \quad 3 \quad 7 \quad 4 \quad 5 \quad 8 \quad 4 \quad 6 \quad 5 \quad 3 \quad 4 \quad 5$$
$$6 \quad 7 \quad 4 \quad 5 \quad 3 \quad 8 \quad 5 \quad 4 \quad 6 \quad 5 \quad 3 \quad 7 \quad 4 \quad 6 \quad 5$$

Every number the class needs is in those two lines, and nothing at all can be seen in them. Is a house of four commoner than a house of six? Are big houses rare? A reader can answer neither question by looking, and this is only thirty numbers. A census of a district has forty lakh.

Numbers collected like this are called data, and the list as it comes is raw data — in the order it happened to arrive, which is no order at all. This chapter is about the two things that are done to raw data to make it readable. The first is to count it into groups. The second is to draw it, because the eye can compare six heights in an instant and cannot compare six numbers at all.

The last section turns the question round. A table records what did happen; the reader will want to know what is likely to happen next, and the two are not the same question. That is where the chapter ends.

Nothing here is peculiar to a classroom. A weather station writes down a temperature every hour, a hospital ward a pulse every four, a shopkeeper the day's takings every evening; a census writes down a household at a time until it has written down the country. All of them end with a list nobody can read, and all of them do to it what this chapter does to thirty numbers. The methods do not change with the size of the list. Only the counting gets handed to a machine.

One warning belongs at the start rather than the end. A picture is easier to believe than a table, and easier to believe than it deserves — a reader who would check a column of figures will accept a bar graph at a glance. Section 5.8 is about what can be done with that, and it is the section a reader of newspapers will use most often.
