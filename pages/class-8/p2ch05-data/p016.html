5.8Reading a Graph Carefully

A graph is quick to look at but takes care to read. Many mistakes come from reading it too fast. It helps to read it in two separate steps.

First what is given, then what follows

Step one: see how the graph is made. What is on each axis, in what unit and at what scale? Where does each axis begin? Which points were measured, and which were only joined? Step two: only then, say what the shape shows: where it rises, where it is steepest, where two lines cross. What you say in step two can only be as good as what you noticed in step one.

Here is why step one matters. A dairy collected $188$, $190$, $193$ and $196$ thousand litres of milk in four years. Both graphs in Fig. 5.5 show these four numbers. Each bar is drawn correctly against its own axis, and no number has been changed.

The left graph says that the dairy's collection hardly changed. The right graph seems to say that the last bar is five times the first. A reader who skipped step one will believe it, because we read bars by comparing their lengths. But a bar tells the truth only when its length is in proportion to its value. An axis that starts at $186$ cuts the same $186$ off every bar. The first bar keeps $2$ of its $188$ and the last keeps $10$ of its $196$. So a rise of about four per cent looks like five times as much.

An axis that does not start at zero is not always wrong. A graph of a patient's body temperature through a fever would be an almost flat line if it started at zero, because the useful range is only a few degrees. The rule is about bars, because a bar's length stands for the quantity. A line graph may start its axis above zero if it says so clearly. A bar graph should always start at zero.
