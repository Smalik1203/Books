5.9What Has Not Happened Yet

Everything so far has been a record. The tally, the histogram and the line graph all report what was counted or measured, and none of them says a word about tomorrow. But a record is very often collected in order to say something about tomorrow, and the step from one to the other is worth taking slowly.

Go back to the thirty students. Suppose the teacher folds the thirty names into a box and draws one out without looking. Nine of the thirty live in a house of five, so nine of the thirty slips would give that answer, and every slip is as likely to be drawn as every other. The chance of drawing one of those nine is $\tfrac{9}{30}$, which is $\tfrac{3}{10}$. A table of what happened has become a statement about what is about to.

The language for this is worth fixing. Drawing a slip is an experiment; each of the thirty slips is an outcome; and the student lives in a house of five is an event — a collection of outcomes, nine of them here. The whole argument rests on one condition, that the thirty outcomes are equally likely, and that is what drawing without looking is for.

Probability counts outcomes, not events

When every outcome of an experiment is equally likely, the probability of an event is

$$P(E) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } E} {\text{total number of outcomes}}.$$

Both numbers count outcomes. The condition above the line is not negotiable: with outcomes that are not equally likely, this fraction is not the probability of anything.
