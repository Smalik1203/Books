Using What You Know

Almost none of the questions below give you a list of numbers to average. They give an average and ask for a number, or give two averages and ask what happened in between — which is the same arithmetic entered from the other end.

The thing to hold on to is that a mean and a count between them carry the sum. Once the sum is written down, most of these become one line. Try each before reading what follows it.

Each is answered straight after it. Most of them can be done twice — once by finding the sum and once by thinking about what the change does to the balance — and where both are short, both are given.

A collection of eight values has a mean of $15$. The value $22$ is removed from it. What is the new mean?

The direct route is to recover the sum, take the value away, and divide by what is left.

the sum $8 \times 15 = 120$
without the 22 $120 - 22 = 98$ over $7$ values
the new mean $98 \div 7 = 14$

The balance route says the same thing faster. The value $22$ stood $7$ above the mean, and removing it takes those $7$ out of the pile — leaving $7$ less to be shared among the $7$ that remain, which is exactly $1$ less each. The mean falls by $1$, from $15$ to $14$.

Five numbers have a mean of $12$. A sixth number is added and the mean becomes $13$. What is the sixth number?

Both sums are available, and their difference is the answer.

before $5 \times 12 = 60$
after $6 \times 13 = 78$
the new value $78 - 60 = 18$

Why $18$ and not $13$? Because the newcomer has two jobs. It must bring its own share of $13$, and it must also bring enough to lift the five that were already there by one apiece — another $5$. A newcomer that brought only $13$ would have left the mean at $12$, which is the rule from section 5.3 about adding a value equal to the mean.
