5.8Lines for Things That Change

Every collection so far has been a heap. The thirty household sizes could have been collected in any order and the mean and the median would be the same, which is exactly why a frequency table was allowed to throw the order away.

Some data is not like that. Think of a temperature taken every three hours, a price recorded every year, a patient's pulse through a night. Here the readings come in an order that is part of the information. The thing being measured also has a value at every moment in between, not only at the moments somebody wrote it down. That is what allows the dots to be joined.

Bars would be wrong here, and it is worth seeing why. Six bars would say that the temperature stood at $22^\circ$ for three hours, then at $26^\circ$ for three hours, then jumped again — six flat stretches and five leaps, which is not what a morning does. Every reading would be carried correctly and the picture would describe a day nobody has lived through.

Because the line is unbroken it can be read at places where nothing was measured. At half past ten it stands a little over $28^\circ$, and that is a reasonable estimate of the temperature then. It is an estimate and not a measurement: the real temperature wandered about between the readings, and a straight segment from one dot to the next is the simplest guess, not the truth.

The same graph also settles what could not be settled from a table. The steepest climb is between $9$ am and noon, and steepness is the whole point of the picture — the eye reads a rate off a slope without being asked to subtract anything. A table of the same six numbers holds that information and hides it.
