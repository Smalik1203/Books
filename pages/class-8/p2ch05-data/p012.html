That corner is worth a second look, because Chapter 3 has already met it under another name. The lower line passes through the origin, which says that no distance costs nothing and that double the distance costs double the fare: fare and distance are in direct proportion. The upper line is just as straight and does not pass through the origin, and for it none of that is true — $8$ km costs ₹$160$ while $4$ km costs ₹$100$, which is not half.

Straight is not the same as proportional

A straight line says the quantity rises at a fixed rate. A straight line through the origin says more: that the two quantities are in direct proportion, so their quotient is the same everywhere on the line. Any fixed charge added at the start lifts the line off the corner and destroys the proportion while leaving the line perfectly straight.

Example 4

Use Fig. 5.6 to find both fares for a journey of $5$ km, and check them by arithmetic.

off the graph at $5$ km the lines stand at about ₹$75$ and about ₹$115$
by arithmetic $15 \times 5 = 75$, and $40 + 75 = 115$

The graph and the arithmetic agree, as they must — the line was drawn from the rule. What the graph adds is everything at once: a reader can see at a glance that the gap between the two fares never closes, because a fixed charge of ₹$40$ stays ₹$40$ however far you go.

A linear graph can also be read backwards, which the earlier graphs could not be. Asked what distance ₹$90$ buys, go across from $90$ on the fare axis to the lower line and down: $6$ km. The same question on the upper line lands at a little over $3$ km, and the difference between the two answers is the fixed charge making itself felt.

Notice what has quietly changed. Fig. 5.5 was drawn from six measurements and says nothing about a time nobody stood at the thermometer. Fig. 5.6 was drawn from a rule, and the rule holds at every distance, so the line is not an estimate of anything — it is the whole fare table, printed as a line, for journeys nobody has yet taken.

It is the whole fare table only where the rule holds, which is a shorter stretch than the paper. Beyond thirty or forty kilometres nobody is charging by this rule at all, and the line, which will happily be drawn to the edge of the page, says nothing about that. A straight line invites you to continue it; the rule behind it decides how far you may.
