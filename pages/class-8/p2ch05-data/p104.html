That last comparison is the thread through all eight. An average is a total in disguise, and most of these questions were answered by taking the disguise off — multiplying the mean by the count, doing whatever the question described to the total, and dividing back.

The other half of them had a second, shorter road: ask what the change does to the balance and how many values have to absorb it. Where the numbers are ugly that road is much the better one, and it is the one the chapter's picture of a fulcrum was for.

Solved Examples

Type 1 · The mean as a balance point

Example 1

Without adding the values, decide whether $14$ is the mean of $9$, $12$, $13$, $15$ and $21$.

Solution.

Step 1 below 14: $5 + 2 + 1 = 8$ distances of 9, 12, 13
Step 2 above 14: $1 + 7 = 8$ distances of 15, 21
Answer The two totals balance, so $14$ is the mean

A check by adding agrees: $70 \div 5 = 14$.

Type 2 · Including and removing values

Example 2

Nine values have a mean of $40$. A tenth value, $60$, is included. Find the new mean.

Solution.

Step 1 $9 \times 40 = 360$ the old sum
Step 2 $360 + 60 = 420$ the new sum
Step 3 $420 \div 10 = 42$ ten values now
Answer The new mean is $42$

By balance: $60$ is $20$ above the old mean, and $20 \div 10 = 2$ is the rise.
