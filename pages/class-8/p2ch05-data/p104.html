Two answers, because there are two axes

The height above the axis is what the scale converts, and the axis itself is what that height is measured from. Both are needed.

the value $40 + 4 \times 5 = 60$
from zero $60 \div 5 = 12$ cm

So two thirds of the bar has been cut away, and every other bar on the graph has lost the same $8$ cm. Losing the same amount from each is precisely what destroys the comparison — the differences survive and the ratios do not.

What splitting a class can and cannot do

Each class of width $10$ is cut into two of width $5$, and the $22$ readings in the tallest one are shared between its two halves. Nothing is added and nothing is lost, so the two halves must come to $22$ between them.

most it can be $22$, if every one of the readings falls in the same half
least it can be $11$, if they split evenly

And no bar anywhere else can beat it, since every other class of width $10$ held fewer than $22$ to begin with. So the tallest bar of the new histogram is somewhere between $11$ and $22$, and the table alone will not say where. Narrower classes never raise a bar; they only ever share one out.

A probability read as a proportion

$P(\text{red}) = \tfrac37$ says that three of every seven counters are red, so the $12$ red counters are three sevenths of the bag.

one seventh $12 \div 3 = 4$ counters
the whole bag $7 \times 4 = 28$ counters
so blue $28 - 12 = 16$

The last two lines can be had in one step from the other direction: $P(\text{blue}) = 1 - \tfrac37 = \tfrac47$, and four sevenths of $28$ is $16$. Either road is short. What made the question look hard is only that the probability was given and the count was wanted.
