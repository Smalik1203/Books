Once we know the rule, the strip is easy to read. Kabir slept from midnight until half past five, and again from half past nine at night. He travelled twice, for half an hour each way. School ran from half past seven until two, with a half-hour lunch break at half past twelve. None of this is written in words anywhere on the strip.

Example 10

Use Fig. 5.6 to find how many hours Kabir was awake. What fraction of his waking time did he spend in lessons at school, not counting lunch?

Solution.

Step 1 Awake: $48 - (11 + 5) = 32$ boxes, which is $16$ hours
Step 2 Lesson boxes: $10 + 2 = 12$, which is $6$ hours
Step 3 Fraction of waking time: $\frac{12}{32} = \frac{3}{8}$
Answer Kabir was awake for $16$ hours and spent $\frac{3}{8}$ of that time in lessons

Counting boxes was all the arithmetic we needed. Each box is the same length of time, so a count of boxes is a length of time.

What makes the strip readable is its key: one box is half an hour, and one colour is one activity. Without the key it is only a band of colours. A line graph gives its rule along its axes, a coloured map gives its rule in a colour scale, and a strip gives its rule in its key. In every case the reader's first job is to find the rule. This is step one of Section 5.8 again.

Strips for three days, drawn one under another, show more than three separate days. On two school days the school boxes would line up. On a Sunday they would be missing, and that alone would show which day was the Sunday. Pictures are good for comparing, and that is why every strip must use boxes of the same size.

Every picture has a rule

A picture of data can be read only through its rule: the axes and scale of a graph, or the key of a strip or a map. Find the rule first.
