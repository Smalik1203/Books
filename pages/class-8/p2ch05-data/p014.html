5.7Lines for Things That Change

In the library data, the order of the answers did not matter. The thirty answers could have come in any order, and the mean and the median would be the same. That is why the frequency table could leave the order out.

Some data is different. Think of the temperature read every three hours, the price of rice each year, or a baby's mass each month. Here the readings come in an order, and the order is part of the information. The quantity also has a value at every moment between the readings, not only at the moments someone wrote it down. This is why we can join the dots.

The weather club of a school reads a thermometer every three hours through one day. Fig. 5.4 shows its six readings, joined by straight lines.

A bar graph would be wrong here. Six bars would say that the temperature stayed at $21^\circ$ for three hours, then jumped to $25^\circ$ and stayed there, and so on. That is six flat steps and five sudden jumps, and the temperature does not change like that.

Because the line has no gaps, we can read it at times when nothing was measured. At half past ten the line is halfway between $25^\circ$ and $30^\circ$, so it stands at about $27.5^\circ$. This is a fair estimate of the temperature then. It is an estimate, not a measurement. The real temperature may have gone up and down between the readings. A straight line from one dot to the next is the simplest guess, not a fact.

The graph also shows things that a table hides. The steepest part of the line is between $9$ am and noon, so that is when the temperature rose fastest. We can see this from the slope, without subtracting anything.

We can also put a number on how fast it changed. From $9$ am to noon the temperature rose from $25^\circ$ to $30^\circ$, which is $5^\circ$ in $3$ hours, or about $1.7^\circ$ an hour. From noon to $3$ pm it rose only $3^\circ$, which is $1^\circ$ an hour. The steeper the line, the faster the change.
