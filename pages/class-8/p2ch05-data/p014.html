Example 5

What picture would you draw for each of these, and why?

a shop's daily takings for one month a line graph — one quantity, day after day
how a village of 900 divides between six castes of work a pie chart — parts of one whole, labelled with the $900$
the ages of those 900 people a histogram — one measurement, grouped in tens of years

The middle one could be drawn as a bar graph instead, and would then answer a different question — which kind of work is commonest, rather than what share each takes. Neither is wrong. The question decides.

5.8A Graph That Tells the Truth

A shop's sales over four years were $96$, $98$, $101$ and $104$ lakh rupees. Below are two bar graphs of those four numbers. Every bar in both is drawn to its own axis correctly, and no figure has been altered.

The left graph says the shop is steady. The right graph says the last bar is five times the first, and a reader who does not stop to look at the axis will believe it, because that is what bars are for — comparing lengths without thinking.

What has gone wrong is exactly the rule from section 5.3. A bar means what it means because its length is proportional to its value, and an axis that starts at $94$ chops off nine tenths of every bar. The first bar loses $94$ of its $96$ and keeps $2$; the last loses $94$ of its $104$ and keeps $10$. Five times, from a rise of about eight per cent.
