5.5Bars That Touch

Drawn as bars, this table becomes a histogram. It looks almost like a bar graph and differs in one visible way: the bars touch.

The touching is not a matter of style. In Fig. 5.2 the gaps said that a household of four and a household of five are two separate answers with nothing in between. Here there is something in between: a height of $139.6$ cm is a real height, and it belongs to the second bar. The classes butt against each other and cover the range without a break, so the bars must do the same. A gap would be a claim that no student is that tall.

A second difference follows from the first, and it is the one that settles any doubt about which kind of graph you are looking at. The bars of a bar graph may be put in any order — alphabetical, tallest first, or the order the survey happened to use — because the horizontal axis is only a row of labels. The bars of a histogram may not be moved at all. Its horizontal axis is a number line, and $150$ has to come after $145$ for the same reason it does on a ruler.

One number is often wanted from a class in place of the class itself — for a rough average, or to plot a single point. The number used is the class mark, the halfway point between the two ends: for $140$–$145$ it is $142.5$, found as $(140 + 145) \div 2$. It is a stand-in and not a measurement, and it is the fairest one available, because within its class the table gives no reason to prefer any value to any other.

It is also how a rough average is got out of a grouped table. Take each class mark, multiply it by the frequency of its class, add the five products and divide by $40$. The answer is not the true average of the forty heights — those are gone — but it is close, and it is the best the table can do.
