Two sets on one pair of axes

Two years of rainfall, or two sections' marks, are usually asked about together: the question is not what either one is but how they differ. Drawing two graphs side by side answers it badly — the eye must carry a height across the gap between them. Put the pairs together instead, and the comparison becomes a glance.

Read the pairs across and one thing is plain at once: $2024$ was the drier year in June, July and September. Read them again and a second thing appears that is easy to miss — August went the other way, and by a wide margin. A reader who glances at the graph and says a bad monsoon in 2024 has seen three of the four months and stopped.

The totals settle it: $1{,}000$ mm against $925$ mm, so the season really was drier, but only by about a fourteenth, and all of the shortfall and more came in the months on either side of August. That total is not on the graph. It never is — a bar graph shows the parts, and any question about the whole is arithmetic the reader has to do.

In a double bar graph the spacing carries meaning. The two bars of a pair touch, because they belong to the same month; the pairs stand apart, because the months do not. Space the eight bars evenly and the graph still contains all the numbers, and the reader can no longer see which belongs with which.
