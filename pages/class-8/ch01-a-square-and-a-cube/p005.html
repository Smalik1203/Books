Zeros at the end, and parity

Now look at the numbers that end in zeros.

$10^2 = 100, \quad 20^2 = 400, \quad 40^2 = 1600, \quad 100^2 = 10000, \quad 200^2 = 40000, \quad 700^2 = 490000$

A number ending in one zero has a square ending in two; a number ending in two zeros has a square ending in four. The zeros double. So a perfect square can end in two zeros, or four, or six — but never in one, or three, or any odd number of them. A number such as 4000 or 250 is not a square, and no counting of factors is needed to say so.

Perfect squares and odd numbers

Take the differences between consecutive squares:

$4 - 1 = 3, \quad 9 - 4 = 5, \quad 16 - 9 = 7, \quad 25 - 16 = 9, \quad 36 - 25 = 11$

The differences are the odd numbers, in order. Turn that round and it says that adding the odd numbers from 1 gives the squares, in order:

$1 = 1 = 1^2$

$1 + 3 = 4 = 2^2$

$1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = 3^2$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 = 4^2$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 25 = 5^2$

$1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 = 36 = 6^2$

You met this pattern in Grade 6. The picture says why it never stops (Fig. 1.2). Each square of dots grows into the next by an inverted L along two of its sides — one new column and one new row, sharing the corner dot. A $3 \times 3$ square needs $3 + 3 + 1 = 7$ new dots to become $4 \times 4$; an $n \times n$ square needs $n + n + 1 = 2n + 1$, which is always odd, and is the next odd number each time.
