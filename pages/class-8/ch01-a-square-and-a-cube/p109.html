Why the other options are wrong

A4 $9261 = 3^3 \times 7^3 = 21^3$. It can be seen without factorising: the number ends in $1$, so its cube root does too, and it lies between $8000 = 20^3$ and $27000 = 30^3$. That leaves $21$ alone.
B4 The slab is $0.5$ m, so $60$ slabs fit along each side and $60^2 = 3600$ cover the garden. Option (b) is $30^2$, the area in square metres, which is the count only if the slabs were $1$ m across; (c) doubles the side rather than squaring it.
B8 A unit cube with exactly one painted face comes from the middle of a face, never from an edge or a corner. Each face of a cube of side $4$ has a $2 \times 2$ middle, so $4$ per face and $24$ in all. Option (c) counts the edge cubes, which have two painted faces.
C6 $n^2 + n = n(n + 1)$, a product of two consecutive numbers. Past $0$, it sits strictly between $n^2$ and $(n + 1)^2$, and there is no square there. So only $n = 0$ works. Trying $n = 1$ gives $2$, which is not a square, which rules out (b) and with it (c) and (d).
C9 $41^2 = 1681$ and $42^2 = 1764$, so $1750$ sits between them and the next square above it is $1764$. The gap is $14$. Option (c) is the distance down to $1681$, which would mean subtracting rather than adding; (d) reaches $1786$, which is past the next square altogether.
What to take away
  1. The prime factorisation answers most questions about squares and cubes without any multiplication. Primes in pairs make a square, primes in triplets a cube, and what is left over is exactly what must be supplied or removed.
  2. The last digit and the number of zeros can prove that a number is not a square. Nothing about the last digit can prove that it is, and no last digit at all rules out a cube.
  3. Consecutive squares are $2n + 1$ apart, and that gap grows. Knowing it settles how many numbers lie between two squares, and it shows that a number just above a square cannot be one.
