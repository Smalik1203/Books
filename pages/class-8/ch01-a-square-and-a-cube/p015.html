$\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^3 = \dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{8}{27}, \qquad (1.2)^3 = 1.2 \times 1.2 \times 1.2 = 1.728, \qquad (-6)^3 = (-6) \times (-6) \times (-6) = -216$

Notice the last one. Two negatives multiply to a positive, and a third negative turns it back: the cube of a negative number is negative. A square never is. This is a real difference between the two, and we will meet it again with roots.

Taxicab numbers

In 1918 Srinivasa Ramanujan was working with G. H. Hardy at the University of Cambridge, and fell ill. Hardy came to visit him in hospital, and remarked that the taxi he had ridden in was numbered 1729 — rather a dull number, he said, and he hoped it was not a bad omen.

Ramanujan answered at once. No, Hardy, it is a very interesting number. It is the smallest number that can be expressed as the sum of two cubes in two different ways.

$1729 = 1^3 + 12^3 = 9^3 + 10^3$

Since then 1729 has been called the Hardy–Ramanujan number, and any number that is a sum of two cubes in two different ways is a taxicab number. How did Ramanujan know? He had loved numbers all his life; his colleague John Littlewood said that every positive integer was one of his personal friends.

Cubes and consecutive odd numbers

Consecutive odd numbers have a part to play with cubes too. Look at this pattern:

$1 = 1 = 1^3$

$3 + 5 = 8 = 2^3$

$7 + 9 + 11 = 27 = 3^3$

$13 + 15 + 17 + 19 = 64 = 4^3$

$21 + 23 + 25 + 27 + 29 = 125 = 5^3$

$31 + 33 + 35 + 37 + 39 + 41 = 216 = 6^3$
