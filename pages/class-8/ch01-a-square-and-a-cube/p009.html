Is it a perfect square?

Given a number such as 576 or 327, how do we tell whether it is a perfect square — and if it is, find its square root? The units digit settles 327 at once: it ends in 7, and no square does. But 576 ends in 6, which decides nothing. There are three ways to go on.

List the squares. We know $20^2 = 400$, so we can square 21, 22, 23, … until we reach 576 or pass it:

$20^2 = 400, \quad 21^2 = 441, \quad 22^2 = 484, \quad 23^2 = 529, \quad 24^2 = 576$

So $\sqrt{576} = 24$. This works, but for a large number it means a great many multiplications.

Subtract odd numbers. Every perfect square is a sum of consecutive odd numbers from 1, so the test of Example 3 finds the root as it goes.

Example 5

Find $\sqrt{81}$ by subtracting odd numbers.

$81 - 1 = 80, \quad 80 - 3 = 77, \quad 77 - 5 = 72, \quad 72 - 7 = 65, \quad 65 - 9 = 56$

$56 - 11 = 45, \quad 45 - 13 = 32, \quad 32 - 15 = 17, \quad 17 - 17 = 0$

We reached 0 at the ninth step, so $\sqrt{81} = 9$. Could we find $\sqrt{729}$ the same way? Yes — but it would take 27 subtractions.

Factorise into primes. A perfect square is a number multiplied by itself, so its prime factors ought to split into two identical groups. This is the method that scales.

Example 6

Is 324 a perfect square? Is 156?

$324 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3$. Split the factors into two equal groups:

$324 = (2 \times 3 \times 3) \times (2 \times 3 \times 3) = (2 \times 3 \times 3)^2 = 18^2$

The same thing seen as pairs: $324 = (2 \times 2) \times (3 \times 3) \times (3 \times 3)$, every prime paired off. So 324 is a perfect square and $\sqrt{324} = 2 \times 3 \times 3 = 18$.

For 156, the factorisation is $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 13$. The two 2s pair, but the 3 and the 13 have no partners. The factors cannot be split into two identical groups, so 156 is not a perfect square.
