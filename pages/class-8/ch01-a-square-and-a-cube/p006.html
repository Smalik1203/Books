Squares and odd numbers

The sum of the first $n$ odd numbers is $n^2$. Every perfect square is a sum of consecutive odd numbers starting from 1 — and the $n$th odd number is $2n - 1$.

A test for perfect squares

This gives a way to test a number without knowing any square roots. Subtract 1, then 3, then 5, and so on, and watch whether you land exactly on 0.

Example 3

Is 25 a perfect square? Is 38?

$25 - 1 = 24, \quad 24 - 3 = 21, \quad 21 - 5 = 16, \quad 16 - 7 = 9, \quad 9 - 9 = 0$

Five subtractions bring 25 exactly to 0, so $25 = 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + 9$, the sum of the first five odd numbers, and $25 = 5^2$. Now 38:

$38 - 1 = 37, \quad 37 - 3 = 34, \quad 34 - 5 = 29, \quad 29 - 7 = 22, \quad 22 - 9 = 13, \quad 13 - 11 = 2, \quad 2 - 13 = -11$

The subtractions jump over 0 without landing on it. So 38 is not a sum of consecutive odd numbers from 1, and it is not a perfect square.

The rule, then: a natural number is a perfect square exactly when subtracting $1, 3, 5, \ldots$ in turn lands on 0, and the number of subtractions is its square root. The same fact answers a different kind of question.
