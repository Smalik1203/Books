Perfect squares by prime factors

A number is a perfect square exactly when its prime factors can be split into two identical groups — every prime occurs an even number of times. The square root is the product of one group.

Use prime factorisation to decide whether 1156 and 2800 are perfect squares. For the one that is, write down its square root.

Prime factors also tell you how to make a square out of a number that is not one: supply the partner that each unpaired prime is missing.

Example 7

Find the smallest number by which 252 must be multiplied to give a perfect square, and the square root of the result.

$252 = 2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 7$. The 2s pair and the 3s pair; the 7 is alone. One more 7 completes it:

$252 \times 7 = (2 \times 2) \times (3 \times 3) \times (7 \times 7) = (2 \times 3 \times 7)^2 = 42^2$

So the smallest multiplier is 7, and $\sqrt{1764} = 42$. Multiplying by any other number would either leave the 7 unpaired or add more than was needed.

Estimating a square root

For a larger perfect square there is a quicker route: trap the root between two squares you know, then use the last digit. To find $\sqrt{1936}$:

1936 lies between $1600 = 40^2$ and $2500 = 50^2$, so $40 < \sqrt{1936} < 50$.

1936 ends in 6, so its root ends in 4 or 6. It can only be 44 or 46.

$45^2 = (40 + 5)^2 = 40^2 + 2 \times 40 \times 5 + 5^2 = 1600 + 400 + 25 = 2025$, which is more than 1936.

So $40 < \sqrt{1936} < 45$, and the root must be 44. Check: $44^2 = 1936$.

The same bracketing gives a useful answer even when the number is not a perfect square at all.
