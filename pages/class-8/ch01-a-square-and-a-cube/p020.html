Āryabhaṭa, 499 CE

A square figure of four equal sides and the number representing its area are called varga. The product of two equal quantities is also called varga.

Why is the word root, the root of a plant, used for the operations $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ and $\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}$? Because in ancient India the Sanskrit mūla — root of a plant, and so basis, cause, origin — was the word for taking roots. Varga-mūla, the origin of the square, was the square root, and ghana-mūla the cube root. Arabic and then Latin each reached for their own word for a plant's root — jidhr and radix — and English root and radical follow from those. Another word was pada, foot or basis: Brahmagupta, in 628 CE, explains that the pada of a kṛti is that of which it is the square.

One chapter, two families of numbers, and one habit running through both: a square or a cube is a number multiplied by itself, and every fact here — the odd factor count, the last digits, the sums of odd numbers, the pairing and tripling of primes — comes from that.

What this chapter established
  1. A number obtained by multiplying a number by itself is a square number; the squares of the natural numbers, $1, 4, 9, 16, \ldots$, are the perfect squares. They are exactly the numbers with an odd number of factors.
  2. A perfect square ends in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9, and in an even number of zeros. The last digit can show that a number is not a square; it can never show that it is.
  3. The sum of the first $n$ odd numbers is $n^2$. Subtracting $1, 3, 5, \ldots$ from a number tests whether it is a perfect square and counts its root; two consecutive triangular numbers add to a square.
  4. The square root undoes squaring. Every perfect square has two integer square roots, one positive and one negative; $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ names the positive one, so $\sqrt{9} = 3$.
  5. A number is a perfect square when its prime factors split into two identical groups, and the root is the product of one group. A root can also be trapped between two known squares and settled by its last digit.
  6. A number multiplied by itself three times is a cube; $1, 8, 27, 64, \ldots$ are the perfect cubes. A cube can end in any digit, its last digit is fixed by the number's last digit, and the cube of a negative number is negative.
  7. A number is a perfect cube when its prime factors split into three identical groups. The cube root is written $\sqrt[3]{\phantom{x}}$: $\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$, and every number has exactly one.
