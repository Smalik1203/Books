Patterns in the units digit

Look first at the last digit of each square. Every number in the table ends in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9. Not one ends in 2, 3, 7 or 8 — and that will stay true however far the table is extended, because the last digit of a square depends only on the last digit of the number being squared.

number ends in01 or 92 or 83 or 74 or 65
its square ends in014965

Is a number that ends in one of those six digits always a square? No. 16 and 36 are squares, but 26, which also ends in 6, is not. So the units digit can never prove that a number is a square. What it can do is prove that a number is not one: anything ending in 2, 3, 7 or 8 is out at a glance.

Example 2

Which of 452, 733 and 1156 are certainly not perfect squares?

452 ends in 2, and no square does not a square
733 ends in 3, and no square does not a square
1156 ends in 6, so the digit decides nothing undecided

The third needs more work. It happens that $34^2 = 1156$, but the units digit alone could not have told us so.

Read the table the other way and it says something about the number itself. The squares $1^2, 9^2, 11^2, 19^2, 21^2$ and $29^2$ all end in 1: a number ending in 1 or 9 has a square ending in 1. And the squares ending in 6 — $4^2 = 16$, $6^2 = 36$, $14^2 = 196$, $16^2 = 256$, $24^2 = 576$, $26^2 = 676$ — all come from numbers ending in 4 or 6.
