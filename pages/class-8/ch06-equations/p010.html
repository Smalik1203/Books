6.5.2 Fractions

Fractions in an equation are cleared in one stroke: multiply every term on both sides by a number that all the denominators divide into — their lowest common multiple is the tidiest choice. Every fraction then cancels and the equation has whole numbers in it.

The words every term are the ones that get skipped. Multiplying both sides means multiplying all of both sides, including any term that had no fraction in it to begin with.

Example 8

Solve $\dfrac{x}{2} + \dfrac{x}{3} = 10$.

The denominators are $2$ and $3$, so multiply everything by $6$.

$\times 6$ $3x + 2x = 60$
gather $5x = 60$
$\div 5$ $x = 12$
check $6 + 4 = 10$  true

The $10$ became $60$. A reader who multiplies only the two fractions gets $5x = 10$ and an answer of $2$, which fails the check at once.

Where a whole numerator is a bracketed expression, keep the bracket while you multiply and expand afterwards. The fraction bar acts as a bracket whether one is written or not, and half of the errors in this section come from forgetting that.

Example 9

Solve $\dfrac{2x - 1}{3} = \dfrac{x + 4}{5}$.

$\times 15$ $5(2x - 1) = 3(x + 4)$
expand $10x - 5 = 3x + 12$
gather $7x = 17$,   $x = \dfrac{17}{7}$

Both numerators kept their brackets across the multiplication. Without them the second line would read $10x - 1 = 3x + 4$, which is a different equation with a different answer.
