Checking a solution costs one line and settles the matter completely. Put your answer back into the original equation — not into a line of your own working, which may already carry the mistake — and see whether the two sides agree. No other subject gives you a free check on every answer.

6.2.1 Which equations this chapter is about

An equation is linear in one variable when it has one letter in it, that letter appears only to the first power, and it is nowhere in a denominator. All three conditions do work, and each rules out equations that behave quite differently.

$4x - 7 = 9$ linear in one variable
$x^2 = 16$ not linear — and it has two solutions
$2x + y = 5$ two variables — and endlessly many solutions
$\dfrac{6}{x} = 3$ the letter is in a denominator

The first kind is the tidy one, and its tidiness is worth stating: a linear equation in one variable has exactly one solution, no more and no less. The last section of this chapter is about the two ways that promise can be broken, and both of them turn out to be interesting.
