6.7.1 When the letters all cancel and the numbers disagree

Example 13

Solve $2x + 3 = 2x + 5$.

$-2x$ $3 = 5$

Which is false, and nothing has been done wrong to get there. The letter has gone, so there is no value left to choose; and what remains is a statement that is false whatever $x$ was. The equation has no solution.

The balance makes it plain. Both pans hold two bags, and the left pan has three grams beside them while the right has five. Take the two bags off each pan and you are left with three grams against five grams, which no bag can rescue: the beam was never level and no weight of bag would have made it level.

6.7.2 When the letters all cancel and the numbers agree

Example 14

Solve $2(x + 3) = 2x + 6$.

expand $2x + 6 = 2x + 6$
$-2x$ $6 = 6$

Which is true — and true whatever $x$ was. So every number is a solution. Try $x = 1$, $x = -40$, $x = \tfrac12$; all of them work, because the two sides were never two different expressions at all. They were the same expression written two ways.

That is an identity, which is exactly what Chapter 4 was about, and the distinction its last section drew now has a use: an identity dressed up with an equals sign is not a question with an answer, and treating it as one is what leads a reader to write down $x = 0$ or to think the working has failed.

Both cases are recognised by the same thing happening: the letter disappears. What you do next depends only on whether what is left is true.
