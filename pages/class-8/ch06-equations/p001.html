6

The Same to Both Sides

6.1Introduction

A balance is level when the two pans hold the same weight. Put a sealed bag on one pan and a twelve-gram weight on the other, and if the beam sits level you know something about the bag without ever opening it: whatever is inside weighs twelve grams.

That is an equation, and reading it is the whole of this chapter. The bag is the unknown, the beam sitting level is the equals sign, and finding out what is in the bag is what solving means.

The useful part is what you may do next. Take four grams off the left pan and the beam tips — unless you take four grams off the right pan as well, in which case it stays exactly as level as it was. Double what is on the left and the beam tips, unless you double the right too. Anything at all may be done to an equation provided it is done to both sides, and that single rule is what the chapter's title is.

Chapter 4 ended by separating two things that look alike on the page. An identity like $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ is true for every value of its letters and there is nothing to find. An equation like $2x = 6$ is true for one value and false for all the others, and the value it is true for is what you are being asked for. This chapter is about the second kind.

6.2What an Equation Says

An equation is two expressions with an equals sign between them. What stands to the left of the sign is the left side, what stands to the right is the right side, and the equation is the claim that the two come to the same number.

$$\underbrace{3x + 5}_{\text{left side}} \;=\; \underbrace{20}_{\text{right side}}$$

That claim is not true for every $x$. Put $x = 2$ and the left side is $11$, which is not $20$, so the equation is false there. Put $x = 5$ and the left side is $20$, which is. A value that makes the two sides agree is a solution of the equation, and it is also called a root.
