3 At $x = -2$, (c) gives $5$ on both sides. (a) gives $-2$, not $2$; (b) gives $7$, not $3$; (d) gives $-8$, not $8$.
6 $12$ is the lowest common multiple of $6$ and $4$. (a) also clears both fractions, but it is not the smallest; (b) adds the denominators and (d) divides into neither.
8 Taking $6x$ from both sides leaves $1 = -1$, which is false whatever $x$ is. (d) treats the false statement as an equation still to be solved.
9 $x + (x + 14) = 50$ gives $x = 18$, so the numbers are 18 and 32. (a) is the smaller number; (d) is half of 50, which would make the two numbers equal.
12 $x + 35 = 3(x + 5)$ gives $x = 10$. (a) comes from writing $3x + 5$ for $3(x + 5)$: the son's 5 years are added before his age is tripled.
14 $2(w + w + 3) = 30$ gives $w = 6$, so the length is 9 cm. (a) is the width, which is not what was asked; (d) is half the perimeter.
15 $5(0) = 2(0)$, so A is true. R is false: dividing by $x$ is not allowed when $x$ may be zero, and here it loses the solution. So the answer is (c).
17 Taking $2x$ from both sides leaves $3 = 7$, so A is true. R is true too, but it is not the reason: A holds because the letter cancels and a false statement is left. So the answer is (b).

One rule solved every equation in this chapter: do the same to both sides. Every step in every worked answer was an instance of it. The only small print is that the thing you divide by must not be zero. That is the whole of what went wrong for Ravi, and worth more attention than its one line suggests.

The harder half was never the solving. It was reading a sentence closely enough to write the equation. That means naming what the letter stands for and in what units, noticing that thirty coins makes the second kind $30 - x$ and not a second letter, and remembering that everybody in a question about ages gets older at the same rate. An equation solved perfectly from a sentence read carelessly gives a wrong answer with no working to blame.

And keep the two odd cases. When the letter cancels, nothing has failed: you have found either that the two sides can never agree, or that they always did. Both are answers, and outside a textbook they are often the answer somebody actually wanted.
