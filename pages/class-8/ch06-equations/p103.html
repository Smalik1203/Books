Ravi solves $7x = 4x$ by dividing both sides by $x$. He gets $7 = 4$, which is false, and says the equation has no solution. Show that he is wrong, and name the rule he broke.

Try $x = 0$ in $7x = 4x$: the left side is $0$ and so is the right. It is a solution, so the equation certainly has one, and Ravi's conclusion is false.

The step that failed is the division. Both sides may be divided by the same number provided it is not zero — and dividing by $x$ is dividing by a number nobody has checked. If $x$ happens to be zero, the step is not allowed; and it is precisely that value which then goes missing from the answer.

instead $7x - 4x = 0$
so $3x = 0$,   $x = 0$

Subtracting $4x$ carries no condition and keeps the solution. This is the one place where the small print on a rule decides the answer: never divide by something that contains the unknown.

The rule is not arbitrary. Dividing both sides by a quantity is legitimate only when that quantity cannot be zero, and a quantity containing the unknown is exactly one whose value you do not yet know. Divide by it and you have quietly assumed the answer is not the value that would make it zero — which is very often the value the question was about.

Subtracting carries no such small print, which is why it is the move to reach for first whenever both sides carry the unknown.

A claim: every linear equation whose coefficients are whole numbers has a whole-number solution. Disprove it.

A claim about every equation is disproved by one that fails, and the smallest one will do.

take $2x = 5$
solution $x = \dfrac{5}{2}$, which is not a whole number

The coefficients are whole and the solution is not, so the claim is false. That is the whole answer: disproving a general statement takes one case, while proving one takes an argument covering all of them.

Think of a number. Double it, add ten, halve the result, then take away the number you first thought of. Try it with $7$ and with $100$. Then show that the answer is always the same, whatever you started with.

With $7$: double to $14$, add ten to $24$, halve to $12$, take away $7$, and the answer is $5$. With $100$: $200$, $210$, $105$, take away $100$, and the answer is $5$ again. Two cases are a suspicion, not a reason.
