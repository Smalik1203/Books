An equation in letters is worth doing once even where a numerical one would have served. It answers not one question but the whole family the question came from, and it puts the awkward cases on the page instead of leaving them to be found by accident later.

That is the difference between solving and knowing how to solve. Six numerical examples show that a method worked six times; one lettered one shows what the method needs in order to work at all.

6.5Brackets and Fractions

Two things can stand between an equation and the methods of the last two sections, and both are cleared before solving rather than during it. Brackets are removed first; fractions are removed first. After that the equation is an ordinary one and nothing new is needed.

6.5.1 Brackets

Expand them, using the distributive law of Chapter 4 — and remembering that a minus in front of a bracket reverses the sign of everything inside, which is where the marks are lost.

Example 6

Solve $3(x + 4) = 2(x + 9)$.

Solution.

Step 1 $3x + 12 = 2x + 18$ expand both
Step 2 $x + 12 = 18$ $-2x$ both sides
Step 3 $x = 6$ $-12$ both sides
Check $3(10) = 2(15) = 30$ true
Answer $x = 6$
