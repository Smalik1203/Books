5.1Introduction

In earlier grades you worked with triangles and quadrilaterals — figures built from straight edges, where almost any claim could be settled by measuring the sides and adding up the angles.

This chapter takes the next step, into the circle. It has no straight edges, no corners, and no pieces to count. Yet nearly everything you are about to prove about it follows from one sentence saying what a circle is. Before that sentence, something to explain.

People have been drawing circles for as long as they have been drawing anything. Among the rock paintings at Gudāhāndi in Odisha, beside the triangles and the squares, there are circles and ovals. The shape was there to be copied: a raindrop spreads across still water as a circle, the cut face of a stem is a circle, and so, to the eye, are the full moon and the sun.

Example 1

Jamuna cuts a disc out of paper. Nothing on it marks the centre.

She folds the disc so that the boundary falls exactly onto itself, and presses the crease flat. She opens it out, folds it again a different way, and presses again.

The two creases cross at a single point — and that point is the centre.

Cut a fresh disc, carelessly, and it happens again. Fold a third time and the third crease runs through the same point. Folding can go on confirming this for as long as you have paper, but it can never tell you why the creases meet at all, nor why they meet at the centre rather than somewhere near it. For that you need to say what a circle is — and by the end of Section 5.3 you will settle it in three lines.

A definition is where a proof starts: say exactly what a shape is, and its properties stop being coincidences.

5.2What a Circle Is

When we speak of a circle, a triangle or a square, we take the figure to be drawn on a flat sheet — a plane. Everything in this chapter happens on one.

Fix a point of that plane, and fix a distance. Now collect every point of the plane that lies exactly that far from the fixed point. That collection is a circle, and it is the whole of the definition.

Circle

A circle is the set of all points of a plane that are at a fixed distance from a fixed point of that plane. The fixed point is the centre; the fixed distance is the radius.

Notice what the definition does not say. It says nothing about roundness, nothing about smoothness, and nothing about how to draw one. It fixes a point and a distance, and everything else in this chapter — every symmetry, every theorem — has to be squeezed out of those two things.

A set of points singled out by a condition like this is called the locus of points satisfying the condition. In that language, a circle is the locus of points at a given distance from a given point. The word will earn its keep in Section 5.4, where the condition changes.

The parts of a circle have names. Take two points AAA and BBB on it; the segment joining them is a chord. Join the two ends of a chord to the centre and an angle appears there: the chord ABABAB is said to subtend the angle ∠AOB\angle AOB∠AOB at the centre. A chord that happens to pass through the centre is a diameter. Fig. 5.1 shows all three.

Only PPP lies on the circle. Notice that being inside or outside is not a near miss that might be argued about: the definition allows no degrees. A point either stands at distance rrr from the centre or it is no part of the circle at all.

A diameter is a chord of a particular kind, and its length follows from the definition alone: both ends lie at distance rrr from the centre, and the centre lies between them. Every diameter of a given circle is 2r2r2r long.

No chord is longer. A chord that misses the centre still has both ends at distance rrr from it, so going from one end to the other by way of the centre measures 2r2r2r — and going straight across is shorter. Nothing here was measured: the argument used the definition, and that a straight path is the shortest one.

5.3Symmetries of a Circle

A circle looks the same from every direction, and that is not a vague compliment — it is a statement with consequences, and it follows from the definition in a line. There are two kinds of sameness to separate.

Watch a wheel go past. At one moment some point of the rim is touching the road; a moment later some point of the rim is touching the road again. Is it the same point? Nothing you can see decides it. Chalk a mark on the rim and you can follow it, as in Fig. 5.2 — but rub the mark out and the two pictures become one picture.

Turn a circle about its centre, through any angle you like, and you get the circle back — not another circle of the same size, but the very same set of points. The reason is the definition and nothing else. Turning about OOO leaves every point's distance from OOO unchanged, so a point at distance rrr goes to a point at distance rrr; the set of all points at distance rrr is carried onto itself.

No other shape in this book does that. Turn a square about its centre and it comes back to itself four times in a full turn, at multiples of 90∘90^\circ90∘, and at no other angle. A circle comes back at every angle. We say it has complete rotational symmetry.

Now the other kind of sameness. Draw a circle, cut it out, and fold it so that the boundary falls exactly onto itself. Open it out: the crease is a line of reflection symmetry. It is Jamuna's fold from the first page of this chapter, and we are at last in a position to say what it does.

Back to the folded paper

Call the crease ℓ\ellℓ. Folding along ℓ\ellℓ carries the circle onto itself, and folding does not change distances. So the point where the centre lands — call it O′O'O′ — is again a point from which every point of the circle is at distance rrr. Could O′O'O′ be anywhere other than OOO? If it were, every point of the circle would be equidistant from two different points, and so would lie on the perpendicular bisector of OO′OO'OO′ — the whole circle would sit on one straight line. It does not. So O′=OO' = OO′=O. And the only points a fold leaves where they were are the points of the crease itself.

So OOO lies on ℓ\ellℓ. Every crease of this kind is a line through the centre — a diameter. Two different creases are two different lines through OOO, and two different lines meet at no more than one point. They meet at OOO. That is why Jamuna's construction cannot fail, and why her third fold was never going to tell her anything.

Lines of symmetry of a circle

A line is a line of reflection symmetry of a circle exactly when it passes through the centre. The lines of symmetry of a circle are its diameters — and there are infinitely many of them.

5.4How Many Circles?

In Section 5.2 the word locus was introduced and then left alone: a circle is the locus of points at a given distance from a given point. Change the condition and you get a different locus. Here is the change worth making.

Instead of one fixed point, take two. Let AAA and BBB be points of the plane, and ask a question not about points but about circles: how many circles pass through both of them?

At least one does. If a circle passes through AAA and BBB, its centre OOO must satisfy OA=OBOA = OBOA=OB — and one point obviously does, the midpoint of ABABAB. Take that as the centre, with radius half of ABABAB, and the circle drawn passes through both. For that circle, ABABAB is a diameter.

Are there others? Notice that the question has quietly turned back into a question about points: which points of the plane are equidistant from AAA and BBB? You met the answer in an earlier grade. They are the points of the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB — and there are infinitely many of them.

That answer needs stating with some care, because it is really two statements and a locus needs both of them:

Every point of the perpendicular bisector is equidistant from AAA and BBB; and every point equidistant from AAA and BBB lies on the perpendicular bisector. The first says that no point of the line is missed out. The second says that no point outside it sneaks in. Drop either half and "the locus of" becomes the wrong phrase — you would have described some of the centres, or too many.

With both halves in hand the count is settled. The centres of the circles through AAA and BBB are exactly the points of the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB: one circle for each point of a line, and so infinitely many.

Circles through two points

Infinitely many circles pass through any two given points. Their centres are precisely the points of the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them, and the smallest of those circles has that segment as a diameter.

Two points, then, are not enough to pin a circle down. Three might be — and the answer turns out to depend on how the three are arranged.

The first thing to notice about three points is that they need not have any circle through them at all.

Suppose AAA, BBB and CCC lie on one straight line — that they are collinear. A circle through all three would need a centre equidistant from AAA and BBB, so lying on the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB; and equidistant from BBB and CCC, so lying on the perpendicular bisector of BCBCBC. But both of those bisectors are perpendicular to the same line, which makes them parallel, and they are not the same line, because ABABAB and BCBCBC have different midpoints. Parallel and distinct: they never meet. There is no such centre, and so no such circle.

Three points in a row, then, are hopeless. Rule that case out and everything changes.

Theorem 1

Through any three points that are not collinear there passes exactly one circle.

Why is this true? The argument is the one just used, run forwards instead of backwards. Suppose some circle passes through AAA, BBB and CCC, and call its centre OOO.

Since OA=OBOA = OBOA=OB, the point OOO lies on the perpendicular bisector of ABABAB.

Since OA=OCOA = OCOA=OC, the point OOO lies on the perpendicular bisector of ACACAC.

Now AAA, BBB and CCC are not collinear, so ABABAB and ACACAC point in different directions, and the two bisectors are therefore not parallel. Two non-parallel lines of a plane meet at exactly one point. So there is only one candidate for OOO — which settles the exactly one before we have even shown that a circle exists.

And it does exist. Take that meeting point as centre and draw the circle of radius OAOAOA. Because OOO is on the bisector of ABABAB we have OB=OAOB = OAOB=OA, and because it is on the bisector of ACACAC we have OC=OAOC = OAOC=OA. So BBB and CCC lie on that circle as well, and all three points are on it.

Now nudge CCC off the line, a little at a time. The two bisectors begin to meet. Where does their meeting point go as CCC approaches the line again — and what is happening to the circle?

Three non-collinear points also determine a triangle, and what we have just built is the circle through its vertices. Both the circle and its centre have names.

The circle through the vertices of a triangle is its circumcircle, and its centre is the circumcentre — the point where the perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet. The circle is said to circumscribe the triangle, and the triangle to be inscribed in the circle.

Note what the argument gave us along the way. All three perpendicular bisectors pass through OOO, though only two were used to find it; the third has no choice in the matter, since OOO is equidistant from BBB and CCC too.

Theorem 1 says the circumcircle exists and that there is only one. It says nothing about where the centre lands — and the answer is not the one you might guess, because the circumcentre need not lie inside the triangle at all.

5.5Chords and the Angles They Subtend

Tie a thread between two points of a wheel and pull it tight. What you have is a chord. Join its two ends to the centre, and the thread subtends an angle there — the angle named in Section 5.2.

Now turn the wheel. The thread goes round with it and comes to rest across a different pair of points; but it is the same thread, so its length has not changed. Has the angle at the centre changed with it?

Section 5.3 all but answers this. Turning the circle about its centre carries the circle onto itself and carries the thread along without stretching it, so nothing in the picture is different except which part of the rim the ends are tied to. The angle cannot have moved.

That is a good reason to believe what follows. It is not yet a proof of it, because the argument assumed the second chord was the first one turned — and a chord of the same length lying somewhere else on the circle has not been shown to be that. Congruent triangles settle the matter without the assumption.

Theorem 2

Equal chords of a circle subtend equal angles at the centre.

Let the chords be ABABAB and DEDEDE, with AB=DEAB = DEAB=DE, in a circle of centre CCC and radius rrr. We want ∠ACB=∠DCE\angle ACB = \angle DCE∠ACB=∠DCE. Join all four ends to the centre, as in Fig. 5.6, and compare the two triangles that appear.

CA=CB=rCA = CB = rCA=CB=r and CD=CE=rCD = CE = rCD=CE=r, so CA=CDCA = CDCA=CD and CB=CECB = CECB=CE.

AB=DEAB = DEAB=DE is given.

So △CAB≅△CDE\triangle CAB \cong \triangle CDE△CAB≅△CDE, by SSS.

Corresponding angles of congruent triangles are equal, and ∠ACB\angle ACB∠ACB and ∠DCE\angle DCE∠DCE are corresponding. So the two chords subtend the same angle at the centre.

Now run the argument backwards. Suppose instead that the two angles at the centre are equal — must the chords be?

Theorem 3

Chords of a circle that subtend equal angles at the centre are equal in length.

CA=CD=rCA = CD = rCA=CD=r and CB=CE=rCB = CE = rCB=CE=r.

∠ACB=∠DCE\angle ACB = \angle DCE∠ACB=∠DCE is now what is given, and it is the angle between those two pairs of sides.

So △CAB≅△CDE\triangle CAB \cong \triangle CDE△CAB≅△CDE, by SAS, and therefore AB=DEAB = DEAB=DE.

Notice how little separates the two proofs: the same figure, the same two triangles, the same four radii. All that changes is which fact is given and which is wanted — and with it, the congruence rule that does the work.

5.6Chords, Midpoints and Perpendiculars

Section 5.5 took the triangle made by a chord and the two radii to its ends, and got two theorems out of it. That triangle has more to give. It is isosceles — which is what Exercise Set 5.3 asked you to show — and an isosceles triangle is symmetric about the line through its apex and the midpoint of its base.

Here the apex is the centre. So draw a circle with centre CCC, take any chord ABABAB, and let MMM be the midpoint of ABABAB. What can be said about the segment CMCMCM?

Theorem 4

The segment joining the centre of a circle to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to that chord.

Why is this true? Compare the two triangles that CMCMCM cuts the big one into.

CA=CBCA = CBCA=CB, both being radii.

AM=BMAM = BMAM=BM, because MMM is the midpoint.

CMCMCM is common to both.

So △CMA≅△CMB\triangle CMA \cong \triangle CMB△CMA≅△CMB, by SSS.

Congruent triangles give ∠CMA=∠CMB\angle CMA = \angle CMB∠CMA=∠CMB. But those two angles sit side by side on the straight line ABABAB, so they add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. Two equal angles adding to 180∘180^\circ180∘ are 90∘90^\circ90∘ each. So CMCMCM is perpendicular to ABABAB.

Now the converse. Drop a perpendicular from the centre onto a chord instead, and ask where it lands.

Theorem 5

The perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.

CA=CBCA = CBCA=CB, both radii — and in the right-angled triangles CMACMACMA and CMBCMBCMB these are the hypotenuses.

CMCMCM is common, and ∠CMA=∠CMB=90∘\angle CMA = \angle CMB = 90^\circ∠CMA=∠CMB=90∘ is now what is given.

So △CMA≅△CMB\triangle CMA \cong \triangle CMB△CMA≅△CMB, by RHS, and therefore AM=BMAM = BMAM=BM.

Three things have been in play: that the segment runs from the centre, that it meets the chord at a right angle, and that it hits the midpoint. Theorem 4 takes the first and third and delivers the second; Theorem 5 takes the first and second and delivers the third. Any two of the three force the remaining one — and the last pairing is the first question below.

5.7How Far a Chord Lies from the Centre

The distance from a point to a line means the perpendicular distance — the shortest way across. So the distance from the centre to a chord is measured along the perpendicular from the centre, and Theorem 5 has already told us where that perpendicular lands: on the midpoint of the chord.

Take a paper circle and fold a piece of the boundary inwards; the crease is a chord. Fold again so that the two ends of that crease meet, and open both folds. The second crease crosses the first at its midpoint, square on. Theorems 4 and 5, in paper.

Now make a second chord of the same length somewhere else on the circle, and measure how far it lies from the centre. The two distances come out equal. Try another pair and they come out equal again.

Is that convincing? It is the same trap as Jamuna's folded disc on the first page of this chapter. A statement can hold in every case you have patience to try and still be false; and even when it is true, a stack of measurements never says why. So here is why.

Theorem 6

Chords of a circle that have the same length lie at the same distance from the centre.

Let ABABAB and FGFGFG be chords of a circle with centre CCC and radius rrr, with AB=FGAB = FGAB=FG. Let EEE and HHH be their midpoints, so that by Theorem 4 the segments CECECE and CHCHCH are perpendicular to the chords — and are therefore the two distances we are comparing.

AE=12AB=12FG=FHAE = \tfrac{1}{2}AB = \tfrac{1}{2}FG = FHAE=21​AB=21​FG=FH, since EEE and HHH are the midpoints.

CA=CF=rCA = CF = rCA=CF=r, and these are the hypotenuses.

∠CEA=∠CHF=90∘\angle CEA = \angle CHF = 90^\circ∠CEA=∠CHF=90∘.

So △CEA≅△CHF\triangle CEA \cong \triangle CHF△CEA≅△CHF, by RHS, and therefore CE=CHCE = CHCE=CH.

There is a second route. Triangles CABCABCAB and CFGCFGCFG are congruent by SSS — two pairs of radii and the given equal chords — and CECECE and CHCHCH are corresponding altitudes of congruent triangles. Either argument will do; the first has the advantage of never leaving the right-angled halves.

Turn it round and the converse follows from the same two triangles, with the given fact and the wanted one exchanged once more.

Theorem 7

Chords of a circle that lie at the same distance from the centre are equal in length.

CE=CHCE = CHCE=CH is now given, and CA=CF=rCA = CF = rCA=CF=r.

∠CEA=∠CHF=90∘\angle CEA = \angle CHF = 90^\circ∠CEA=∠CHF=90∘, so RHS gives △CEA≅△CHF\triangle CEA \cong \triangle CHF△CEA≅△CHF again.

Hence AE=FHAE = FHAE=FH, and doubling gives AB=FGAB = FGAB=FG.

Both theorems come out of one relation, which is worth having on its own. Look again at the right-angled triangle CEACEACEA in Fig. 5.8. Its hypotenuse is the radius rrr, one leg is the distance d=CEd = CEd=CE, and the other leg is half the chord. The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem gives

r2=d2+(12 AB)2,soAB=2 r2−d2 r^{2} = d^{2} + \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,AB\right)^{2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad AB = 2\sqrt{\,r^{2} - d^{2}\,}r2=d2+(21​AB)2,soAB=2r2−d2​

Read that formula and both theorems fall out of it at once. In a given circle rrr is fixed, so the length of a chord depends on ddd and on nothing else whatever. Equal distances therefore force equal lengths, and equal lengths force equal distances. Theorems 6 and 7 are the two directions of a single statement.

Take each chord's distance from the centre first. The combining comes second, and it is where the care is needed, because the question as asked has two answers.

the 16 cm chord d1=102−82=36d_1 = \sqrt{10^{2}-8^{2}} = \sqrt{36}d1​=102−82​=36​ 666 cm
the 12 cm chord d2=102−62=64d_2 = \sqrt{10^{2}-6^{2}} = \sqrt{64}d2​=102−62​=64​ 888 cm

Nothing in the question says which side of the centre each chord is on, and the two arrangements do not agree:

opposite sides 6+86 + 86+8 141414 cm
the same side 8−68 - 68−6 222 cm

Both are right. A question like this is not finished until you say which case you are in — or give both.

It settles the comparison of unequal chords too, which no amount of congruence was going to give us.

Theorem 8

Of two chords of a circle that are not equal, the longer lies nearer the centre.

Suppose AB>DEAB > DEAB>DE, at distances d1d_1d1​ and d2d_2d2​ from the centre. Then 2r2−d12>2r2−d222\sqrt{r^2 - d_1^2} > 2\sqrt{r^2 - d_2^2}2r2−d12​​>2r2−d22​​, so r2−d12>r2−d22r^2 - d_1^2 > r^2 - d_2^2r2−d12​>r2−d22​, so d12<d22d_1^2 < d_2^2d12​<d22​ — and since distances are never negative, d1<d2d_1 < d_2d1​<d2​. The longer chord is the nearer one.

The two extreme cases are worth following to the end. Slide a chord towards the centre until d=0d = 0d=0: the formula gives length 2r2r2r, and the chord has become a diameter. That is Section 5.2's result — no chord is longer than a diameter — arriving by an entirely different road. Slide it the other way until d=rd = rd=r and the length falls to zero: the chord has shrunk to a single point of the circle. Which is the answer to the question left hanging in Section 5.2. There is no shortest chord, because the moment a chord is short enough to be a candidate it has stopped being a chord at all.

Example 9

A circle has radius 252525 cm. One of its chords is 484848 cm long and another is 141414 cm long. Which lies nearer the centre, and by how much?

Theorem 8 answers the first half before any arithmetic starts: the longer chord is the nearer, so the 484848 cm chord is the closer of the two. Only the how much needs calculating, and for that we halve each chord and use the right-angled triangle again.

the 48 cm chord 252−242=49\sqrt{25^{2}-24^{2}} = \sqrt{49}252−242​=49​ 777 cm
the 14 cm chord 252−72=576\sqrt{25^{2}-7^{2}} = \sqrt{576}252−72​=576​ 242424 cm

The longer chord sits 777 cm out and the shorter 242424 cm, a difference of 171717 cm — the ordering Theorem 8 promised before any arithmetic began.

5.8Angles Subtended by an Arc

Everything so far has been about chords. But a chord comes with arcs attached — two of them — and an arc subtends angles of its own.

An arc is a connected piece of a circle, fixed by its two end points. Two points AAA and BBB cut a circle into two arcs, and unless ABABAB is a diameter one is shorter than the other: the shorter is the minor arc, the longer the major arc. Naming a third point says which you mean — in Fig. 5.10, arc AXBAXBAXB is the minor one and arc AYBAYBAYB the major.

The angle an arc subtends at the centre is the angle swept out as you travel along it: start with the radius at AAA, move round to BBB the way the arc goes, and measure how far the radius has turned. For the minor arc that is the ordinary angle ∠AOB\angle AOB∠AOB. For the major arc you sweep the other way and get the reflex angle instead. The two together make a complete turn.

Now a question that is not about the centre at all. Take the arc AXBAXBAXB, and a point DDD lying on the circle but not on that arc. Join DDD to AAA and to BBB. How big is ∠ADB\angle ADB∠ADB?

You would expect the answer to depend on where DDD sits. It does not. Slide DDD anywhere along the rest of the circle and the angle under which it sees ABABAB does not move at all. Check it before reading on — draw a circle, fix a chord, and measure the angle at three or four points of the far arc.

Theorem 9

The angle an arc subtends at the centre of a circle is twice the angle it subtends at any point of the circle outside the arc.

Let the arc be AFBAFBAFB with centre CCC, and let DDD be a point of the circle outside it. Join DDD to CCC and carry the line on until it meets the circle again, at EEE. Two isosceles triangles now do all the work, because CACACA, CBCBCB and CDCDCD are all radii.

In △BCD\triangle BCD△BCD: CB=CDCB = CDCB=CD, so ∠CBD=∠CDB\angle CBD = \angle CDB∠CBD=∠CDB.

∠BCE\angle BCE∠BCE is the exterior angle of △BCD\triangle BCD△BCD at CCC, so ∠BCE=∠CBD+∠CDB=2 ∠BDC\angle BCE = \angle CBD + \angle CDB = 2\,\angle BDC∠BCE=∠CBD+∠CDB=2∠BDC.

In △ACD\triangle ACD△ACD: CA=CDCA = CDCA=CD, so ∠CAD=∠CDA\angle CAD = \angle CDA∠CAD=∠CDA, and in the same way ∠ACE=2 ∠ADC\angle ACE = 2\,\angle ADC∠ACE=2∠ADC.

Adding the two results, ∠BCA=∠BCE+∠ECA=2 ∠BDC+2 ∠ADC=2 ∠BDA\angle BCA = \angle BCE + \angle ECA = 2\,\angle BDC + 2\,\angle ADC = 2\,\angle BDA∠BCA=∠BCE+∠ECA=2∠BDC+2∠ADC=2∠BDA, which is the theorem.

One step there was taken without comment: that the line DCDCDC, carried on, lands on the arc AFBAFBAFB — which is what put EEE between AAA and BBB and let the two angles at CCC be added. For some positions of DDD it lands outside the arc instead, and the figure looks different.

Nothing else changes. The same two isosceles triangles appear, and the same exterior-angle step applies to each; only now the angles at CCC must be subtracted rather than added, and so must the angles at DDD. That gives ∠ACB=∠ACE−∠BCE=2 ∠ADC−2 ∠BDC=2 ∠ADB\angle ACB = \angle ACE - \angle BCE = 2\,\angle ADC - 2\,\angle BDC = 2\,\angle ADB∠ACB=∠ACE−∠BCE=2∠ADC−2∠BDC=2∠ADB, and the theorem stands in that case too. It is worth drawing.

Theorem 9 is stated about the centre, but the useful part is what it says about everywhere else. The angle at the centre plainly does not depend on DDD — the centre does not know where DDD is. So the angle at DDD, being exactly half of it, cannot depend on DDD either.

Angles in the same segment

All the points of a circle lying outside a given arc see that arc's chord under the same angle.

Example 10

An arc AXBAXBAXB subtends 110∘110^\circ110∘ at the centre of a circle. What angle does it subtend at a point of the major arc? And what angle does the other arc subtend at a point of the minor arc?

The first is Theorem 9 read straight off. The second asks about a different arc, so the angle to halve is the one that arc sweeps at the centre — the reflex one.

arc AXB 110∘110^\circ110∘ at the centre, halved 55∘55^\circ55∘
the other arc 360∘−110∘=250∘360^\circ - 110^\circ = 250^\circ360∘−110∘=250∘, halved 125∘125^\circ125∘

A point on the major arc sees the chord at 55∘55^\circ55∘; a point on the minor arc sees it at 125∘125^\circ125∘. Notice that the two add to 180∘180^\circ180∘. That is not a coincidence of these numbers — the two central angles always make a full turn, so their halves always make a straight angle. Section 5.9 turns the observation into a theorem.

One case of Theorem 9 is used so often that it has a name of its own. A corollary is a fact that drops out of a result already proved, with no fresh argument needed.

Corollary

The angle subtended by a diameter at any point of the circle is a right angle.

Let ABABAB be a diameter and DDD any other point of the circle. The arc from AAA to BBB that avoids DDD is a semicircle, and the angle it sweeps at the centre is a straight angle, 180∘180^\circ180∘. By Theorem 9 the angle at DDD is half of that: ∠ADB=90∘\angle ADB = 90^\circ∠ADB=90∘, wherever on the circle DDD happens to be.

Section 5.4 left a debt here. It claimed that the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle lands on the midpoint of the hypotenuse, and gave no reason. The corollary is half of the reason. The other half — that a point seeing ABABAB under a right angle has no choice but to lie on the circle with ABABAB as diameter — is exactly the sort of statement Section 5.9 is about, and you will be able to finish the argument there.

Example 11

ABABAB is a diameter of a circle and CCC is a point on the circle, with ∠ABC=35∘\angle ABC = 35^\circ∠ABC=35∘. Find ∠BAC\angle BAC∠BAC.

Nothing is said about the angle at CCC — and nothing needs to be, because ABABAB is a diameter, and the corollary fixes that angle wherever on the circle CCC happens to sit.

angle at C the angle in a semicircle 90∘90^\circ90∘
angle at B given 35∘35^\circ35∘

The three angles of △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC add to 180∘180^\circ180∘, so

∠BAC=180∘−90∘−35∘=55∘\angle BAC = 180^\circ - 90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ∠BAC=180∘−90∘−35∘=55∘

The corollary did all the work: without it, one angle and a diameter would not determine the triangle at all.

5.9Points on One Circle

Section 5.4 counted the circles through two points — infinitely many — and through three, where there is exactly one so long as they are not in a row. Four points are a different matter altogether. Any three of them already fix a circle, so the fourth has no freedom left: either it happens to land on that circle or it does not. Points that all lie on one circle are called concyclic.

So when does the fourth point land? Theorem 9 supplies the clue. If AAA, BBB, CCC and DDD are concyclic, with CCC and DDD on the same side of ABABAB, then CCC and DDD are on the same arc, and angles in the same segment are equal: ∠ACB=∠ADB\angle ACB = \angle ADB∠ACB=∠ADB. That much is forced. The question is whether it works in reverse.

Theorem 10

If a segment ABABAB subtends equal angles at two points CCC and DDD lying on the same side of it, then AAA, BBB, CCC and DDD are concyclic.

The proof takes a shape not used anywhere else in this chapter: rather than building the circle, we show that DDD has nowhere else to be.

The points AAA, BBB, CCC are not collinear — if they were, ∠ACB\angle ACB∠ACB would be a straight angle or nothing at all — so by Theorem 1 there is exactly one circle through them. Draw it. Now DDD is either outside that circle, or inside it, or on it. Rule out the first two and only the third is left.

Suppose DDD lies outside. Then the segment ADADAD crosses the circle at some point EEE. Now CCC and EEE are both on the circle and on the same side of ABABAB, so ∠ACB=∠AEB\angle ACB = \angle AEB∠ACB=∠AEB. But ∠AEB\angle AEB∠AEB is an exterior angle of △BED\triangle BED△BED, and an exterior angle is larger than either interior angle opposite it, so ∠AEB>∠ADB\angle AEB > \angle ADB∠AEB>∠ADB. Putting the two together, ∠ACB>∠ADB\angle ACB > \angle ADB∠ACB>∠ADB — and we were given that those two angles are equal. So ∠ACB\angle ACB∠ACB would be greater than itself.

Suppose instead DDD lies inside. Extend ADADAD until it meets the circle at EEE. This time ∠ADB\angle ADB∠ADB is the exterior angle of △BED\triangle BED△BED, so ∠ADB>∠AEB=∠ACB\angle ADB > \angle AEB = \angle ACB∠ADB>∠AEB=∠ACB, and the same impossibility arrives from the other direction.

Both positions are ruled out, so DDD lies on the circle through AAA, BBB and CCC. The four points are concyclic.

A quadrilateral whose four vertices are concyclic is called a cyclic quadrilateral. Theorem 10 tells us when four points form one; the next result tells us what we get in return.

Theorem 11

The two opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add up to 180∘180^\circ180∘.

Let ABCDABCDABCD be cyclic, with centre OOO, and consider the two opposite angles at AAA and at CCC. Each is an angle subtended by an arc at a point of the circle outside it, so Theorem 9 applies to both — and the trick is to be careful about which arc.

∠BAD\angle BAD∠BAD is subtended at AAA by the arc from BBB to DDD that passes through CCC. So it is half the angle that arc sweeps at OOO.

∠BCD\angle BCD∠BCD is subtended at CCC by the arc from BBB to DDD that passes through AAA. So it is half the angle that arc sweeps at OOO.

Between them the two arcs make up the whole circle, so the two angles they sweep at OOO add to a complete turn of 360∘360^\circ360∘. Halving,

∠BAD+∠BCD=12×360∘=180∘\angle BAD + \angle BCD = \tfrac{1}{2}\times 360^\circ = 180^\circ∠BAD+∠BCD=21​×360∘=180∘

The same holds for the other pair, ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC and ∠ADC\angle ADC∠ADC, by the identical argument on the arcs through DDD and through BBB. And the converse is true as well.

Example 12

A cyclic quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD has ∠A=95∘\angle A = 95^\circ∠A=95∘ and ∠B=70∘\angle B = 70^\circ∠B=70∘. Find its other two angles.

Theorem 11 pairs each angle with the one opposite it, never with a neighbour. So AAA goes with CCC, and BBB with DDD.

A with C ∠C=180∘−95∘\angle C = 180^\circ - 95^\circ∠C=180∘−95∘ 85∘85^\circ85∘
B with D ∠D=180∘−70∘\angle D = 180^\circ - 70^\circ∠D=180∘−70∘ 110∘110^\circ110∘

Check the total: 95+70+85+110=36095 + 70 + 85 + 110 = 36095+70+85+110=360, which the angles of any quadrilateral must come to. That check is worth doing every time, because it catches the commonest slip here — pairing angles that sit next to each other instead of across from each other.

Theorem 12

If two opposite angles of a quadrilateral add up to 180∘180^\circ180∘, then its four vertices are concyclic.

The proof runs like Theorem 10's — by showing the fourth vertex has nowhere else to go. Suppose ∠BAD+∠BCD=180∘\angle BAD + \angle BCD = 180^\circ∠BAD+∠BCD=180∘. Since AAA, BBB, DDD are not collinear there is a circle through them; suppose CCC lay outside it, and let EEE be where DCDCDC meets the circle. Then ABEDABEDABED is cyclic, so Theorem 11 gives ∠BAD+∠BED=180∘\angle BAD + \angle BED = 180^\circ∠BAD+∠BED=180∘. Comparing with what we were given, ∠BED=∠BCD\angle BED = \angle BCD∠BED=∠BCD. But ∠BED\angle BED∠BED is an exterior angle of △BEC\triangle BEC△BEC and so exceeds ∠BCE\angle BCE∠BCE, which is ∠BCD\angle BCD∠BCD. Once again a quantity is greater than itself. The case with CCC inside is the same argument with the roles exchanged, and is worth writing out.

Closing an old debt

Twice now this chapter has promised that the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle sits at the midpoint of the hypotenuse. Theorem 10 pays up.

Let △ABD\triangle ABD△ABD have its right angle at DDD, and let MMM be the midpoint of the hypotenuse ABABAB. Draw the circle with centre MMM and radius MAMAMA, so that ABABAB is a diameter of it, and mark any point D′D'D′ of that circle on the same side of ABABAB as DDD. By the corollary in Section 5.8, ∠AD′B=90∘\angle AD'B = 90^\circ∠AD′B=90∘ — which is also ∠ADB\angle ADB∠ADB. Two points, equal angles, same side: by Theorem 10 the four points AAA, BBB, D′D'D′ and DDD are concyclic, so DDD is on that circle too. Its centre MMM is therefore equidistant from AAA, BBB and DDD — which is exactly what it means to be the circumcentre.

Example 13

A quadrilateral has angles 85∘85^\circ85∘, 100∘100^\circ100∘, 95∘95^\circ95∘ and 80∘80^\circ80∘, taken in order round the shape. Can a circle be drawn through all four vertices?

Theorem 12 is the test, and it is a test on opposite pairs — so the angles have to be paired first and second with third and fourth, not as they are listed.

first and third 85∘+95∘85^\circ + 95^\circ85∘+95∘ 180∘180^\circ180∘
second and fourth 100∘+80∘100^\circ + 80^\circ100∘+80∘ 180∘180^\circ180∘

Both pairs come to 180∘180^\circ180∘, so by Theorem 12 the four vertices are concyclic and the circle can be drawn. In fact only one pair ever needs checking: all four angles total 360∘360^\circ360∘, so if one opposite pair makes 180∘180^\circ180∘ the other pair has no choice but to do the same.

The starred questions are harder. Most can be settled by picking the right theorem rather than by calculating — and deciding which one is part of the work.

One chapter, twelve theorems, and a single sentence underneath all of them: a circle is the set of points at a fixed distance from a fixed point. Nothing else was ever assumed. Every symmetry, every chord, every angle in this chapter was squeezed out of that one sentence — which is what a good definition is for.

What this chapter established
  1. A circle is the locus of points of a plane lying at a fixed distance, the radius, from a fixed point, the centre.
  2. A circle has complete rotational symmetry about its centre, and reflection symmetry in every diameter — and in no other line.
  3. Infinitely many circles pass through two given points, with their centres strung along the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them. Through three points that are not collinear there passes exactly one.
  4. The segment from the centre to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to it, and the perpendicular from the centre bisects it. Of the three facts — from the centre, through the midpoint, at a right angle — any two force the third.
  5. Equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre and lie at equal distances from it, and each of those statements holds in reverse. A chord at distance ddd has length 2r2−d22\sqrt{r^{2}-d^{2}}2r2−d2​, which settles all of it at once, including that the longer of two chords is the nearer.
  6. An arc subtends at the centre twice the angle it subtends at any point of the circle outside it. So every point of the far arc sees a chord under the same angle, and a diameter is seen at a right angle from everywhere on the circle.
  7. Four points are concyclic exactly when a segment joining two of them subtends equal angles at the other two on the same side — equivalently, when opposite angles of the quadrilateral they form add to 180∘180^\circ180∘.
  8. Measuring confirms; only an argument explains. More than once in this chapter the measurements were right and still proved nothing at all.
The results, in one place
  1. There is exactly one circle through three non-collinear points.
  2. Equal chords subtend equal angles at the centre.
  3. Chords subtending equal angles at the centre are equal.
  4. The centre joined to the midpoint of a chord is perpendicular to it.
  5. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it.
  6. Chords of equal length are equidistant from the centre.
  7. Chords equidistant from the centre are of equal length.
  8. Of two unequal chords, the longer lies nearer the centre.
  9. An arc subtends at the centre twice the angle it subtends at a point of the circle outside the arc.
  10. If a segment subtends equal angles at two points on the same side of it, the four points are concyclic.
  11. Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral add to 180∘180^\circ180∘.
  12. If opposite angles of a quadrilateral add to 180∘180^\circ180∘, it is cyclic.

Next year the circle gives up more: tangents, the lengths cut off when two chords cross, and circles that touch one another. All of it comes from the same one sentence.
