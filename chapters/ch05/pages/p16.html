CM is common to both.

So \triangle CMA \cong \triangle CMB, by SSS.

Congruent triangles give \angle CMA = \angle CMB. But those two angles sit side by side on the straight line AB, so they add to 180^\circ. Two equal angles adding to 180^\circ are 90^\circ each. So CM is perpendicular to AB.

Now the converse. Drop a perpendicular from the centre onto a chord instead, and ask where it lands.

Theorem 5

The perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord.

CA = CB, both radii — and in the right-angled triangles CMA and CMB these are the hypotenuses.

CM is common, and \angle CMA = \angle CMB = 90^\circ is now what is given.

So \triangle CMA \cong \triangle CMB, by RHS, and therefore AM = BM.

Three things have been in play: that the segment runs from the centre, that it meets the chord at a right angle, and that it hits the midpoint. Theorem 4 takes the first and third and delivers the second; Theorem 5 takes the first and second and delivers the third. Any two of the three force the remaining one — and the last pairing is the first question below.

Example 6

A chord of length 16 cm is drawn in a circle of radius 10 cm. How far is the chord from the centre?

Distance from the centre means the perpendicular distance, and Theorem 5 says that perpendicular lands on the midpoint of the chord. That is what makes the calculation possible: it produces a right-angled triangle with a radius as its hypotenuse and half the chord as one leg.

half the chord \tfrac{1}{2}\times 16 8 cm
hypotenuse a radius of the circle 10 cm
