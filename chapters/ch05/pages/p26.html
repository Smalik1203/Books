A point on the major arc sees the chord at 55^\circ; a point on the minor arc sees it at 125^\circ. Notice that the two add to 180^\circ. That is not a coincidence of these numbers — the two central angles always make a full turn, so their halves always make a straight angle. Section 5.9 turns the observation into a theorem.

One case of Theorem 9 is used so often that it has a name of its own. A corollary is a fact that drops out of a result already proved, with no fresh argument needed.

Corollary

The angle subtended by a diameter at any point of the circle is a right angle.

Let AB be a diameter and D any other point of the circle. The arc from A to B that avoids D is a semicircle, and the angle it sweeps at the centre is a straight angle, 180^\circ. By Theorem 9 the angle at D is half of that: \angle ADB = 90^\circ, wherever on the circle D happens to be.

Section 5.4 left a debt here. It claimed that the circumcentre of a right-angled triangle lands on the midpoint of the hypotenuse, and gave no reason. The corollary is half of the reason. The other half — that a point seeing AB under a right angle has no choice but to lie on the circle with AB as diameter — is exactly the sort of statement Section 5.9 is about, and you will be able to finish the argument there.

Example 11

AB is a diameter of a circle and C is a point on the circle, with \angle ABC = 35^\circ. Find \angle BAC.

Nothing is said about the angle at C — and nothing needs to be, because AB is a diameter, and the corollary fixes that angle wherever on the circle C happens to sit.
