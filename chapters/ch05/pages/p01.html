5.1Introduction

In earlier grades you worked with triangles and quadrilaterals — figures built from straight edges, where almost any claim could be settled by measuring the sides and adding up the angles.

This chapter takes the next step, into the circle. It has no straight edges, no corners, and no pieces to count. Yet nearly everything you are about to prove about it follows from one sentence saying what a circle is. Before that sentence, something to explain.

People have been drawing circles for as long as they have been drawing anything. Among the rock paintings at Gudāhāndi in Odisha, beside the triangles and the squares, there are circles and ovals. The shape was there to be copied: a raindrop spreads across still water as a circle, the cut face of a stem is a circle, and so, to the eye, are the full moon and the sun.

Example 1

Jamuna cuts a disc out of paper. Nothing on it marks the centre.

She folds the disc so that the boundary falls exactly onto itself, and presses the crease flat. She opens it out, folds it again a different way, and presses again.

The two creases cross at a single point — and that point is the centre.

Cut a fresh disc, carelessly, and it happens again. Fold a third time and the third crease runs through the same point. Folding can go on confirming this for as long as you have paper, but it can never tell you why the creases meet at all, nor why they meet at the centre rather than somewhere near it. For that you need to say what a circle is — and by the end of Section 5.3 you will settle it in three lines.

A definition is where a proof starts: say exactly what a shape is, and its properties stop being coincidences.
