There is a second route. Triangles CAB and CFG are congruent by SSS — two pairs of radii and the given equal chords — and CE and CH are corresponding altitudes of congruent triangles. Either argument will do; the first has the advantage of never leaving the right-angled halves.

Turn it round and the converse follows from the same two triangles, with the given fact and the wanted one exchanged once more.

Theorem 7

Chords of a circle that lie at the same distance from the centre are equal in length.

CE = CH is now given, and CA = CF = r.

\angle CEA = \angle CHF = 90^\circ, so RHS gives \triangle CEA \cong \triangle CHF again.

Hence AE = FH, and doubling gives AB = FG.

Both theorems come out of one relation, which is worth having on its own. Look again at the right-angled triangle CEA in Fig. 5.8. Its hypotenuse is the radius r, one leg is the distance d = CE, and the other leg is half the chord. The Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem gives

r^{2} = d^{2} + \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\,AB\right)^{2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad AB = 2\sqrt{\,r^{2} - d^{2}\,}

Read that formula and both theorems fall out of it at once. In a given circle r is fixed, so the length of a chord depends on d and on nothing else whatever. Equal distances therefore force equal lengths, and equal lengths force equal distances. Theorems 6 and 7 are the two directions of a single statement.

Example 7

A chord lies 8 cm from the centre of a circle of radius 17 cm. How long is it?

This is the relation just proved, used in the direction it was built for. Put r = 17 and d = 8 into it and there is nothing left to decide.
