Theorem 10

If a segment AB subtends equal angles at two points C and D lying on the same side of it, then A, B, C and D are concyclic.

The proof takes a shape not used anywhere else in this chapter: rather than building the circle, we show that D has nowhere else to be.

The points A, B, C are not collinear — if they were, \angle ACB would be a straight angle or nothing at all — so by Theorem 1 there is exactly one circle through them. Draw it. Now D is either outside that circle, or inside it, or on it. Rule out the first two and only the third is left.

Suppose D lies outside. Then the segment AD crosses the circle at some point E. Now C and E are both on the circle and on the same side of AB, so \angle ACB = \angle AEB. But \angle AEB is an exterior angle of \triangle BED, and an exterior angle is larger than either interior angle opposite it, so \angle AEB > \angle ADB. Putting the two together, \angle ACB > \angle ADB — and we were given that those two angles are equal. So \angle ACB would be greater than itself.

Suppose instead D lies inside. Extend AD until it meets the circle at E. This time \angle ADB is the exterior angle of \triangle BED, so \angle ADB > \angle AEB = \angle ACB, and the same impossibility arrives from the other direction.
