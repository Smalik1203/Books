Let \triangle ABD have its right angle at D, and let M be the midpoint of the hypotenuse AB. Draw the circle with centre M and radius MA, so that AB is a diameter of it, and mark any point D' of that circle on the same side of AB as D. By the corollary in Section 5.8, \angle AD'B = 90^\circ — which is also \angle ADB. Two points, equal angles, same side: by Theorem 10 the four points A, B, D' and D are concyclic, so D is on that circle too. Its centre M is therefore equidistant from A, B and D — which is exactly what it means to be the circumcentre.

Example 13

A quadrilateral has angles 85^\circ, 100^\circ, 95^\circ and 80^\circ, taken in order round the shape. Can a circle be drawn through all four vertices?

Theorem 12 is the test, and it is a test on opposite pairs — so the angles have to be paired first and second with third and fourth, not as they are listed.

first and third 85^\circ + 95^\circ 180^\circ
second and fourth 100^\circ + 80^\circ 180^\circ

Both pairs come to 180^\circ, so by Theorem 12 the four vertices are concyclic and the circle can be drawn. In fact only one pair ever needs checking: all four angles total 360^\circ, so if one opposite pair makes 180^\circ the other pair has no choice but to do the same.
