Notice how little separates the two proofs: the same figure, the same two triangles, the same four radii. All that changes is which fact is given and which is wanted — and with it, the congruence rule that does the work.

Example 5

A chord of a circle of radius 9 cm subtends 60^\circ at the centre. How long is the chord? And if a second chord of the same circle is also 9 cm long, what angle does it subtend?

Join the ends of the chord to the centre. The two radii and the chord make a triangle, and here it is better than isosceles.

two sides both are radii, so 9 cm each isosceles
apex angle 60^\circ, at the centre equilateral

The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal, and if the apex takes 60^\circ the remaining 120^\circ splits into 60^\circ each. All three angles equal makes all three sides equal, so the chord is 9 cm — exactly the radius.

The second part needs no calculation. The other chord is the same length, so by Theorem 2 it subtends the same angle.

Exercise Set 5.3
  1. Show that the triangle formed by a chord of a circle and the two radii to its ends is isosceles.
  2. Two such triangles, in the same circle, have bases of equal length. Show that they are congruent.
  3. Two chords of a circle of radius 10 cm each subtend 60^\circ at the centre. How long is each chord? (No calculation should be needed.)
  4. A chord of a circle of radius 8 cm subtends a right angle at the centre. Find its length.
  5. One chord subtends 60^\circ at the centre and another subtends 120^\circ. Is the second chord twice as long as the first? Give a reason.
