In \triangle BCD: CB = CD, so \angle CBD = \angle CDB.

\angle BCE is the exterior angle of \triangle BCD at C, so \angle BCE = \angle CBD + \angle CDB = 2\,\angle BDC.

In \triangle ACD: CA = CD, so \angle CAD = \angle CDA, and in the same way \angle ACE = 2\,\angle ADC.

Adding the two results, \angle BCA = \angle BCE + \angle ECA = 2\,\angle BDC + 2\,\angle ADC = 2\,\angle BDA, which is the theorem.

One step there was taken without comment: that the line DC, carried on, lands on the arc AFB — which is what put E between A and B and let the two angles at C be added. For some positions of D it lands outside the arc instead, and the figure looks different.

Nothing else changes. The same two isosceles triangles appear, and the same exterior-angle step applies to each; only now the angles at C must be subtracted rather than added, and so must the angles at D. That gives \angle ACB = \angle ACE - \angle BCE = 2\,\angle ADC - 2\,\angle BDC = 2\,\angle ADB, and the theorem stands in that case too. It is worth drawing.
